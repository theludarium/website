# The Ludarium Newsletter: Welcome Sequence

## Purpose

A new subscriber has given us one thing: permission to show up in their inbox. This sequence earns the second thing, which is the habit of opening. Five emails over two weeks, each one short, each one useful on its own, each one pointing to a single article worth the click.

The goal, in order:

1. Turn a subscriber into a reader who opens the weekly issue.
2. Turn a reader into someone who plays in person more often than they did before.
3. Where it fits, turn that reader into a founding member of the Indianapolis lounge, or into a lead for the corporate events service.

Nothing in these five emails sells hard. Each email has one soft ask, and the ask is usually "read this" or "try this." The events ask in email 4 is the only one that asks for a reply.

## Email 1: Welcome (Day 0)

**Subject:** The game is the reason. The people are the point.
**A/B alternate:** You're in. Here's the one article to read first.

**Preview text:** A short note on what this newsletter is for, and one word you will see a lot.

**Body:**

Welcome to The Ludarium. Thank you for signing up.

Here is what this is. Once a week, you get one email about playing games with other people, in the same room. Sometimes it is about which game to bring. Sometimes it is about how to host, how to teach, or how to keep a group together for years. Sometimes it is about why any of this matters for your health.

That last part is where the word "togethering" comes in. It is a noun. It means people coming together, usually in person, to share life. The game is the medium. It gives you a reason to show up, something to do with your hands, and a structure for the next two hours. What you walk away with is the people.

We think most adults are short on togethering and do not have a good word for the gap. A board game is one of the cheapest, lowest-pressure ways to close it. A copy of Codenames costs less than a round of drinks and works for eight people who have never met.

If you read one article from us, read this one. It explains the idea behind everything else we write, and why we treat it as a longevity practice rather than a hobby.

Read: Togethering: Why It Is a Longevity Practice
/articles/togethering

One small ask. Add this address to your contacts so the weekly issue lands where you can see it. That is all for today.

Never stop playing.

## Email 2: The health case (Day 2)

**Subject:** Why your doctor should ask if you play games
**A/B alternate:** The cheapest health habit nobody prescribes

**Preview text:** What the research says about play, connection, and living longer. With sources.

**Body:**

Yesterday's note was the idea. Today is the evidence.

The [Harvard Study of Adult Development](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2017/04/over-nearly-80-years-harvard-study-has-been-showing-how-to-live-a-healthy-and-happy-life/) has followed the same families since 1938. Its directors keep landing on the same finding: the quality of a person's relationships in midlife is a stronger predictor of health in old age than cholesterol levels. Not money. Not fame. Relationships.

The U.S. Surgeon General's [2023 advisory on loneliness](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf) put a number on the other side of that. Lacking social connection carries a mortality risk the advisory compared to smoking up to 15 cigarettes a day. About one in two American adults reported measurable loneliness even before the pandemic.

Here is the part that matters for this newsletter. Relationships do not maintain themselves. They need repeated, low-stakes contact. A standing game of Wingspan every other Thursday is exactly that. You do not have to plan a dinner party. You do not have to come up with conversation. The game carries the evening, and the friendship accrues on the side.

There is more. Play itself, separate from the company, appears to do something for adult stress and attention. A hard puzzle like Azul or a shared crisis in Pandemic pulls your focus onto something bounded and solvable for an hour. Most people stand up from the table lighter than they sat down.

The article below walks through the research in plain language, with the hedges where they belong.

Read: Benefits of Play for Adults: The Case for Playing
/articles/benefits-of-play-for-adults

The ask today is small. Think of one person you have not sat across a table from in six months. Hold that name until Friday's email.

## Email 3: Start a standing game night (Day 5)

**Subject:** The one rule that keeps a game night alive
**A/B alternate:** How to start a game night that survives month three

**Preview text:** Most game nights die by the third month. Here is the fix, in one evening of setup.

**Body:**

On Wednesday we asked you to think of one name. Today we give you something to do with it.

Most game nights fail the same way. The first one is great. The second one is fine. The third one gets rescheduled, and nobody sends the fourth invite. The problem is not interest. The problem is that each session requires a fresh decision, and fresh decisions lose to tired weeknights.

The fix is a standing time. Second Tuesday, 7 p.m., same place, whether four people show or two. No poll. No group text asking who is free. The night exists, and people fit themselves to it. Groups that do this last for years. Groups that reschedule every time last for weeks.

A few other things that help:

- Keep the core group small. Three reliable people beat eight maybes.
- Own the opener. Have one game you can teach in five minutes, like The Crew or Codenames, so the night starts the moment the second person arrives.
- End on time. A night that ends at 10 gets a yes next month. A night that ends at 1 a.m. gets a maybe.
- Make it phone-free for the first game. A basket by the door does the work.

The full article covers how to pick the night, how to handle the person who never comes, and what to do when the host gets tired.

Read: How to Start a Board Game Group That Lasts
/articles/how-to-start-a-game-night-group

The ask: send one text today. "I'm starting a game night, second Tuesday of the month. Want in?" Use the name from Wednesday. That is the whole job.

## Email 4: What we are building (Day 9)

**Subject:** Coworking, but for playing games together
**A/B alternate:** What we are building in Indianapolis

**Preview text:** The lounge, the membership model, and a service for teams. Plus a question we would like you to answer.

**Body:**

You have had four emails from us. You should know what we are up to.

The Ludarium is a game lounge, starting in Indianapolis. Think of it as coworking, but for playing tabletop games together. Members get a good table, good light, a curated library of around 300 games instead of a dusty wall of 3,000, and a room full of people who came to play. Pay once a month instead of a cover charge at the door, so the fifth visit costs nothing extra and the habit forms.

Why a lounge? Because the hardest part of togethering is the place. Sociologists call it the third place: not home, not work, somewhere you can show up without an invitation and belong. Coffee shops close at 8. Bars are loud. Most game stores are built for buying, not staying. The article below is about that gap and why we think it is worth filling.

Read: The Third Place Problem: Where Adults Go to Belong
/articles/third-place-problem

There is a second thing we do now, before the lounge opens. We run tabletop events for companies. Not trust falls. Forty-five minutes of Pandemic shows you who leads under pressure better than a personality test. A round of Codenames shows you how a team actually communicates. We design the session around what the manager wants to learn, bring the games, and run the table.

Here is the ask. Reply to this email and tell us one of two things:

1. If you are in or near Indianapolis: what would make you walk into a game lounge on a Tuesday night?
2. If you run or sit on a team: when is your next offsite, and what has not worked before?

A real person reads every reply. We answer all of them.

## Email 5: One question (Day 14)

**Subject:** What game got you into this?
**A/B alternate:** One question, then we get out of your inbox until Thursday

**Preview text:** Hit reply with one title. Then here is what the weekly issue looks like from now on.

**Body:**

Two weeks in. One question.

What game got you into this? Not the best game you own. The first one that made you think, "I want to do that again with these people."

For a lot of readers it is Catan, borrowed at a friend's apartment in college. For some it is a parent's deck of cards. For some it is Ticket to Ride at a holiday when the TV was off. For the founder it was Magic: The Gathering in a basement, and the group that formed around one Cube is still drafting together today.

Reply with one title and, if you want, one sentence about the table. We read these, and the best ones end up in the weekly issue with your first name, if you give permission.

If you are still looking for your game, or for one to hand a friend who thinks they do not like board games, this article is the list we send most often.
