# Who It's For

Every game recommendation hides an assumption about who is at the table. Four adults with a free evening is the default, and most lists are written for them. This category is for everyone else: the couple with one hour, the senior who lives alone, the youth group of thirty, the new parents with twenty minutes and no energy left.

Each guide starts with the audience and works backward. What do these people actually have? How much time, how much table, how many hands, how much patience for rules? Then it picks games that fit those limits and, more importantly, says how to get the game to happen. For seniors living alone, the hard part is not the game. It is getting someone to a table and back again next week. For long-distance friends, it is tools and a calendar. For people who say they don't like games, it is figuring out which of three real complaints they have.

Some of these guides are about a life stage. New parents, college students, couples. Some are about a group that already exists and needs a reason to gather: church groups, neighbors, a friend group scattered across four states. And two are about playing when the table is small or missing. The solo games piece argues that playing alone can feed a social life rather than replace one. The social anxiety piece explains why a board game is one of the easiest ways into a room for people who find rooms hard.

If you are a host, these guides tell you what to put on the table for a specific guest. If you are the guest, they tell you what to ask for.

We think of this category as the middle of the site. The lists in What to Play tell you what is good. The pieces here tell you what is good for the people you actually have. That is usually the question.
