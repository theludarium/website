# Family and Intergenerational Play

Family board games have a job that no other game has: they need to work for a six-year-old and a sixty-year-old at the same table, on the same night, without anyone pretending. Most games fail that test. The ones that pass are worth knowing by name, and this category names them.

The core question parents ask is which games to buy. The real question underneath is how to make play a habit in a house where screens are easier. So the pages here cover both. There are picks by age, from five-year-olds up through teenagers and grandparents, with play times and what to skip. And there are the pieces about the rest of it: the kid who melts down when he loses, the siblings who cannot share a team, the family game night that keeps dissolving into a rules argument.

A few things we believe. Losing is a skill, and the table is the cheapest place to teach it. Cooperative games are the best tool for siblings who fight, because the game gives them a shared enemy that is not each other. Grandparents who say they do not play games usually mean they do not want to learn forty pages of rules, and there are ten games that get around that. Teenagers will play with you if the game gives everyone something to do besides talk.

If you are starting from Monopoly fatigue, begin with the family picks list. If you have a young child, the five-year-old piece has ten games an adult can play without faking enthusiasm. If your game nights end in tears, read the two pieces on losing before you buy anything new.

There is also a Thanksgiving guide for the three-generation table, a piece on what board games teach that school does not, and a Screen-Free Saturday plan for turning one good game night into a standing habit.

The ritual matters more than the game. A family that plays on the same night each week has a place where the teenager and the grandparent talk to each other, and that is rare enough to protect.
