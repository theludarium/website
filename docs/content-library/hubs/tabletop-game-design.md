# Tabletop Game Design

Tabletop game design is the study of why a game feels the way it does, and how to make one that feels right. It is useful to designers, obviously. It is also useful to anyone who hosts, because knowing why a game works tells you which game to put in front of which people, and how to teach it in five minutes instead of twenty.

This category answers two questions. For players: what is going on under the hood of the games I love? For designers: how do I get from an idea to a prototype, a playtest, a rulebook, and maybe a publisher, without lying to myself along the way?

The first batch of ten covers the mechanics and patterns that explain most of the modern hobby. Worker placement, through one game. Catch-up mechanics that keep an ending in doubt without punishing the leader. Luck versus skill, and why the games that last use luck to set the problem and skill to solve it. Social deduction and the psychology of a good lie. The quarterback problem in co-ops. Why the play time on the box is wrong, and what table presence does for a game from across the room. It closes with the two most practical pieces we have: how to teach any game in five minutes, and how to build a playable prototype in one weekend with index cards and a Sharpie.

The second batch goes further into the craft. Deck building from Dominion onward. Area control that stays tense without cruelty. Roll-and-write, which looks easy and is not. Then the parts of design that are not about mechanics at all: writing a rulebook people will read, balancing a card game with math you can do, designing for the player count your group really has, deciding whether theme or mechanics comes first, testing through print-and-play, and the sober look at Kickstarter and the alternative of pitching a publisher.

If you host, start with the five-minute teach and the box-lies piece. If you design, start with the weekend prototype, then the playtesting piece, then the rulebook. If you just want to understand why Catan still works, read luck versus skill.

Every game is a machine for producing a particular kind of evening. This is where we take the machines apart.
