# Ludus Latrunculorum: research brief for a beginner course

Prepared 2026-11-26. Every claim carries a source. Labels used:
- **[Primary]** ancient text, checked against the Latin where possible (mostly The Latin Library or LacusCurtius).
- **[Scholarly]** peer-reviewed or academic work.
- **[Museum]** museum or heritage body.
- **[Hobby]** hobby site, wiki or forum. Use with care. Do not let these carry a claim alone.
- **[Unverified]** I could not confirm it from a source I read. Check before you print it.

## 1. Ancient evidence

### 1a. Roman literary sources (in rough date order)

**Sources sometimes cited that do NOT describe latrunculi:**
- **Quintilian 11.2.38**: Scaevola replays a game from memory, but the game named is *duodecim scripta* [Primary: thelatinlibrary.com/quintilian/quintilian.institutio11.shtml]. I found no latrunculi mention in Quintilian.
- **Petronius, *Satyricon* 33**: Trimalchio's terebinth board, crystal dice, and gold and silver coins used instead of white and black counters. Dice point to a race game, not latrunculi [Primary: thelatinlibrary.com/petronius1.html].
- **Ovid *Ars* 3.363-366** and ***Tristia* 2.481-482** describe other games (twelve lines; a little board with three pieces each).

**"Ad incitas"**: a proverb, "reduced to the immovable ones," meaning brought to a standstill. Plautus *Poenulus* 4.2.85 and *Trinummus* 2.4.136 (old act.scene.line numbering; modern line numbers not confirmed), plus Lucilius [Scholarly: Lewis & Short, *incita*, alatius.com/ls/index.php?l=incita]. Tilley (1892) links it to Isidore's *inciti* [Scholarly: Tilley, "Ludus Latrunculorum," *Classical Review* 6.8 (1892) 335-336, penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Journals/CR/6/8/Ludus_Latrunculorum*.html]. Whether it comes from latrunculi specifically is not proven.

**Terms checklist**
- *latrunculi / latrones* ("little robbers," "robbers," also "mercenaries"): attested (Varro, Seneca, Martial, Ovid's *latronum*).
- *calculi* (counters, pebbles): attested (Ovid, Seneca, Martial).
- *miles* (soldier): attested (Ovid, Martial, *Laus Pisonis* 193). *Bellator* (warrior): Ovid *Ars* 3.359. *Milites/bellatores* as formal piece names is a modern tidying.
- *mandra / mandrae*: attested (Martial 7.72; *Laus Pisonis* 203). Meaning unknown: "pen" or barrier; suggested as a line of pieces, a blockade or a camp wall (Thayer's Loeb note 27; Tilley 1892). Smith's old dictionary said "squares" [Hobby-grade old reference: penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA*/Latrunculi.html]. Do not teach a single meaning as fact.
- *ordinarii / vagi / inciti*: Isidore only, and disputed (above).
- *alligatus* (bound): Seneca 117.30; *alliget* in *Laus Pisonis* 194; *ligato ... obligat* 201-202.
- *tabula latruncularia*: Seneca 117.30.
- ***dux***: **no ancient source names a dux piece.** The nearest word is *te duce* in *Laus Pisonis* 195, which means "with you (Piso) as leader," not a piece. The dux was introduced by R. C. Bell (1960) [Scholarly: Wikipedia summary of Bell; see section 3].

### 1b. The *Laus Pisonis* passage (190-208)
Latin from LacusCurtius [Primary: penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Laus_Pisonis/text*.html]; Loeb translation (1934, rev. 1935) on the English page [penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Laus_Pisonis/text*.html].
- 190-191: when tired of study, you play games of skill.
- 192-193: on the open board, pieces are set out in a more cunning way; wars are fought with glass soldiers (*tabula variatur aperta / calculus*; *vitreo peraguntur milite bella*). Locus Ludi and Crist et al. read "open board" as an empty board at the start, filled by placement.
- 194: so that white binds black and black binds white (*alliget*).
- 195-196: which piece did not yield to you as leader? which, about to die, did not take an enemy with it?
- 197-200: your line fights in a thousand ways: one piece flees and then seizes its pursuer; one comes up from far back where it stood on watch; one dares to join the fight and tricks the enemy coming for prey.
- 201-202: one accepts risky delays and, looking bound itself, binds two.
- 202-204: one moves toward bigger things, bursting through the broken *mandra* into the enemy columns and plundering walls after the rampart falls.
- 205-208: however fierce the battle, with your phalanx full or only lightly stripped, you win, and both hands rattle with captured pieces.
Scholarship: Richmond, "The Ludus Latrunculorum and *Laus Pisonis* 190-208," *Museum Helveticum* 51 (1994) 164-179 [Scholarly; I could not read the full text]. Dating and authorship: Wikipedia, *Laus Pisonis* [Hobby-grade summary]; Thayer's Loeb introduction.

### 1c. The Greek background: *petteia* and *polis*
- **Plato, *Republic* 487b-c**: Adeimantus says Socrates' opponents end up like unskilled *pessoi* players, shut in by experts until they "have no move to make" [Scholarly: Bakewell, "Plato Plays Polis," *Board Game Studies Journal* 16.1 (2022) 413-430, doi:10.2478/bgs-2022-0014, with Greek text and translation].
- **Plato, *Republic* 422e-423a**: a pun on the game *polis* ("city") [same].
- **Pollux, *Onomasticon* 9.98**: *polis* was played with two sets of counters on a board of spaces formed by lines; the board was called the "city" and each piece a "dog" (*kyon*); the aim was to surround and take enemy pieces. Number of spaces and pieces unresolved [Scholarly: Bakewell 2022, citing Costanza 2019]. Wikipedia's "30 pieces each" is disputed.
- Whether latrunculi descends from *polis* is plausible but unproven. Locus Ludi calls it "similar, if not identical" [Scholarly: locusludi.ch/play-ancient-online-games/].

## 2. Archaeology

**The best survey: Crist, Piette, Soemers, Stephenson and Browne, "Computational approaches for recognising and reconstructing ancient games: the case of Ludus Latrunculorum," in *Games in the Ancient World: Places, Spaces, Accessories* (Éditions Mergoil, 2024) 63-80** [Scholarly: cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl, PDF read in full]. Key points:
- **25 known intact quadrangular boards** from Roman sites, ranging from **6x6 to 11x16**. **Nine are 8x8, the most frequent size.** Several are 6x7, 7x8 and 8x9. Larger sizes are one-offs. Fragments show grids up to 17 squares in a row.
- Common sizes (6x7, 7x8, 8x8) appear across the empire, including Rome, Athens and Asia Minor; **7x8 and 8x8 are the only intact sizes found in Italy.** Larger boards cluster at frontier forts.
- Contexts: forts (Britain, Spain, Egypt: e.g. Chesters, Monte de Santa Tegra, Abu Sha'ar), graffiti in cities (the **Basilica Iulia** in the Roman Forum, the **Parthenon**), leisure spaces (Serapeum at Hadrian's Villa, Antonine Baths at Carthage), rural sites.
- Boards survive in stone or tile; most are secondary carvings or graffiti. Only one (formerly in Zurich) seems made as a board. Wooden boards have not survived.
- Two terracotta models show a 6x7 board: one from Athens with players, one from the Fayum with pieces on it.
- Only one 6x6 board is known, so Varro's declension table should not be read as a board size.

**Britain**
- About 100 boards catalogued; square grids are the most common, typically **7x8, 8x8 or 9x10** [Scholarly: Penn and Courts, "Roman gaming boards from Britain," Roman Finds Group Datasheet 13 (2021); Courts and Penn, "A corpus of gaming boards from Roman Britain," *Lucerna* 57 (2019) 4-12. Seen via summaries only].
- R. C. Bell (1960) gave 7x8, 8x8 and 9x10 as common British sizes [via Wikipedia].
- **Vindolanda**: 16 boards, the largest collection in Roman Britain, found inside the fort (near the commanding officer's house and in barracks) and outside near the baths [Museum: Penn and Courts, "Playing games at Vindolanda," Vindolanda Trust, 29 Oct 2022, vindolanda.com/blog/playing-games-at-vindolanda]. Grid sizes are not given there. A 2019 board was found reused as flooring behind the bath house, 3rd c. AD [Museum/press: archaeology.org/news/2019/05/06/190506-roman-england-vindolanda/]. Wikipedia gives it as 7x9 [Hobby; Unverified].
- **Corbridge / Housesteads**: English Heritage holds 11 fragmentary boards and 689 counters from Hadrian's Wall sites, including a Corbridge stone board with bone and Samian counters and dice [Museum: english-heritage.org.uk/learn/histories/board-games/; historicengland.org.uk educational image 9439]. A "56 squares" (7x8) count for a Corbridge board appeared in a search snippet [Unverified]. Housesteads grid size [Unverified].
- **Mons Claudianus** [Unverified]: I found no source for latrunculi boards there. The verified Egyptian military example is **Abu Sha'ar**, a late Roman Red Sea fort with about twenty boards, some in a gaming room [Scholarly: Mulvin and Sidebotham, "Roman game boards from Abu Sha'ar," *Antiquity* 78 (2004)].
- **Stanway "Doctor's grave"** (Colchester, c. AD 40s-50s): a wooden board (only metal corners and hinges survive), about 385 x 565 mm, with **26 glass counters, 13 white and 13 blue**, found laid out as if mid-game, with surgical tools on top [Scholarly: Crummy, "The gaming board in CF47," in Crummy et al., *Stanway: an Élite Burial Site at Camulodunum*, Britannia Monograph 24 (2007) 352-359]. Schädler's study in the same volume says the counter spacing implies a **9x13 or 8x12** grid, matching no known Roman game, and argues it is a Celtic game related to *fidchell / gwyddbwyll*, not latrunculi [Scholarly: Schädler, "The doctor's game: new light on the history of ancient board games," in Crummy et al. 2007; abstract at academia.edu/2135694]. Crist et al. 2024 agree. **This is the only well-known find with pieces apparently in place, and it is probably not latrunculi.** Kowalski based his 8x12 rules on it.
- **Lullingstone** villa mausoleum (c. AD 320): 30 glass counters on the remains of a board, thought more likely *duodecim scripta* [Museum: english-heritage.org.uk Lullingstone history page].

**Counters**
- Typical Roman counter: small plano-convex glass disc, often black (very dark glass) and white [Museum: Museum Wales collections; Museum of London object 477966, a London burial set of 13 black and 10 white counters with 4 dice].
- Tilley (1892) reports 816 glass counters in three colours from a Perugia tomb.
- Caution: many glass "counters" were decoration, not game pieces; a study of Pompeii finds concludes most were unlikely to be used for gaming [Scholarly: "Recreation or decoration: what were the glass counters from Pompeii used for?" *Papers of the British School at Rome* (2016), cambridge.org]. A counter proves a game only when found with a board or dice.
- Bone and pottery counters are common at forts (Vindolanda; English Heritage).
- Locus Ludi's rules assume counters with a domed top and flat underside, so a trapped piece can be flipped [Scholarly: Locus Ludi rules PDF].

- **Bell discrepancy.** Wikipedia's summary of Bell includes a corner capture and an extra move after a capture. ## 4. Certain vs guessed

**Broadly agreed (direct ancient support)**
1. Two players, two colours of pieces (*discolor*: Ovid *Tristia* 2.477, Martial 14.17; white and black: *Laus Pisonis* 194).
2. Pieces were often glass (Ovid *Ars* 2.208; Martial 7.72; *Laus Pisonis* 193), also gem or stone (Martial 14.18).
3. Pieces were thought of as soldiers or robbers: *miles*, *latro*, *bellator*.
4. **Custodian capture**: a piece between two enemies is lost (Ovid twice, Martial 14.17). The one rule every scholar accepts (CAMWS 2022 abstract, camws.org/sites/default/files/meeting2022/LudusLatrunculorum.pdf).
5. A grid board (Varro), usually 8x8, 7x8 or 6x7 in surviving examples, with no fixed size (Crist et al. 2024).
6. Pieces move in straight lines (Ovid *Tristia* 2.477). Not diagonal is the standard reading.
7. A skill game without dice (no dice in any description; Austin 1934).
8. Pieces could be "bound" and possibly freed (Seneca 117.30; *Laus Pisonis* 194, 201-202).
9. You won by taking pieces; the winner ends with hands full of captives (*Laus Pisonis* 208); counting pieces mattered (Canus, "one ahead").

**Probable, with scholarly support but not proven**
- The board started empty and pieces were placed in turn (*Laus Pisonis* 192-193; Austin, Schädler, Locus Ludi, Crist et al.).
- All pieces were identical (no unique piece in any text; Austin; Schädler; CAMWS 2022).

**Modern guesswork**
- Board size and piece count for "the" game.
- One step versus any distance per move.
- Jumping of any kind.
- Corner captures.
- Whether stepping between two enemies is safe or suicide.
- Multiple captures in one move.
- The two-step "trap then remove" capture and freeing rule (an interpretation of Seneca 117.30).
- **The dux** (Bell's invention) and every rule about it.
- Fixed starting rows.
- The exact win condition (all captured, one left, blockade, most captures) and any draw or move-limit rule.
- Meanings of *mandra*, *ordinarii*, *vagi*, *inciti* as game terms.

## 5. Related games with documented rules

- **Seega** (Egypt; documented by E. W. Lane, *Manners and Customs of the Modern Egyptians*, 1836): 5x5, 7x7 or 9x9; board starts empty; players place two pieces a turn, centre left empty; pieces step one square orthogonally; custodian capture by the moving player only (entering between two is safe); a capturing piece may continue while it keeps capturing; blockade rules vary [Hobby: Wikipedia, Seega (game); scholarly description in Crist et al. 2024].
- **Kharebga** (El Oued, Algeria; Bellin 1964): like Seega but pieces slide any distance; capture all to win [Scholarly: Crist et al. 2024].
- **Hasami shogi** (Japan): 9x9, nine men each on the back rank; rook moves, no jumping; custodian capture; a corner piece is taken by occupying both adjacent squares; a whole line of enemies between two of yours is taken; entering between two enemies is safe; win by taking all but one. Dai hasami shogi: 18 a side, single jumps, five in a row wins [Hobby: Wikipedia, citing Bell 1979 and Murray 1978].
- **Tafl / Tablut**: Linnaeus recorded Tablut in 1732 (9x9; king plus 8 defenders against 16 attackers); rook moves; custodian capture; king taken by four in the castle, three beside it, otherwise two; unequal sides and a king, unlike latrunculi [Scholarly: Crist et al. 2024; Hobby: Wikipedia, Tafl games]. A link from latrunculi to tafl is often asserted; Crist et al. note no medieval source confirms tafl used custodian capture. Hall and Forsyth, "Roman rules? The introduction of board games to Britain and Ireland," *Antiquity* 85 (2011) 1325-1338, discuss Roman influence on northern games.
- ***Polis / petteia***: see 1c.
- **Alquerque**: captures by jumping, not sandwiching. Useful only as a contrast.

## 6. Culture and anecdotes

- **Soldiers**: boards concentrate in forts across Britain, Spain and Egypt (Crist et al. 2024); Vindolanda alone has 16 and the Trust calls it Roman Britain's most popular game (Penn and Courts 2022). The military vocabulary in every text fits.
- **Julius Canus** (Seneca, *Tranq.* 14.4-10, game at 14.7): calm in the face of execution, counting his pieces. The best story for a course opener.
- **Piso** (*Laus Pisonis*): skill at latrunculi praised as a gentleman's mental exercise next to ball games and weapons drill.
- **Ovid**: a social game men and women both played (*Ars* 2.207-208; 3.357-360); manuals on games existed (*Tristia* 2.471-482).
- **Seneca 106.11 and 117.30**: the game as a symbol of clever but trivial effort.
- **Proculus** (HA, *Quadr. Tyr.* 13.2): "imperator" ten times at a banquet game, then hailed Augustus. Possibly fiction. The Vindolanda blog's "winners were called Imperator" rests on this single passage.
- **Macrobius 1.5.11**: still a common pastime in the early 5th century.
- **Name confusion**: medieval Latin used *ludus latrunculorum* for chess, so older translations say "chess" or "draughts." Austin (1934): no connection with chess [via Wikipedia and search snippets].

## 7. Strategy notes (all modern)

I found **no published strategy guide** for any latrunculi reconstruction. Two kinds of material can be used honestly:

**Tactics the ancient poets describe** (primary, but poetic):
- Feigned retreat, then strike the pursuer (*Laus Pisonis* 197-198).
- Bring a reserve from far back (198-199).
- Bait: let the enemy come for prey, then trap it (199-200).
- A piece that looks trapped traps two (201-202).
- Break through the *mandra* into the enemy rear (202-204).
- Keep your phalanx intact; win without heavy losses (206-207).
- Support the piece ahead and know when to pull it back; a lone piece in retreat is in danger (Ovid, *Tristia* 2.479-480).
- A warrior without its partner is vulnerable (Ovid, *Ars* 3.359).

**Logic of custodian capture** (my analysis from the rules. Mark it as modern in the course):
- A corner piece can be taken by two attackers, so corners are weak.
- An edge piece can only be sandwiched along the edge.
- Two friendly pieces side by side in a line cannot be sandwiched along that line, so pairs protect.
- A 2x2 block cannot be captured under single-piece sandwich rules (true in Locus Ludi Piso; not under Hasami-style line captures).
- If entering between two enemies is safe, infiltration is a real tactic, and the moved piece threatens both neighbours.
- During placement, avoid squares where one enemy move completes a sandwich.

Crist et al. (2024) observe a fast capturing phase after placement, then a long tail where finishing off scattered pieces is hard. Classroom players report the same (Tibaldini, cited there). Plan an endgame rule.

## 8. Recommended table rules for the course and print-and-play kit

**Recommendation:** Build on the **Locus Ludi "Piso variant"** (Schädler's ERC project). It is the only ruleset published by a scholarly team that keeps the attested core and avoids the dux. Add one clearly labelled modern endgame rule. Offer slide movement as the "advanced" option, backed by Crist et al. 2024.

**Ludarium table rules (proposed)**
1. **Board**: 8x8 squares. **Pieces**: 16 per side in two colours. Locus Ludi uses 20 and Schädler allows 16-24; 16 plays faster for beginners and fits a chessboard kit. Print counters with a marked face so they can be flipped if you teach the Seneca variant later.
2. **Placement**: the board starts empty. Players take turns placing one piece on any empty square. No captures while placing.
3. **Movement**: one square forward, back or sideways, never diagonally. No jumping. (Advanced option: slide any distance in a straight line, like a rook.)
4. **Capture**: if your move leaves an enemy piece between two of yours in a row or column, remove it. One move can capture more than one piece. A piece in a corner is captured by enemies on its two neighbouring squares.
5. **Safe entry**: you may move a piece between two enemies without being captured.
6. **No shuffling**: a piece may not move back and forth on consecutive turns (from Locus Ludi).
7. **End**: the game ends when a player has one piece left or cannot move. The player who captured more pieces wins. **Modern addition** (adapted from Bell's 30-move rule): if 30 turns in a row pass without a capture, stop; most pieces left wins; equal is a draw.

**What to tell learners is reconstruction:** points 1 (count), 3, 4 (corners, multiple captures), 5, 6 and 7 are modern choices. The empty board and placement (2) are a strong scholarly reading of *Laus Pisonis*. The sandwich capture is ancient.

**Is 8x8 defensible?** Yes, and it is the best-supported single size. Nine of the 25 intact Roman boards are 8x8, the most common size (Crist et al. 2024). 8x8 is one of the two intact sizes found in Italy. It is among the typical British sizes (Penn and Courts 2021; Bell 1960). Locus Ludi chose it for "a certain preference for 8x8." Caveat: I could not confirm named sites for individual 8x8 boards; the site list is in Crist et al. 2024, Figure 2, and Courts and Penn 2019. Say "the most common surviving size," not "the official size." A chessboard-compatible kit is honest on those terms. Offer a 7x8 sheet as an "also authentic" bonus board.

**Avoid in the course**: presenting the dux as Roman, the Stanway board as a latrunculi board, Isidore's terms as certain, Quintilian or Petronius as latrunculi sources, and "the" Roman rules.
