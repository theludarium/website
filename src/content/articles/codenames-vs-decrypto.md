Codenames vs Decrypto looks like a coin flip from the outside. Both are team word games. Both ask one person to give a clue that their teammates will understand and the other team will not. Both come in small boxes, teach fast, and are beloved by people who otherwise dislike party games.

But they produce different rooms. Codenames is a party game that happens to be clever. Decrypto is a deduction game that happens to be played with words. The right one for you depends on whether you want a loose, loud table or a quiet, leaning-in one.

## How does each game work?

**Codenames** <!-- AFFILIATE --> lays out a 5-by-5 grid of 25 word cards. Two teams each have a spymaster who can see a key card showing which words belong to which team, which are neutral, and which single word is the assassin. Each turn, the spymaster gives a one-word clue and a number, such as "ocean, 3", meaning three of the team's words relate to ocean. The team discusses and touches words one at a time; a correct guess lets them continue, a wrong one ends the turn. Touching the assassin loses the game immediately. First team to find all its words wins. Publisher specs list 4 to 8+ players, ages 10 and up, about 15 minutes ([Czech Games Edition](https://czechgames.com/en/codenames/)).

**Decrypto** <!-- AFFILIATE --> gives each team four secret keywords, numbered 1 to 4, hidden behind a screen. Each round, one player on each team draws a code card with three of those digits in some order, say 4-2-1, and gives three clues, one per digit, out loud. Their own team must decode which numbers the clues point to. The other team listens and also writes a guess. If the opposing team cracks your code, they earn an interception token; if your own team fails to decode, you earn a miscommunication token. Two interceptions wins; two miscommunications loses. The tension is that the same four keywords stay all game, so every clue you give leaks information to people who are writing it all down. It plays 3 to 8, ages 12 and up, 15 to 45 minutes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decrypto)).

## Codenames vs Decrypto: the side-by-side

| | Codenames | Decrypto |
|---|---|---|
| Players | 4–8+ (2–3 with variant rules) | 3–8 |
| Time | 15 minutes | 15–45 minutes |
| Ages (box) | 10+ | 12+ |
| Weight (complexity) | Light | Light-medium |
| Teach time | 3 minutes | 7–10 minutes, plus one practice round |
| Who gives clues | One spymaster per team, all game | Rotates every round |
| Core skill | Finding one word that links several | Being clear to friends and vague to strangers at once |
| Downtime | Teams watch each other; low | Everyone works every round; very low |
| Noise level | Loud, laughing, table talk | Quiet, note-taking, occasional groans |
| What it's best at | Parties, mixed company, first-time game nights | Tight groups who want a mental workout |

## Which one is easier to teach?

Codenames. You can explain it in three sentences and point at the grid. Most people have played something like it, and the one rule that needs emphasis is the assassin.

Decrypto needs a little more care. The idea of giving clues that your team can match to numbers but the other team cannot is easy to state and strange to feel until you have done it. Most groups need one throwaway round before the game makes sense. After that it moves quickly, but if you have a table of people who tune out during rules, budget for that first round.

Neither game is hard. The difference is that Codenames can be taught to someone who arrived late and is holding a drink, and Decrypto cannot.

## Which one handles bigger groups?

Codenames scales up without limit because extra players just join a team's discussion. Eight, ten, twelve people can play, which is why it leads our list of [party games for large groups](/articles/party-games-for-large-groups/). The downside is that in a large group, the quieter people stop participating and two or three voices make every guess.

Decrypto is best at four to six, with two or three per team. At eight it still works but the clue-giving rotation takes longer to come around. Below four it uses variant rules and loses some of its spark. If you regularly host [large game nights](/articles/large-group-game-night/), Codenames is the one to own; Decrypto is for the smaller standing group.

## Which one is more interesting to play?

This is where opinions split, and it is the heart of the comparison.

Codenames puts almost all the thinking on the spymaster. They stare at the grid for a long minute searching for a word that connects three of their words and none of the other team's or the assassin. Their teammates get to argue and guess, which is fun, but the clever part belongs to one person per team. If you are not the spymaster, you are mostly reacting.

Decrypto spreads the thinking around. Every round, everyone on both teams is working: the clue-giver is threading a needle, their teammates are decoding, and the opponents are cross-referencing every clue from every round against their notes trying to guess what keyword number 3 could be. Nobody is idle. The payoff when you intercept a code after three rounds of careful notes is a different kind of satisfaction than guessing a word correctly.

Players who love puzzles tend to prefer Decrypto. Players who love people tend to prefer Codenames. Both camps are right about themselves.

## Which one goes wrong more often?

Codenames has two well-known failure modes. The first is the assassin: a spymaster gives a clue that accidentally fits the one deadly word, the team touches it, and the game ends in a second with everyone staring at the spymaster. It is funny in a good group and painful in a sensitive one. The second is the slow spymaster, who takes four minutes per clue and drains the table. [Our Codenames clue guide](/articles/codenames-clue-tips/) covers both.

Decrypto's failure mode is subtler. A team that keeps miscommunicating, because the clue-giver is being too clever or the teammates are tired, loses to itself rather than to the opponents. That can feel bad in a way that losing Codenames rarely does, because the blame has nowhere else to go. It also rewards note-taking, and a player who does not write things down is at a real disadvantage against one who does.

## Which one has more replay value?

Both are close to infinite. Codenames ships with hundreds of double-sided word cards and more in the Pictures, Duet, and other versions. Decrypto's keyword cards are similarly plentiful, and the tension comes from the people, not the cards.

The practical difference is that Codenames gets played constantly because it can be dropped into any evening. Decrypto gets played deliberately, when the right four to six people are together. A box of Codenames gets more plays. A box of Decrypto may get more memorable ones.

## Verdict: which word game should you buy?

**Buy Codenames if:**

- You host parties, family gatherings, or mixed groups where someone always arrives late.
- You want a game that can be taught in the time it takes to deal the grid.
- Your group likes to talk, argue, and laugh at a spectacular wrong guess.
- You need something that works from four players to a dozen.

**Buy Decrypto if:**

- You have a regular group of four to six who like puzzles and deduction.
- You want everyone working every round, not one spymaster at a time.
- Your table enjoys quiet concentration as much as table talk.
- You have already played Codenames to death and want the next step.

**Buy both if** you host more than one kind of evening. They sit together on a shelf happily. Codenames is the opener; Decrypto is the game for the people who stayed. Both are small, both are cheap by hobby standards, and both will be on the shelf at The Ludarium in Indianapolis.

## FAQ

### Is Decrypto harder than Codenames?

Slightly. The rules take a few more minutes to explain and the first round feels odd. Once a group has played a round, the difficulty is in the play, not the rules.

### Can you play Codenames with 2 or 3 players?

Yes. The base game includes a cooperative variant for two or three players, and Codenames Duet is a dedicated two-player version that many couples prefer.

### How many players is best for Decrypto?

Four to six, split into two teams of two or three. That keeps the clue rotation quick and gives every player a turn giving clues.

### Which is better for a work event?

Codenames, usually. It is faster to teach, scales to a big room, and the shared grid makes it easy to project or lay out for a crowd. Decrypto works for a smaller team that already knows each other.
