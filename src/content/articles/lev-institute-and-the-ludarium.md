Longevity and social connection are usually filed under different headings. One belongs to labs, supplements, and biomarkers. The other belongs to community centers and advice columns. The research does not support the separation, and neither do we. The LEV Institute is a longevity-ethics project. The Ludarium is a game lounge. This article explains why they are connected, and why we think a Thursday night of Codenames is a longevity practice in the plainest sense.

We write this in first person because it is our story. Where a detail is still being worked out, we say so.

## Why does a longevity project care about social connection?

Because the evidence is some of the strongest in the field, and it is routinely overlooked by people chasing smaller effects.

Julianne Holt-Lunstad's [2010 meta-analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316), which pooled 148 studies covering more than 300,000 people, found that those with stronger social ties had about a 50 percent greater likelihood of survival over the study periods, an effect the authors described as comparable to quitting smoking and exceeding physical inactivity or obesity. A [2015 analysis](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691614568352/) by the same group found that social isolation, loneliness, and living alone were each associated with higher mortality risk, on the order of 26 to 32 percent higher.

The [Harvard Study of Adult Development](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2023/02/work-out-daily-ok-but-how-socially-fit-are-you), running since 1938, has reported that satisfaction in close relationships in midlife predicted health and happiness at 80 better than cholesterol or blood pressure at 50. Its directors coined "social fitness" to describe the ongoing work those relationships require.

The [U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf) drew on this body of work to describe loneliness as a public health concern, associating poor social relationships with a 29 percent higher risk of heart disease and a 32 percent higher risk of stroke, and chronic loneliness or isolation with roughly 50 percent higher dementia risk in older adults, more than double the odds of depression, and premature death.

If a pill produced effects of that size, every longevity project in the world would be studying it. Connection is not a pill, so it mostly gets a paragraph at the end. We decided to put it at the front.

## Why games, specifically?

Because when you ask what a social practice needs in order to last fifty years, a game answers every requirement, and almost nothing else does.

| What a lifelong social practice needs | What a standing game provides |
|---|---|
| Recurring, without a decision each time | A fixed night; the game is already chosen |
| The same people over years | A natural group of four to six that rewards familiarity |
| Something to do, so talk is not the burden | The game itself |
| Mental engagement | Memory, planning, language, reading people |
| Works across age and temperament | A 9-year-old and a 79-year-old can play Ticket to Ride |
| Survives bad weeks and hard seasons | Two people and a deck of cards is still a game night |
| Low cost | One table, one game |

The mental side has its own evidence. The [Lothian Birth Cohort in Scotland](https://www.ed.ac.uk/research-innovation/latest-research-news/games-can-protect-thinking-skills-in-older-age) found that people who played more non-digital games like cards and board games in their 70s scored better on memory and thinking tests, and that those who played more over the decade showed less decline, particularly in memory and thinking speed. The researchers described the association as small and did not claim cause. We take the same care; [cognitive reserve and games](/articles/cognitive-reserve-and-games/) lays out what the evidence does and does not support.

Games also do something the research is only beginning to measure: they let people be unguarded. Laughter, teasing, a bad bluff, a shared loss to the board in Pandemic. That is the texture of friendship, and most adult settings have no room for it. A game table has nothing but room.

## What does the ethics part have to do with a game lounge?

More than it might seem.

A longevity ethics project has to ask not only how to extend life but what a long life should be full of, and who gets access to the things that fill it. The research says connection is among the most important of those things. It also says connection is unevenly distributed: by income, by age, by whether you happen to live near your friends, by whether you have a spouse to organize your calendar.

A game lounge is a small, concrete attempt to even that out. A place where a widow can find a table on a Tuesday afternoon without an invitation. Where a caregiver can walk in alone for two hours and be handed a hand of cards. Where a 25-year-old new to the city and a 75-year-old who has lived there forever end up on the same team in Codenames. We have written about each of those people in this series: [finding a table after losing a spouse](/articles/games-after-losing-a-spouse/), games for caregivers, friendship after retirement.

The ethics question becomes a design question. If connection is a longevity practice, what does the gym for it look like? Our answer is a membership lounge, open every night, with a library of games and people whose job is to make sure nobody sits alone. <!-- EDITOR: add LEV specifics -->

## What we will not claim

A longevity project runs on evidence, and the evidence has limits. So, for the record:

- We will not say games prevent dementia. The studies are observational and the researchers who ran them do not say it.
- We will not say a game night adds years to your life. The research on connection is strong, but it measures populations, not Thursdays.
- We will not sell play as medicine. If your memory, mood, or health is changing, see a doctor. A game is a good habit to keep alongside care, not instead of it.

What we will say is that the research on longevity and social connection is clear enough to act on, that a standing game with the same people is one of the most durable ways to act on it, and that we are building a place to make that easy.

The game is the reason. The people are the point. That sentence is the whole bridge between a longevity institute and a game lounge.

## FAQ

### What is the LEV Institute?

The LEV Institute is a project on longevity ethics, the questions of what it means to live long and live well. One strand of its work treats games as a social and mental health practice, which is where the Ludarium comes from.

### How are longevity and social connection related?

Large meta-analyses and long-running cohort studies associate strong social ties with substantially lower mortality risk and better late-life health, with effect sizes some researchers compare to quitting smoking.

### Why would a longevity project run a game lounge?

Because a standing game with the same people is one of the few social practices that is recurring, in person, mentally engaging, and sustainable across a whole lifespan, and the lounge is a place to practice it.

### Is the Ludarium a research project?

Not in a formal sense. It is a game lounge, starting in Indianapolis, that is built on the research and works alongside the LEV Institute. What it learns from real tables informs the Institute's thinking.
