Finding board games for all ages, the kind that work for a seven-year-old and a seventy-seven-year-old sitting at the same table, is harder than it sounds. Most "family" games bore the adults. Most adult games lose the kids and frustrate the grandparents. But a handful of games sit in the narrow band where everyone has a real chance and a real reason to pay attention. This article lists them, explains what they have in common, and makes the case that mixed-age play is worth the effort.

## Why does mixed-age play matter?

Modern life sorts people by age. Kids are with kids at school. Adults are with adults at work. Older people, increasingly, are with other older people, or alone. The [U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf) on loneliness found that older adults have the highest rates of social isolation. Young adults were the group most likely to report feeling lonely, at almost twice the rate of people over 65.

Holidays and reunions are supposed to fix this, but they often do not. Everyone is in the same house and still sorted: kids on tablets in one room, adults in the kitchen, grandparents on the couch waiting for someone to sit down next to them.

A game table un-sorts people. A game of Codenames puts a nine-year-old and her grandfather on the same team, working the same problem. For 15 minutes, age stops being the organizing fact.

The benefits run in every direction:

- **Kids** learn to lose, to wait their turn, and to read adults who are not their parents. They also get to be good at something in front of grown-ups, which matters more to a child than most adults remember.
- **Teenagers** get a version of family time that does not involve being questioned. A game gives them a role and a reason to stay. We go deeper on that in [our guide to playing with teens](/articles/board-games-with-teenagers/).
- **Parents** get to watch their kids and their parents interact without having to manage it.
- **Grandparents** get attention, which is scarce, and mental exercise, which matters. The [Lothian Birth Cohort study](https://www.ed.ac.uk/research-innovation/latest-research-news/games-can-protect-thinking-skills-in-older-age) in Scotland found that older adults who played more non-digital games showed less decline in memory and thinking speed between 70 and 79.

There is also the memory dividend. Children who play games with grandparents remember it for the rest of their lives, often in more detail than they remember the gifts.

## What makes a game work across generations?

Games that work from 7 to 77 share a short list of traits. Knowing them helps you evaluate any game on the shelf.

**Short rules, deep play.** If the teach takes more than five minutes, you have lost the youngest and the oldest players before the first turn. The game can still have depth, but the depth has to emerge from simple rules.

**Low reading load.** Small print on cards is a problem at both ends of the age range. Games with icons, colors, and physical pieces beat games with paragraphs of text.

**Luck that softens skill.** If the best strategist always wins, the kids stop playing after two losses. A dose of luck means the seven-year-old can beat her uncle sometimes, and the uncle does not mind.

**No elimination.** A game where players get knocked out early is a game where the youngest player is sitting alone within ten minutes.

**Something to do on other people's turns.** Downtime kills attention at every age. Games where you watch, react, or plan while others play hold the table together.

**Physical presence.** Big pieces, bright tiles, a map that spreads across the table. Grandparents with aging eyesight and kids who want to touch things both benefit.

**Under an hour.** Attention spans at both ends of the range are shorter than in the middle.

## Which board games work for all ages?

Here are the games that pass the test, grouped by what they are good at.

| Game | Ages | Players | Time | Why it works across generations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ticket to Ride | 8+ | 2–5 | 45–60 min | Collect colors, claim routes. Grandparents love the map. |
| Azul | 8+ | 2–4 | 30 min | Beautiful tiles, simple drafting, real strategy underneath. |
| Codenames | 10+ | 4+ | 15 min | Teams mix ages. Everyone contributes a guess. |
| Sushi Go | 8+ | 2–5 | 15 min | Pass cards, collect sets. Teaches in two minutes. |
| Kingdomino | 8+ | 2–4 | 15 min | Dominoes meets map-building. Almost no text. |
| Just One | 8+ | 3–7 | 20 min | Cooperative word game. Nobody loses alone. |
| Qwirkle | 6+ | 2–4 | 45 min | Shapes and colors. Accessible to the youngest and oldest. |
| Carcassonne | 7+ | 2–5 | 35 min | Tile laying. Kids love placing meeples; adults love the scoring. |
| Catan | 10+ | 3–4 | 60–90 min | Trading creates talk. Best when the youngest is 10 or older. |
| Wingspan | 10+ | 1–5 | 60 min | Gorgeous bird cards. Grandparents who like birds are hooked. |

A few notes on using this list.

Ticket to Ride is the single most reliable four-generation game we know. The rules fit in three sentences. The map gives everyone something to look at. Older players often enjoy the geography more than the kids do, and younger players love the colored trains.

Codenames scales to large family groups in a way almost nothing else does. Twelve people at a reunion can split into two teams in a minute. Put the youngest and oldest players on the same team and watch them figure each other out.

Just One is the pick when you want zero conflict. Everyone writes a one-word clue for the guesser, duplicate clues are removed, and the whole table wins or loses together. It is impossible to feel bad after a game of Just One.

For very young players, Qwirkle and Kingdomino are the entry points. A six-year-old can play Qwirkle for real, not just as a mascot.

## How do you run a mixed-age table?

Having the right game is half of it. The other half is how you set the table.

1. **Teach the youngest and the oldest first.** Sit them next to you. If they understand, everyone understands.
2. **Pair across ages for team games.** Grandchild with grandparent, teen with aunt. Break up the usual clusters on purpose.
3. **Let the kids handle the pieces.** Dealing cards, placing trains, counting tiles. It keeps them engaged and gives them a job that matters.
4. **Keep scoring visible.** Score tracks on the board are better than pencil math. Everyone can see where they stand.
5. **Call the ending.** A game that runs long loses the edges of the age range. If the youngest is fading, declare the next round the last and count points.
6. **Put the phones somewhere else.** A [phone basket](/articles/phone-free-game-night/) is the simplest way to keep the teenagers and, let us be fair, the adults present.
7. **Expect to lose to a child.** It will happen in Sushi Go or Kingdomino, and it will be the highlight of their month. Let it be.

## What should you skip?

Some beloved games are traps for a mixed table.

Monopoly is long, eliminates players, and ends in tears more often than not. Risk has the same problems. Trivia games split the table by generation, since the questions favor whoever grew up in the right decade. Heavy strategy games like Gloomhaven or Twilight Imperium belong to a different evening with a different group.

Party games that rely on pop culture references have the same split problem. A clue that is obvious to a 14-year-old is meaningless to a 74-year-old and the reverse. Stick with games where the content is colors, shapes, routes, and words everyone knows.

## Why does this matter in the long run?

The [Harvard Study of Adult Development](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2017/04/over-nearly-80-years-harvard-study-has-been-showing-how-to-live-a-healthy-and-happy-life/) has tracked its participants since 1938, and its central finding is that close relationships predict health and happiness in old age better than social class, IQ, or genes. Those relationships are built in ordinary hours, and a game table is one of the few places where four generations can log those hours together without anyone having to perform.

A grandparent who plays Ticket to Ride with a grandchild every Sunday for three years has given that child something no gift can match: the experience of being known by someone from a different century. The child, in turn, has given the grandparent a reason to stay sharp and a seat at the center of the family.

That is what "togethering" means. The game is just how you start.

## FAQ

### What is the best board game for all ages at a family gathering?

Ticket to Ride is the most reliable single pick, with Codenames the best choice for large groups. Both teach in minutes and work from about age 8 to any age.

### What board games can a 6-year-old play with adults?

Qwirkle, Kingdomino, Sushi Go, and Carcassonne all work with a six-year-old playing for real, not just along. Look for games with icons and physical pieces instead of text.

### Are board games good for elderly people?

Yes. Regular game play in older adults is [associated with slower cognitive decline](https://www.ed.ac.uk/research-innovation/latest-research-news/games-can-protect-thinking-skills-in-older-age), group activities are [among the more effective ways to reduce isolation](https://bmcpublichealth.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1471-2458-11-647) in later life, and games with large pieces and simple rules are easiest on aging eyes and patience.

### How do you keep kids engaged while adults take their turns?

Choose games with short turns and give kids a job like dealing or placing pieces. Team games like Codenames and Just One keep everyone involved every round.
