A cube from collection is the one most Magic players could build this month for nothing. The boxes of draft leftovers in the closet already hold hundreds of playable cards. What they lack is a structure, and structure is free. This article is about sorting what you own into a cube that drafts well, deciding the power level your collection can actually support, and spending money only on the handful of holes that matter.

Plenty of people build a cube this way, and the ones who succeed share one habit: they let the collection pick the power level, instead of picking a power level and discovering the collection cannot pay for it.

## Which power level does your collection support?

Pull every box out and do a rough census before you decide anything. You are not counting every card. You are answering three questions.

1. **How many rares and mythics do you own that you would be glad to see in a draft pack?** If the answer is under 100, your collection wants a peasant or pauper cube. If it is 200 or more across all five colors, a budget rare cube is realistic.
2. **Do you own dual lands of any kind?** Count anything that taps for two colors. Fewer than 20 means mana fixing is your first purchase, no matter which power level you pick.
3. **Which sets are you heavy in?** Three years of drafting one era gives you depth there and nothing elsewhere. That is fine. It will shape the cube's identity.

The honest result for most players is a peasant cube with a dozen rares sprinkled in, and that is a good cube. Commons and uncommons from the last ten years of Limited sets are better than many rares from twenty years ago.

## How do you sort a collection into a skeleton?

Set up the skeleton first, then sort into it. For a 360-card cube: 50 cards per color, 50 gold, 20 colorless, 40 lands. Those are house numbers, not rules, and we explain the tradeoffs in the piece on [color balance](/articles/cube-color-balance/).

Sort in three passes.

**Pass one: color.** Dump everything into white, blue, black, red, green, multicolor, colorless, land. Do not evaluate yet. Just sort. This is the long pass; put a podcast on.

**Pass two: playable or not.** Within each color pile, split into "would be a fine pick in a draft" and "no." Be ruthless about vanilla creatures and overcosted spells. You want each color's playable pile to be 80 to 120 cards deep, so you have choices in pass three.

**Pass three: fill the slots.** Now lay each color's playable pile out by mana value. You are trying to fill a curve, not pick the 50 best cards. A reasonable target per color for a 360 cube is roughly:

| Mana value | Creatures | Noncreature spells |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 to 6 | 3 to 5 |
| 2 | 8 to 10 | 5 to 7 |
| 3 | 6 to 8 | 4 to 6 |
| 4 | 4 to 5 | 2 to 3 |
| 5+ | 3 to 4 | 1 to 2 |

Those bands are a guideline, and aggressive colors (white, red) should lean toward the creature side at one and two mana. The reasoning behind the bands is in [the cube mana curve](/articles/cube-mana-curve/).

## What will your collection be short on?

Every collection built from draft leftovers has the same holes, because retail drafting rewards different cards than cube drafting does.

- **Cheap removal.** Retail drafters play the removal they open and trade the rest away, so collections are thin on it. Expect to buy a few two-mana removal spells per color.
- **One-drops and two-drops.** Nobody keeps the 2/1 for one. Cube needs them for aggressive decks to exist.
- **Mana fixing.** Dual lands in draft leftovers tend to be the common tapped cycles, which is a start. Mana rocks (Signets, Talismans) are cheap and fill the colorless section quickly.
- **Build-around enablers.** Sacrifice outlets, reanimation spells, and card draw that feeds a graveyard deck are rare in leftovers because they were low picks in retail.
- **Gold cards.** You will have plenty of gold cards and most will be the wrong pairs. Every collection is heavy in whichever guilds its favorite sets pushed.

The order of purchases is fixing first, cheap removal second, one- and two-drops third. Those are the categories where a small order of a few dozen commons and uncommons changes how the cube plays.

## Should a collection cube have a theme?

Let the theme emerge rather than imposing one. If you drafted three years of one block, your cube will have that block's flavor whether you planned it or not, and leaning into it is a feature. A cube built from heavy Innistrad and Theros boxes becomes a graveyard-and-enchantments cube naturally; a cube from Ravnica boxes becomes a guild cube.

Resist the urge to cut good cards because they are off-theme. In a first collection cube, the theme is a description, not a rule.

## When do you stop pulling and start drafting?

When each section of the skeleton has a card in every slot, even if some of those cards are placeholders you plan to replace. A cube with 50 white cards where eight of them are mediocre drafts better than a cube with 42 white cards you love. Empty slots break draft math; weak cards just get picked late.

Write down the placeholders as you go. That list becomes your first shopping list and your first changelog.

## How does a collection cube compare to a purchased list?

| | Collection cube | Purchased list |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Near zero, plus gap fills | A few hundred dollars for peasant through budget rare; a 450-card Pauper list on CubeCobra ([The Pauper Cube](https://cubecobra.com/cube/overview/thepaupercube)) totals roughly $150 to $200 at the time of writing |
| Time to build | Two or three weekends of sorting | One weekend once cards arrive |
| Balance at first draft | Lumpy; some colors deeper than others | Usually tested by the list's owner |
| Identity | Yours from day one | Someone else's, until you edit |
| Risk | Weak archetypes you do not notice | Cards you do not care about |

Neither is better. A hybrid is common: sort the collection into a skeleton, then compare your list against a published one on CubeCobra and buy only where the published list has something clearly stronger in a slot you filled weakly.

## Keep the leftovers sorted

The best side effect of a collection cube is that your collection ends up sorted by color and mana value. Keep it that way in labeled boxes. When you cut a card from the cube, it goes back to its box, and when you need a two-mana black creature, you know where the candidates live. The cube becomes a window into the collection instead of a separate thing you own.

## FAQ

### Can you build a cube entirely from a collection?

Yes, if you accept the power level the collection supports. Most players can fill a 360-card peasant cube from draft leftovers, then spend a small amount on fixing and cheap removal.

### How many cards do you need to own to build a cube from collection?

Roughly three to four times the cube's size in playable cards gives enough choice. For a 360-card cube, 1,000 to 1,500 sorted cards is comfortable; fewer works with more placeholders.

### What is the first thing to buy for a collection cube?

Mana fixing. Dual lands and cheap mana rocks make every other card more playable, and collections built from draft leftovers rarely have enough.

### Should I include rares from my collection in a peasant cube?

If they are not much stronger than the uncommons around them, yes. If a rare wins the game on its own at a table of commons, leave it out; a few bombs over an otherwise flat cube make drafts feel like a lottery.

## Your boxes are a cube already

The cards are there. The structure is a free weekend. The game is the reason. The people are the point.
