The best Thanksgiving board games handle three things most games cannot: a crowd of eight or more, an age range from kindergarten to retirement, and a room full of people who are tired, full, and did not come to learn rules. Codenames, Telestrations, Just One, Wavelength, and Rummikub all pass. They teach in two minutes, run with big uneven groups, and let people drift in and out between pie and dishes.

The right game at the right moment turns the dead hour after dinner into the part of the day people talk about on the drive home. The wrong one ends with Uncle Dave explaining Catan to a six-year-old while everyone else finds somewhere to be.

## What makes a game work on Thanksgiving?

The holiday has constraints no ordinary game night does. Pick for them.

- **Big, uneven numbers.** You might have six players or fourteen, and it will change when someone gets up to check the turkey. Party games and team games handle this. Four-player strategy games do not.
- **Sixty-year age spread.** The game has to be playable by a seven-year-old and interesting to a seventy-year-old at the same time. That rules out reading-heavy games and anything with small text.
- **No appetite for rules.** People are full and half-watching football. The teach has to be under two minutes or it will not happen.
- **Interruptions.** Players leave and return. Games with short rounds, or no fixed turn order, survive this.
- **Mixed energy.** Some people want to shout. Grandma wants to sit with a cup of coffee. The best plan has one of each.

If you only remember one thing: team games. Putting people on teams absorbs the age gap, the number problem, and the shy cousin all at once.

## When should you bring the games out?

Timing matters as much as the pick. A rough schedule that works:

**Before dinner, while cooking.** Qwirkle or Rummikub on a side table for the people who are not cooking and not watching football. Low energy, no commitment, easy to abandon when the food is ready.

**Right after dinner.** Nothing. People need twenty minutes. Forcing a game on a full room is how games get a bad name at your family's holiday.

**The dead hour before dessert.** This is the moment. Happy Salmon for two minutes to wake everyone up, then Codenames or Telestrations with the whole room. Thirty to forty minutes total.

**After dessert, late.** The crowd thins to the people who want to stay. Wavelength if the group is still big, Ticket to Ride or Skull King if it is down to five or six.

Do not announce "game time." Set up Codenames on the cleared table, start dealing yourself in with two or three willing people, and let the rest wander over. Announcing creates resistance. Starting creates curiosity.

## How do you handle the age gap?

Teams, and smart pairing. In any team game, pair the youngest child with the oldest adult. Both are often the least confident at the table, and together they are a unit with a reason to talk. A seven-year-old and a grandmother giving a Codenames clue together is the whole point of the day.

A few more things:

- **Pick games with no reading or big pieces for the low-energy table.** Qwirkle, Rummikub, and Dixit all qualify. Our piece on [games to play with grandparents who say they don't play games](/articles/games-to-play-with-grandparents/) goes deeper.
- **Give the kids a real role.** Timer-keeper, card-dealer, scorekeeper. Kids who have a job stay at the table.
- **Let the teenagers run one.** A sixteen-year-old explaining Wavelength to the adults is a sixteen-year-old who stays off their phone for forty minutes.
- **Keep a second, quieter game running.** Not everyone wants Telestrations. A Rummikub game in the next room is a kindness to the introverts.

We have written more broadly about [four generations at one table](/articles/multigenerational-board-games/), and the rule from that piece holds here: the game that works is the one the youngest can play and the oldest does not find childish.

## What should you not bring to Thanksgiving?

- **Monopoly, Risk, anything long.** The holiday has no three-hour window. Even if it did, elimination games and holidays are a bad mix.
- **Trivia.** Knowledge gaps between generations make it lopsided, and it turns the table into a quiz.
- **Cards Against Humanity.** Your grandmother is at the table. Even if she would laugh, your cousin's new partner is at the table too.
- **Anything you have not played.** Thanksgiving is not the day to learn a game from the rulebook. Play it once at home first.
- **Games with direct attacks.** Family dynamics are already present. Do not hand them a mechanic.
- **Games that need everyone for the whole time.** People will get up. Pick games that survive it.

## What if your family "doesn't play games"?

Start smaller than you think. Happy Salmon for two minutes. Just One for one round. A game that ends before anyone can object is the best argument that games are fine.

And accept that the first year is a pilot. Three people playing Codenames in the corner while everyone else watches football is a success. Next year it is six. The year after, someone else brings a game. That is how a family tradition starts, and it is better than trying to launch one with a speech.

## FAQ

### What is the best board game for a big family on Thanksgiving?

Codenames. It plays with almost any number, splits into teams that absorb the age gap, and a round takes fifteen minutes.

### What games can kids and grandparents play together on Thanksgiving?

Qwirkle, Rummikub, Dixit, and Codenames: Pictures all work across sixty years of age without reading-heavy cards or long teaches.

### When is the best time to play games on Thanksgiving?

The dead hour between dinner and dessert. Everyone is still there, the table is clear, and nothing else is happening.

### How do you get a family that doesn't play games to try one?

Set the game up and start playing with two or three willing people rather than announcing it. Pick something that takes two minutes to learn and under twenty to play.
