The Ludarium is a game lounge business, not a game store, and the difference is the whole plan. A store sells boxes and gives the tables away. A lounge sells the tables, the people at them, and the habit of coming back, and it does not sell boxes at all. This is the first in a series of articles where we explain, as plainly as we can, what we are building in Indianapolis and why. We will get some of it wrong. We would rather do that in public.

## What is the difference between a game store and a game lounge?

A game store is a retailer. Its revenue is product: board games, card packs, miniatures, paint. The play space in the back exists to move product, and it is a cost line, not a revenue line.

A game lounge flips that. The room is the product. Revenue comes from people paying to belong to it, monthly, the way they pay for a gym or a coworking desk. The library of games is an amenity, like the weights or the Wi-Fi.

We have written about this contrast before, in [what coworking got right that game stores got wrong](/articles/coworking-vs-game-stores/). The short version: coworking proved that people will pay to be in a room full of people doing what they do. Game stores proved the love of the hobby is there. We have not found anyone doing both in our city, so we are.

## Why not just open a good game store?

We considered it. Plenty of people love their local store, and so do we. But three things pushed us the other way.

**The store's best customer is its worst customer.** The person who shows up every Thursday, teaches newcomers, and holds the room together might buy one sleeve pack a month. A store needs that person and cannot afford them. A lounge is built for exactly that person, because that person is the membership.

**Retail margins are not ours to win.** Online sellers will always undercut a shop on a copy of Wingspan. A store competes on service and community, then gives both away for free. We would rather charge for the thing people came for.

**The room has to be designed for people, not stock.** A store needs square footage for shelves and fast table turns for events. A lounge needs comfortable chairs, good light, quiet corners, and long evenings. Those pull in opposite directions, and we did not want to spend a decade losing the tug-of-war.

## What does a game lounge business actually sell?

Four things, roughly in order of how much we expect them to matter.

1. **Membership.** What we expect it to include: table time, library access, reservations, member events, and guest passes. We explain the mechanics in [how a game lounge membership works](/articles/game-lounge-membership/). This is the core.
2. **Corporate tabletop events.** Team sessions for companies, run by us, on their calendar. This comes first in time, before the room exists, because it funds the room. More on that in a later article.
3. **Food and drink.** Coffee by day, something stronger by night, food you can eat with one hand. A real revenue line, but a supporting one.
4. **Content and digital products.** The articles you are reading, a newsletter, RPG demo PDFs, and affiliate links when we recommend a game we love. This is how people find us before there is a door to walk through.

What we will not sell is games. We will recommend them, teach them, and tell you where to buy them locally. But the moment we have a retail shelf, we have a retail business, and the shelf starts making decisions about the room. We give that its own article: [why we won't sell games](/articles/why-game-lounges-dont-sell-games/).

## Why does the membership model matter more than the games?

Because a membership changes who the business is for.

Under a cover charge, the ideal customer is a loud group of eight on a Saturday night. The owner needs volume, so the room gets designed for volume: party games, fast turns, hard surfaces. The quiet Tuesday campaign table, where a group of four has been playing Gloomhaven for eighteen months, does not pay.

Under a membership, the ideal customer is the person who comes three times a week for two years. Designing for that person produces a different building. Quieter. Warmer. More like a club than a bar.

It also produces a business that can plan. Recurring revenue lets us sign a lease we can carry, hire someone whose only job is to teach games and remember names, and buy chairs that are still comfortable at hour four. A cover-charge venue is always reacting to last weekend. We want to be planning next year.

## Why Indianapolis?

Because it is home, and because it already has the ingredients.

Indianapolis hosts Gen Con, the [largest and longest-running tabletop convention in North America](https://gamingtrend.com/news/gen-con-2025-breaks-attendance-records-again/), every summer. For four days the city is the center of the hobby, and then everyone goes home and the energy scatters. We think a city that can fill a convention center can fill a lounge the other 361 days.

It also has what every mid-sized American city has: a lot of adults who played games in college, had kids, moved to the suburbs, and quietly lost the people they used to play with. That is the person we are building for. Not the hobbyist with four hundred games on the shelf, though they are welcome. The person who misses the table and does not know where to find one.

## What is "togethering" and why is it on everything we make?

Our mission line is "Togethering for a lifetime." Togethering is our word for people coming together, usually in person, to share life. The game is the medium. It gives a group of adults a reason to sit down, a structure for the time, and a way to be together without having to perform.

We needed the word because the existing ones did not fit. "Socializing" sounds like a cocktail party. "Networking" is worse. "Hanging out" undersells what happens when four people spend three hours solving a problem together. We will write a full piece on the word itself. For now, know that it is the thing the business exists to produce. Memberships, events, and coffee are how we pay for it.

## What are we worried about?

Several things, and we would rather say them now than pretend later.

| Worry | What we are doing about it |
|---|---|
| We open the room before we can fill it | Events and content first; the room comes after the community exists |
| Rent eats the membership | Grow into space rather than ahead of it; no lease the member count cannot carry |
| It becomes a hobbyist clubhouse | Program for newcomers every week, not just regulars |
| We underestimate hospitality | Treat it as a hospitality business with a membership layer, not a tech company |
| People love the idea and do not join | Test willingness to pay with an early membership offer before signing anything |

That last one is the big one. Everyone says they want a third place. Fewer people pay for one. The only way to find out is to ask, early, and we will share what we learn.

## FAQ

### What is a game lounge business?

A venue where people pay a membership to access tables, a curated game library, and a community of regulars, rather than paying per visit or buying products. Revenue comes from membership, events, and food and drink.

### Is a game lounge the same as a board game cafe?

Not quite. A cafe usually charges a cover per visit and may sell games. A lounge runs on memberships and is designed for regulars. The Ludarium is a lounge.

### Will The Ludarium sell board games?

No. We recommend games, teach them, and point you to local stores to buy them. We do not stock retail.

### When does The Ludarium open in Indianapolis?

We do not have a date yet. We are building the community, the content, and the corporate events service first, and the physical space follows.
