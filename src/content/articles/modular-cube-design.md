A modular cube is one fixed core of cards plus two or more labeled modules that can be added or swapped to change the draft. Monday's casual night gets the core plus the low-power module. Saturday's grudge match gets the core plus power and fast mana. New-player night gets the core alone. The cube is one box, the maintenance is one list, and the group gets several formats without the owner building several cubes.

This article covers how to size the core, how to build modules that change the draft without breaking it, and the bookkeeping that keeps the whole thing from turning into a pile.

## What problem does a modular cube solve?

Three problems that a single fixed cube cannot.

**Different nights want different cubes.** The same group that wants a brutal powered draft one week wants a relaxed, synergy-heavy one the next. A fixed cube is tuned to one of those. A modular cube is tuned to a core and adjusts.

**Attendance varies.** A 360 core seats eight. Add a 180 module and it seats twelve, or gives eight players a 540's variety. Remove it and four players draft the core at full visibility. The size follows the room.

**Owners want to test without committing.** A module is a reversible change. Build the "graveyard matters" module, run it for three drafts, and either fold its best cards into the core or put it back in the box. Testing a theme this way is far cheaper than rebuilding the core around it.

Publicly documented examples exist. One on CubeCobra, listed as The Modular Cube by designer TheLameSauce, runs a 299-card core with four optional modules: an Expansion module for added variance and complexity, an Un module, a desert-themed Drought module, and a Conspiracy module built around draft-matters cards. Its description says the modules can be added to the core to shape the kind of draft you want ([CubeCobra](https://cubecobra.com/cube/overview/ModularCore)). The idea scales from a casual kitchen-table cube to a serious one.

## How big should the core be?

Big enough to draft on its own. This is the one rule that separates a modular cube from a cube with a junk drawer.

The core must be a complete, balanced cube at some standard size: 180 for four players, 360 for eight. Every color has its roles filled, every supported lane has its signposts, the land section is proportional. If the core cannot be drafted alone and produce a good night, then it is not a core; it is a 300-card cube that needs its module to function, and the modularity is an illusion.

House guidance: build the core at 360 and modules at 90 or 180. Here is why those numbers.

| Configuration | Total | Seats at 3 × 15 | What it is for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core only | 360 | 8 | The default night; fully visible draft |
| Core + 90 | 450 | 10, or 8 with variety | A theme added without changing size class |
| Core + 180 | 540 | 12, or 8 with more variety | The big-night format |
| Core + 90 + 90 | 540 | 12 | Two themes at once |
| Core minus 180 | 180 | 4 | Weeknight at someone's house, if the core is built to split |

That last row is worth designing for. If the core's 360 is built so that a marked 180 subset is itself balanced, you get a four-player cube for free. Mark the subset in your spreadsheet and sleeve it identically; nobody at the table needs to know.

## What makes a good module?

A module is not "180 more cards." It is a 90- or 180-card set with a single stated effect on the draft, built in the same proportions as the core.

Good module ideas, each with the effect it has:

- **Power module (90).** Moxen, Black Lotus, Sol Ring, Time Walk, Ancestral Recall, Mana Crypt, the strongest fast mana and card advantage. Effect: the draft becomes about who finds the broken cards. Our fast-mana piece covers what this does to a table.
- **Synergy module (180).** Deepens three or four of the core's lanes by six to ten cards each, plus the extra gold to support them. Effect: lanes become reliable and good-stuff drafting gets punished.
- **Low-power module, by subtraction.** Not an addition but a marked set of the core's strongest 60 cards that come out for new-player night. Effect: games are slower, decisions are clearer, nobody loses to a turn-two combo.
- **Fixing module (40 to 60).** A third and fourth land cycle plus more rocks. Effect: three-color decks become normal and gold cards get taken earlier.
- **Chaos module (90).** Conspiracies, draft-matters cards, Un-cards, cards that alter the rules. Effect: the night is about the draft, not the games.
- **Set module (180).** Cards from one block, for a flavored night. Effect: nostalgia and a changed card pool with the core's structure intact.

The test for any module: can you describe its effect in one sentence, and does it keep the cube's proportions? A module that is all four-drops and no lands breaks whichever configuration it joins.

## How do you keep the proportions when swapping?

Build every module to the same ratios as the core. If the core is 360 with 45 per color, 30 gold, 25 colorless, and 50 lands, a 180 module is 22 or 23 per color, 15 gold, 12 colorless, and 25 lands, adjusted for the module's theme.

Two exceptions.

**Power and fixing modules are deliberately lopsided.** A power module is mostly colorless and blue. A fixing module is all lands. That is fine, because they are small and their imbalance is the point. Keep them under 90 so they tilt the cube rather than replace it.

**Subtraction modules remove in proportion.** When you pull the strongest 60 cards for a low-power night, pull twelve per color, not forty blue cards. Otherwise the low-power night is also the no-blue night.

Beyond ratios, watch the roles. If the core has ten white one-drops and a synergy module adds none, the aggro lane is thinner at 540 than at 360. Every module should carry its share of cheap creatures and cheap removal, even when its theme is elsewhere. The [expanding a cube](/articles/expanding-a-cube/) method applies to modules exactly: scale every role, not just the exciting ones.

## The bookkeeping

A modular cube lives or dies on its list. Four habits keep it alive.

1. **One spreadsheet, one column for module.** Every card has a value: core, core-180 (the four-player subset), power, synergy, chaos, and so on. Filter by module and you have the night's list.
2. **Physical separation that survives a shuffle.** Different sleeve colors per module is the cleanest. A small colored dot sticker on the sleeve's back corner is cheaper and works if the sleeves are opaque. Whatever you choose, you need to re-sort 540 cards into their modules at the end of the night, and you want it to take five minutes.
3. **A primer page per configuration.** "Core + synergy" has its own archetype list because the lanes are deeper. "Core + power" has its own pick-order notes. Drafters should know which cube they are drafting before pack one. Our cube primer guide has the shape.
4. **A standing configuration schedule.** First week of the month is core plus power, second is core plus synergy, and so on. Variety is the modular cube's point, and a schedule is what stops the owner from running the favorite module every week.

## When a modular cube is the wrong answer

If your group drafts the same configuration nine times out of ten, you have a fixed cube with a box of extras, and the extras are a maintenance cost with no return. Fold the best of them into the core and retire the rest.

If the core needs a module to be fun, the core is too small or too weak; fix the core.

If re-sorting after each draft is the thing that makes you not want to host, drop to two configurations and a single sleeve color with dot stickers. Convenience decides whether a cube gets played, and a cube that gets played beats a clever one.

The game is the reason. The people are the point. A modular cube is for a group whose people want different games on different nights, and it should cost the owner only a column in a spreadsheet to give it to them.

## FAQ

### What is a modular cube?

A cube built as a fixed, fully draftable core plus one or more labeled modules that can be added or removed to change the draft's size, power level, or theme.

### How big should a modular cube's core be?

A standard draftable size on its own, usually 360 for eight players. The core should produce a good draft with no modules attached.

### How do you keep modules separate?

Different sleeve colors per module, or a small colored sticker on each sleeve, plus a module column in the cube spreadsheet so the list for any configuration is one filter away.

### Can a module be removed instead of added?

Yes. A low-power configuration is often a marked subset of the core's strongest cards that are removed for new-player nights. Remove in proportion across colors.
