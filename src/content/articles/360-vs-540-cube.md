The 360 vs 540 cube question has a short answer. If your group drafts with eight players most weeks and you want every draft to feel different, build 540. If you draft with four to eight players, want a tighter and more consistent format, or want to finish the list this month, build 360. Both numbers come from the same arithmetic: eight drafters, three packs of fifteen cards each, is 360 cards. A 540 cube is that same draft with half the cube left in the box.

Everything else in this article is about what that leftover half does to your games, and whether you want it.

## Where do 360 and 540 come from?

Eight players times 45 cards each is 360. That is the smallest cube that supports a standard eight-player draft with no cards left over, and it is why 360 became the default size when cube was new. Every card in the cube gets opened every draft.

540 is 360 times one and a half. With 540 cards and eight drafters, 180 cards stay in the box each time. Which 180 is random, so the format shifts draft to draft. The MTGO Vintage Cube runs at 540, which is a large part of why that number feels standard today ([MTGO's own update posts](https://www.mtgo.com/news/eoe-vintage-cube-update) describe the cube as having 540 slots).

Other sizes exist and we cover them in [the 180-card cube](/articles/180-card-cube/) and in our piece on 720 and beyond. But 360 and 540 are the two most people choose between, and for good reason: one is the minimum for eight, and the other is the largest size that still feels like one coherent format.

## What does the extra 180 cards actually change?

Four things, and they pull in different directions.

**Variance goes up.** In a 360 cube, every drafter knows Swords to Plowshares is in the draft somewhere. In a 540 cube, there is a one-in-three chance it is in the box. Drafters cannot count on specific cards, which makes reading the table matter more and makes memorized pick orders matter less.

**Archetype support gets thinner per draft.** If your cube supports a Reanimator lane with twelve cards, a 360 draft always has all twelve on the table. A 540 draft has eight on average, and some nights four. Either the lane gets more cards in the full list to compensate, or it becomes a lane that is sometimes open and sometimes not. Designers of 540 cubes usually accept the second outcome and build lanes with more redundancy. Our archetypes guide covers how many lanes a cube can hold at each size.

**Repeat drafts feel fresher.** A group that drafts the same 360 every week starts to know it by heart within a couple of months. That is not bad; it is how a format becomes skill-testing. But some groups want surprise more than mastery, and 540 delivers it.

**The list is harder to finish and keep tight.** Card 361 through 540 are, by definition, weaker or more situational than the first 360. The question is whether they are good enough to be worth opening. A 540 that is really a 360 plus 180 filler plays worse than the 360 alone.

## How does your pod size decide the answer?

This is the practical part. Count how many people show up on a normal night, not your best night.

| Typical attendance | 360 cube | 540 cube |
|---|---|---|
| 4 players | Opens half the cube; can run two drafts back to back without repeats | Opens a third; three distinct drafts possible |
| 6 players | Opens 270 of 360; strong consistency, familiar fast | Opens 270 of 540; feels different every week |
| 8 players | Opens everything; the format is fully visible each night | Opens two thirds; variance is the feature |
| 10 to 12 players | Two pods need two drafts' worth of cards; not enough | Two pods of six can draft from one 540 simultaneously (540 ÷ 2 = 270 each) |

That last row matters for a growing group. A 540 cube lets you split twelve people into two pods of six with one cube and no overlap. A 360 cannot. If your group is on the way to double-digit attendance, 540 solves a problem you will have soon. We wrote up how to run that night in our two-pod logistics piece.

## Which size is easier to build and maintain?

360 is easier to build, easier to maintain, and cheaper. That sounds obvious but the size of the gap surprises people.

**Building.** A 360 cube needs about 45 playable cards per color after lands and gold. Most Magic players with a few years of collection can fill that from a binder, with gaps in removal and fixing. A 540 needs about 65 per color, and the last 20 in each color are where you start buying or proxying.

**Maintaining.** When a new set comes out, a 360 owner looks for the two or three best cards per color and asks what they displace. A 540 owner has more room, which sounds easier but means weaker cards survive longer because there is no pressure to cut. Lists drift. Our guide to updating a cube without breaking it applies to both, but the discipline is harder to keep at 540.

**Cost.** At identical power level, a 540 costs about one and a half times the 360, with the extra cost landing on lands if you run fixing cycles, since a 540 needs more of each cycle to keep the ratio. In practice the gap is wider than the card count suggests: the second land cycle a 540 needs is usually the most expensive cycle you own, so the 540 version of the same cube often costs closer to double the 360 version in real cards.

**Transport.** A 360 in sleeves fits in one 400-count box. A 540 takes a two-row box or two singles, plus basics. Small thing, but the person carrying it every week will notice.

## Does 540 play worse for aggro and two-color decks?

It can, and it is the most common structural complaint about large cubes. Aggro depends on density: a red deck needs enough one- and two-drops in the draft to fill its curve. In a 360 that density is guaranteed. In a 540 it fluctuates, and on a night when half the two-drops stay in the box, the aggro drafter ends up with a midrange deck and a bad time.

The fix is not to avoid 540. It is to overbuild the cheap creature slots so that two thirds of them is still enough. A 540 that runs the same number of one-drops as a 360 will have aggro problems. One that runs one and a half times as many will not. Treat the cube's aggro package as a ratio, not a count, and the size takes care of itself. Our aggro-in-cube piece has the specific counts.

Two-color discipline follows the same logic. If your fixing is one cycle of ten duals, a 540 draft sees about seven of them. Three-color decks get hard, which many designers like. If you want three-color decks to be a real option at 540, run two or three cycles.

## A decision checklist

Answer each one. Count the 540 answers.

1. Do you have eight or more drafters on a normal night? (Yes leans 540.)
2. Do you draft weekly or more often with mostly the same people? (Yes leans 540, for freshness.)
3. Do you want drafters to be able to master the list and plan around specific cards? (Yes leans 360.)
4. Do you want the list finished and stable within a month? (Yes leans 360.)
5. Are you likely to need two pods from one cube within a year? (Yes leans 540.)
6. Do you care more about aggro being reliable or about variety? (Reliable leans 360.)

Three or more for 540 and you should build it. Otherwise build 360, draft it for six months, and expand when the group tells you to. Expanding a tight 360 to 540 later is a far better path than shrinking a bloated 540, and we have a guide for each: [expanding a cube without diluting it](/articles/expanding-a-cube/).

## FAQ

### Is a 540 cube better than a 360?

No. 540 trades consistency for variety and supports larger groups. 360 is tighter, cheaper, and lets drafters learn the format. Both are standard and both are run by experienced designers.

### How many players can a 360 cube support?

Eight players in a standard draft of three fifteen-card packs uses all 360 cards. Fewer players also work and leave cards unopened; more than eight needs a bigger cube or smaller packs.

### Can I draft a 540 cube with four players?

Yes. Four players open 180 cards, a third of the cube, so each draft sees a different slice. Some groups prefer a smaller cube for four-player tables so that archetypes stay dense; see our piece on the 180-card cube.

### Should a beginner build 360 or 540?

Build 360. It is finished sooner, the weak spots are easier to see, and every card gets tested every draft. Grow to 540 when attendance or boredom asks for it.
