Most 7 Wonders strategy advice begins with the card types. It should begin with the two people sitting next to you. In a seven-player game, you only ever interact with your left and right neighbors: you buy resources from them, you fight wars with them, and you pass them the cards you did not take. The other four players might as well be at a different table.

This guide covers the base game, first or second edition. The second edition changed some card art and a few balance points but the shape of the game is the same. <!-- AFFILIATE -->

## How does 7 Wonders work in one paragraph?

Three ages. In each age, every player gets a hand of seven cards, picks one, and passes the rest. The picked card is either built (paying its cost in resources or coins), used to build a stage of your Wonder board, or discarded for three coins. After six picks, the seventh card is discarded. At the end of each age, you compare military strength with each neighbor and gain or lose victory tokens. After the third age, you add up points from military, coins, Wonder stages, civic buildings (blue), commercial buildings (yellow), guilds (purple), and science (green).

That is the whole game. It takes thirty minutes, and almost every decision is "which of these seven cards helps me most and hurts my neighbors least."

## What should you draft in age one of 7 Wonders?

Resources. Nearly always resources.

Age one is where you set up your economy. Brown cards (wood, stone, clay, ore) and grey cards (glass, papyrus, cloth) cost little or nothing and let you build everything that follows. A player with a broad resource base builds whatever they draft in ages two and three. A player without one spends ages two and three discarding for coins.

Priorities for the first hand:

1. A resource card you lack that your Wonder board does not already provide.
2. A card that produces two different resources (the choice cards), because flexibility is worth more than quantity early.
3. A cheap blue card worth three points if nothing above applies.
4. Military, only if a neighbor is already building it and you can match cheaply.

Do not take science in age one unless the card is free and nothing else is useful. Science that does not reach a set is nearly worthless, and age one is too early to know whether the sets will come.

Watch what your neighbors need as well. If the player to your left has no stone, do not pass them stone. You are their supplier of last resort, and every coin they pay you for a resource is a coin you can spend.

## Why is science a trap in 7 Wonders?

Science scores in two ways: each symbol type scores the square of how many you have (one, four, nine, sixteen), and each complete set of all three symbols scores seven. A player with four of each symbol scores forty-eight plus twenty-one. That is a winning score by itself, and it is what makes science so tempting.

Here is why it fails more often than it works.

- **It is visible.** Every green card you play is face up. Once a neighbor sees you with three green cards, they will take green cards out of every hand before passing it to you, even if they discard them. This is called hate-drafting and it is the correct play.
- **It is dependent on the draft.** You cannot control which symbols come. Three gears and one tablet scores nine plus one plus zero for sets. Two of each scores four plus four plus four plus fourteen. The difference between those outcomes is luck.
- **It competes with your economy.** Green cards cost resources, especially in age three. A player who commits to science must also have a strong resource base, which means age one had to go perfectly.

Science works when nobody else at the table is interested in it, when your neighbors are not paying attention, or when you can supplement it with the Wonder boards or guild cards that provide wild science symbols. At a table of experienced players, it is a strategy that wins big one game in four and loses badly the other three.

Our suggestion for improving players: do not plan science. Fall into it. If age one hands you two green cards for free and age two offers a third, take them and see. If the neighbors start cutting you off, stop and pivot to blue and commercial cards. A half-built science strategy that pivots in time loses less than one that stubbornly continues.

## How much military do you need in 7 Wonders?

Military is a two-player game you play against each neighbor separately. At the end of each age you compare shields. The stronger side gets one, three, or five victory tokens depending on the age; the weaker side gets a minus-one token.

The math says military is efficient when you win by one shield and wasteful when you win by five. A single shield that beats a neighbor with zero shields in age three is five points for one card. Four extra shields on top of that earn nothing.

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Both neighbors have zero shields in age one | Take one red card; win both sides cheaply |
| One neighbor is building heavy military | Do not race. Let them win that side, win the other |
| You are one shield behind a neighbor late in age two | Match it. A tie scores nothing for either side and saves you the penalty |
| Both neighbors are militarized | Skip military entirely and spend the cards elsewhere |

Military is also the cheapest way to hurt a neighbor who is running away with the game. A minus-one token per age is small, but denying them the five-point token in age three is not.

The common error is pride. A player who gets beaten in age one decides to "never let that happen again" and spends age two and three on shields. They win military and lose the game, because their neighbor spent those turns building blue cards worth more than the tokens.

## How should you use your Wonder board?

Each Wonder board has a day side (simpler) and a night side (more interesting). Each board provides one starting resource and two to four stages you can build by discarding any card face down and paying the stage cost.

Wonder stages are the game's safety valve. When a hand has nothing you want, and especially when it has something your neighbor wants, bury that card under a Wonder stage. You deny the neighbor, you gain the stage's benefit, and you avoid discarding for a measly three coins.

Some boards reward committing to them fully (Gizah's stages are pure points; Babylon's night side gives science; Halikarnassos lets you build from the discard pile). Others are best left half built. Read your board at setup and decide which stages are worth their cost. A stage that costs three stone for three points is worse than most blue cards.

## How do you read the draft in 7 Wonders?

This is the skill that separates experienced players.

- **Count the hand.** Seven cards in, six picks. At three or four players, a hand you pass comes back around to you with fewer cards in it. At seven players, it never does. Either way, note what is in the hand you pass, because it tells you what your neighbors will have.
- **Watch what your right-hand neighbor keeps.** The cards you receive come from them (in ages one and three; from the left in age two). If they take all the ore, plan to buy ore from your left.
- **Pass the problem left.** Anything that would help your left neighbor should be built, buried, or discarded, not passed. Anything that helps a player three seats away can be passed freely.
- **Yellow cards are underrated.** The commercial cards that reduce the cost of buying from neighbors, or give you coins per resource, are the quiet foundation of most winning boards.

## FAQ

### What is the best strategy in 7 Wonders for beginners?

Draft resources in age one, blue civic buildings in ages two and three, and take one or two military cards when a neighbor has none. It will not produce record scores but it will rarely lose badly.

### Is science good in 7 Wonders?

Science is the highest-ceiling strategy and the most fragile. It works when nobody else wants green cards and your economy can afford them. Against attentive neighbors it is cut off before it scores.

### How many military cards should you play in 7 Wonders?

As few as it takes to beat or tie each neighbor. Winning by one shield scores the same as winning by five.

### Does 7 Wonders play well with three players?

It plays fine, and the draft is tighter because you see each hand more often. The game is at its best with five to seven, where the hands pass around a full table.
