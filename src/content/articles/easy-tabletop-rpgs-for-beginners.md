If you are looking for easy tabletop RPGs for beginners, start with this fact: D&D is one of the harder ones to learn. It is the most popular, the best supported, and the one your friends have heard of. It is also a few hundred pages of rules with a character creation process that takes a new player most of an evening.

The eight systems below trade some of that depth for speed. Each can be taught in an hour or less, several in ten minutes. Each does something D&D does not. And each is a complete, real game that experienced players return to, not a training-wheels version of something else.

## Why would you start with something other than D&D?

Three reasons, and none of them is that D&D is bad.

**Genre.** Not everyone wants elves. If your group would rather do a heist, a horror movie, or a small-town mystery, there is a system built for it that will do the job better than a fantasy game wearing a costume.

**Speed to the table.** Some of the games below have character creation that takes three minutes and rules that fit on an index card. For a group of curious adults with one evening, that matters more than a deep combat system.

**Discovering what you like.** D&D leans toward tactical fights with a lot of dice. Some players find they love that. Others find they love the talking, or the mystery, or the slow-burn drama, and never knew it because their only exposure was a fight with goblins. A lighter game shows you the range of the hobby faster. If you are not sure what the hobby even is, [our tabletop RPG explainer](/articles/what-is-a-tabletop-rpg/) is the place to start.

## The eight systems

Each entry gives you the pitch, how it plays, and how long it takes to learn. Details are described at a general level; check each game's own rulebook for specifics.

### 1. Lasers & Feelings

A one-page science fiction game that fits on a single sheet. Each character has one number that represents where they fall between "Lasers" (cold, technical, precise) and "Feelings" (intuitive, passionate, reckless), and you roll a handful of six-sided dice against that number depending on which kind of action you are taking.

Learn time: ten minutes. It is the game to run when someone says "let's just try one right now." The same one-page design has inspired dozens of free variants covering nearly every genre.

### 2. Honey Heist

You are bears. You are robbing a honey convention. Each character has two stats, Bear and Criminal, and the whole game is the tension between them: get too Bear and you lose the plan, get too Criminal and you forget you are a bear. One page, six-sided dice, pure comedy.

Learn time: ten minutes. Ideal for a group that is nervous about "doing it wrong," because the premise makes wrongness the point.

### 3. Dread

A horror game that uses a wooden block tower instead of dice. When your character attempts something risky, you pull a block. If the tower falls, your character is out of the story. The mounting physical tension of the tower does what no dice mechanic can: everyone at the table is literally holding their breath.

Learn time: five minutes, plus a block tower from the closet. Characters are built by answering a short questionnaire, which doubles as the best character-creation method for beginners we know.

### 4. Fiasco

A game with no game master at all. The group builds a web of relationships, needs, and objects between their characters, Coen brothers style, and then plays out scenes where ambitious plans go badly. Halfway through, a "tilt" makes things worse. At the end, everyone finds out how their character's story ended.

Learn time: thirty minutes, mostly spent on the setup, which is itself the fun part. Best for groups who like drama and dark comedy and want everyone to share the storytelling load.

### 5. Kids on Bikes

Small-town mystery in the Stranger Things mold. Characters are built from tropes (the loner, the popular kid, the brainy one) and stats are assigned different-sized dice rather than numbers, so your strong character rolls a bigger die for strength. The group collectively controls one powered character that the kids are protecting or chasing.

Learn time: about an hour, mostly spent on building the town together, which is a collaborative exercise that gets a quiet group talking fast.

### 6. Blades in the Dark

A heist game set in a haunted industrial city. The mechanic beginners love most is the flashback: instead of planning the job in advance, you start the heist and declare preparations retroactively when you need them. "Actually, I bribed the guard yesterday." This removes the planning paralysis that kills so many RPG sessions.

Learn time: an hour for players. It is the heaviest game on this list for the person running it, but the player-facing rules are compact, and the payoff is a session that feels like a caper film.

### 7. Monster of the Week

A game about a team hunting supernatural creatures, in the style of Buffy or Supernatural. Players pick a playbook (the Chosen, the Expert, the Monstrous) with its own moves, and resolve actions by rolling two six-sided dice and adding a stat, with a three-tier result: success, success with a cost, or failure with a complication.

Learn time: forty-five minutes. The playbooks do the heavy lifting; a player can be ready in ten minutes. The "success with a cost" result is the single best teaching tool for new players, because it keeps the story moving even when the dice are unkind.

### 8. Call of Cthulhu

The oldest game on the list and the only one that is not rules-light, but the core mechanic is the simplest here: your skills are percentages, and you roll two ten-sided dice to get a number from 1 to 100. Roll under your skill, you succeed. Everyone understands percentages on sight.

Learn time: an hour for players. Investigative horror in which the characters are ordinary people and probably doomed. If your group prefers talking and investigating to fighting, this is the classic.

## How do you choose?

| If your group wants... | Try | Prep for the host |
|---|---|---|
| To play tonight with zero setup | Lasers & Feelings, Honey Heist | Almost none |
| Real tension and scares | Dread | A block tower and a few questions |
| No one to be in charge | Fiasco | None; the game runs itself |
| Nostalgia and mystery | Kids on Bikes | An hour of collaborative town-building |
| A caper with a crew | Blades in the Dark | A few hours of reading for the host |
| Monster hunting with a TV feel | Monster of the Week | An hour |
| Classic investigative horror | Call of Cthulhu | A published scenario and a few hours |

If you are not sure, run Lasers & Feelings or Honey Heist first. They cost nothing, they finish in two hours, and they answer the question "does this group enjoy making things up together?" If the answer is yes, every other game on this list is open to you.

## What do these games teach that D&D does not?

The lighter games teach the thing that is hardest to learn in a heavy system: that the rules exist to resolve the story, not to generate it.

In D&D, new players often hunt their character sheet for a legal action. In Honey Heist, the sheet has two numbers, so the player has no choice but to describe what the bear does. The fiction comes first. Once someone has played that way for an evening, they carry the habit back to heavier games, and they are better D&D players for it. It is the same reason a good [five-minute teach](/articles/how-to-teach-a-board-game/) starts with the goal instead of the components.

They also teach the host that running a game is mostly listening. With no monster stat blocks to manage, a first-time GM running Dread or Fiasco spends the whole evening paying attention to the players, which is the actual job.

## Does playing a light game first make D&D harder later?

No. If anything it helps. Players who understand the conversation (describe, decide, roll, narrate) learn D&D's extra layers quickly, because they already know what the layers are for.

What it does change is expectations. A group that has played Dread knows that an RPG can be a tense ninety minutes of whispered decisions. They will not assume every game must be a four-hour fight. That is a healthier starting point than most new D&D players get, and it tends to produce groups that last, because they chose the game that fit the people rather than the people who fit the game. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### What is the easiest tabletop RPG to learn?

One-page games such as Lasers & Feelings and Honey Heist can be learned in about ten minutes and need only a few six-sided dice.

### Can you play a tabletop RPG without a game master?

Yes. Fiasco is designed with no game master; the players share the narration. Several other games offer GM-less or solo modes.

### Are these games cheaper than D&D?

Generally yes. Several on this list are free or pay-what-you-want, and most of the rest cost less than a single D&D hardcover.

### Will D&D players enjoy these games?

Most do. Experienced groups often run light games between campaigns or on nights when a regular player is absent.
