If the game you have been eyeing seems to cost more than it did a year or two ago, you are not imagining it. Board game prices in the US have moved up, and a lot of hobbyists want to know why board games cost more now and whether there is anything to do about it. There is, and most of it is the kind of thing a well-run play group was already doing.

This is the final article in The Math of Game Night, a Ludarium series about what game night really costs. The earlier articles covered [cost per play](/articles/cost-per-play-explained/), unplayed games, and annual budgets. This one is about the price tag itself: where it comes from, why it has risen, and how to keep your cost per hour low while it does.

## Where board games are made

Most of the components in a modern board game are manufactured in China. That includes the plastic pieces in particular, which are the most common example, along with much of the card stock, punchboard, wooden bits, and the printing and assembly that turn all of it into a finished box.

This is not a recent arrangement. The hobby grew up around a supply chain in which a US or European publisher designs and develops a game, a factory in China produces it, and the finished product ships across the ocean to a warehouse and then to a store. The production side of that chain concentrated in China because of its scale, specialization, and cost. A single factory may make games for dozens of publishers.

The result is that a game's shelf price in the US is sensitive to anything that changes the cost of moving goods out of China. Shipping rates, currency moves, and trade policy all show up in the price of a box.

## What changed in 2025

Since 2025, US tariffs on goods imported from China have raised the cost of board games. Tariffs are taxes charged when goods enter the country, paid by the importer, which in this hobby usually means the publisher or its distributor. When the tariff goes up, the landed cost of each game goes up with it.

Publishers have been open about this. Many said the effects would keep showing up through 2026, because games already in warehouses were priced under the old costs, while games produced and shipped afterward carry the new ones. A price increase you see on a store shelf today may reflect a tariff that was applied to a shipment months ago.

That is the whole explanation, stated as plainly as we can. The policy exists, it raises the cost of importing games, and the industry said the effect would continue to work through the system over this year and next. What the policy should be is not a question this article tries to answer, and we are not going to guess what happens after 2026.

## Why publishers cannot simply absorb it

A reasonable question is why the price has to reach the customer at all. The answer is margins. Board game publishing runs on thin ones.

Consider roughly where the money in a game's retail price goes, in the usual shape of the business. A retailer takes a share. A distributor takes a share. The publisher's remaining portion has to cover manufacturing, shipping, art and development, warehousing, and the cost of the games that did not sell. What is left is small, and for many smaller publishers it is very small.

When the cost of manufacturing and importing rises, a publisher has three choices. It can raise the price, cut the components, or lose money on the game. Most have done some mix of the first two: a higher shelf price, or the same price with wooden pieces replaced by cardboard, a smaller box, or fewer extras. A few titles have simply not been reprinted. None of these choices are the publisher being greedy. They are the arithmetic of a business that was never very profitable to begin with.

Keep that in mind when you see a price change. The people making the games you love are mostly small operations, and they are feeling this more than you are.

## How to keep cost per hour low when prices rise

Here is the good news. The price tag is only the first number in the cost per play formula. Price ÷ plays ÷ players ÷ hours has three other terms, and all three are under your control. A higher shelf price is a reason to lean harder on the habits that make a game cheap per hour, not a reason to stop playing.

### Play before you pay

The surest defense against a pricier game is not buying the ones that would not have worked anyway. Play at cafés, conventions, game store open nights, or friends' tables before you commit. [Play before you pay](/articles/play-before-you-pay/) costs nothing, and it turns your estimate of plays into an observation. When prices are up, the [Shelf of Shame](/articles/shelf-of-shame-infinite-cost/) gets more expensive too.

### Buy used

A game that was manufactured and imported two years ago was priced under the old costs, and the person selling it is pricing against what they paid. The used market is where pre-tariff prices still live. It also keeps money inside the hobby instead of sending it through the supply chain again. [How to Buy Used Board Games Without Getting Burned](/articles/how-to-buy-used-board-games/) covers what to check before you pay.

### Share copies across the group

This one does the most work. [The One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/) says a play group needs one copy of a game, not one per person, because the fun from a single copy is already shared by everyone at the table. If the shelf price of a game rises, the cost of one copy for the group rises by that amount once. The cost of four copies rises by it four times, for the same plays.

A group that [claims lanes](/articles/claim-a-lane/), with each person collecting a different genre and the library spread across several homes, is already insulated from price changes in a way an individual collector is not. Each new game is bought once and divided by every seat it serves. The Magic: The Gathering Cube is the model: one card pool, eight drafters, years of play. One copy, many players.

### Prioritize card games and lighter components

Tariffs and shipping costs scale with what is in the box. A game that is mostly cards and a small rulebook has far less plastic and weight than a big box full of miniatures, so its price is less exposed. It is also usually cheaper to begin with: most new games in the US sell for $15 to $60, and the card-driven end of that range is where a lot of the best-value games already sit.

Something like [The Crew](https://link.amazon/B099S9RKf), [Love Letter](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00AGJ4HC2?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Jaipur](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07SRMXRZB?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), or a deck-building game delivers dozens of plays for a fraction of the price of a miniatures-heavy box. None of that is a reason to avoid big games forever. It is a reason to make sure the big games you do buy are the ones your group will play for years.

### Wait for reprints

A game that is out of stock or spiking in price on the secondary market is not gone. Reprints happen, and when they come, the price settles. If a game is not available at a price you like, put it on the list, play it at someone else's table in the meantime, and let the group's shared shelf cover the gap. Patience is one of the cheapest strategies in the hobby.

## The bigger picture

A price increase on a box is real, and it is fair to be annoyed by it. But it changes only one of four numbers in the formula that decides what a game actually costs you. The other three, plays, players, and hours, are set by your group, your habits, and how often the game comes off the shelf.

A $40 game that becomes $48 is twenty percent more expensive at the register. Played 20 times by four people for an hour each, it goes from about 50 cents per person per hour to about 60 cents. A group that shares one copy instead of buying four has saved far more than the tariff ever added. The arithmetic of game night is still overwhelmingly in your favor, as long as the games get played. The game is the reason. The people are the point, and the people were always the part that mattered.

## FAQ

### Why are board games more expensive now?

Most board game components, especially plastic pieces, are manufactured in China. US tariffs on goods imported from China have raised the cost of producing and importing games since 2025, and publishers said the effects would keep appearing on shelves through 2026 as newly produced games replace older stock.

### Do tariffs affect board game prices?

Yes. Tariffs are taxes paid when goods enter the country, and for board games they are usually paid by the publisher or distributor importing from China. Because publishers operate on thin margins, much of that added cost reaches the shelf price, or shows up as reduced components at the same price.

### Will board game prices come back down?

Nobody can say with confidence, and this article does not make predictions. What you can control is the other three parts of cost per play: how many times a game is played, how many people share each copy, and how long the sessions run. Those make far more difference to your real cost than the shelf price.

### How can I save money on board games now?

Play before you pay so you only buy games your group will actually play, buy used where older stock is still priced under current costs, share one copy per play group instead of one per person, favor card games and lighter components, and wait for reprints when a game is out of stock or inflated.

### Are card games cheaper than board games?

Usually. A game that is mostly cards has less material, weight, and plastic than a box full of miniatures, so it costs less to make and ship and is less exposed to tariffs. Many card-driven games sell for $15 to $30 and deliver dozens of plays, which makes their cost per hour very low.
