Commander politics is the negotiation layer that sits on top of every four-player game of Magic: who to attack, who to spare, who to believe, and who to blame when it goes wrong. It is the reason Commander is the most popular way to play the game, and it is the reason more friendships have been strained over a Commander table than over any other format. Both things are true, and this article is about why, and what to do about it.

I love Commander. I also keep a mental list of the games that ended with someone quietly packing up and leaving early. Almost every one of them was a politics problem, not a rules problem.

## What makes Commander political in the first place?

Two-player Magic has one decision about targets: your opponent. Commander, with its usual four players and forty life each, has three opponents and no rule about which one you attack.

That single change creates a social game inside the card game. Every attack is a choice about a person. Every removal spell says "you, specifically." Every turn you do not attack someone is a small favor they may or may not remember.

Add the format's other features and the politics get thicker:

- **High life totals** mean games are long and early aggression rarely wins. Players have time to form opinions about each other.
- **Powerful commanders** make some players obvious threats and others invisible, regardless of what they are actually doing.
- **Table talk is allowed.** You can propose deals, make threats, and lie.
- **Free-for-all scoring.** There is one winner and three losers, so the incentives for temporary alliances are strong and the incentives to honor them are weak.

That is a design for drama. Most of the time the drama is delightful. The rest of the time somebody feels betrayed.

## Why does Commander create resentment?

A few mechanisms come up again and again.

**The focus problem.** Three players decide one player is the threat and attack them until they are out. The target spent two hours building a board and got nothing for it. Sometimes they were the threat. Sometimes they just played the scariest-looking commander. Either way, they went home early and everyone else kept playing.

**Kingmaking.** A player who cannot win decides who does. They spend their last turn attacking one opponent and not another, handing the game to whoever they like best. The loser of that decision remembers it long after the winner forgets.

**Broken deals.** "I won't attack you this turn if you don't attack me." Then the attack comes anyway, because the deal was never binding. In the rules this is fine. In the relationship it is a small lie told to a friend's face.

**The archenemy spiral.** One player wins a few games in a row. The others start targeting them on sight, before they do anything. The winner feels persecuted. The others feel justified. Neither is wrong.

**Mismatched intentions.** Half the table came to win; the other half came to hang out. The winners play tight threat assessment. The hangers-out take it personally. Nobody said which game they were playing.

The pattern underneath all of these is that Commander asks players to make personal decisions and then pretends they were not personal. "It's just the game" is true and also not how the targeted player feels.

## How do other multiplayer games handle this?

Board game designers have spent decades on the same problem, and some of their solutions are worth borrowing.

| Game | Multiplayer problem | How it is handled |
|---|---|---|
| [Catan](https://link.amazon/B0h8BDGR2) | Who to trade with, who to block | Trading is mutual benefit; blocking is costly for the blocker |
| Risk | Pile-on and kingmaking | Not handled; Risk ends friendships for the same reasons Commander does |
| [Cosmic Encounter](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1589944968?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) | Alliances and betrayal | Betrayal is the design; players expect it from turn one |
| Diplomacy | Pure negotiation, binding lies | Same, and it is famous for ruining friendships |
| [Root](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07F454YF3?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) | Asymmetric threat | Each faction's win condition is visible, so threat assessment is objective |
| Commander | All of the above | Left to the table |

The games that work use one of two strategies. Either they make betrayal the explicit theme so nobody is surprised, or they make threat visible and objective so attacking the leader feels like math, not malice. Commander does neither by default. The table has to supply it. The piece on [why social deduction games work](/articles/why-social-deduction-games-work/) covers how designers build betrayal into games on purpose.

## How do you play Commander politics without making enemies?

Rules of thumb that keep a pod healthy. None of them are in the comprehensive rules. All of them matter more.

**Talk before the game.** A thirty-second conversation about power level and intent prevents most mismatches. "I'm playing a fun deck tonight" and "I want to try to win" are both fine. Saying neither is how the trouble starts.

**Attack the board, not the person.** Say out loud why you are attacking. "You have the most cards in hand" is threat assessment. Silence followed by a lethal swing feels like a grudge even when it is not.

**Honor short deals and refuse long ones.** "Not this turn" is a promise you can keep. "Truce until one of us is out" is a promise you will break, so do not make it.

**Spread damage when the threat is unclear.** If nobody is obviously ahead, hit the player with the most life, or rotate. Piling onto one person for no reason is how archenemies are born.

**When you cannot win, do not pick the winner.** Play your best game to the end. If your last turn decides it, attack the player who is most ahead on board, not the one you like least. Say that is what you are doing.

**Do not gloat, and do not sulk.** Both poison the next game.

**Let the eliminated player stay in the room.** Deal them into something, let them watch and heckle, or keep a second game going. Nothing makes politics feel worse than being knocked out at the forty-minute mark and sitting alone for an hour.

## Is there a version of multiplayer Magic without this problem?

Yes, and it is worth knowing about.

Two-headed giant pairs players into teams, which turns politics into partnership. Star and emperor formats give fixed enemies and allies. All of these take the "who do I attack" question off the table.

And cube draft, our favorite format, solves it by returning to one-on-one games. The social layer moves out of the game and into the draft, where the stakes are a card instead of a betrayal. Eight people spend forty-five minutes together passing packs, then play clean two-player matches where the only political question is whether you hate-drafted that dragon. [What a Magic cube is](/articles/what-is-a-magic-cube/) explains why that structure keeps a group together, and [how to run a cube night](/articles/how-to-run-a-cube-draft/) covers the hosting.

Commander is not worse than cube. It is more social, which means it is more dependent on the people being good at being social. When the pod is, it is the best thing in Magic. When it is not, the format offers no help.

## FAQ

### What is politics in Commander?

Politics is the negotiation, alliance, and threat-assessment layer of a four-player Commander game: who you attack, who you spare, what deals you make, and whether you keep them.

### What is kingmaking in Commander?

Kingmaking is when a player who cannot win uses their remaining resources to decide which other player does. It is the most resented move in multiplayer games.

### Is it okay to lie in Commander?

Lying about hidden information is within the rules. Breaking an explicit deal with a friend is also within the rules, and it is the fastest way to make the next game worse. Most healthy pods keep short deals and avoid long ones.

### How do you deal with being targeted every game in Commander?

Ask the table why. If it is because your deck is much stronger than theirs, play a weaker deck. If it is reputation from past games, say so plainly and ask for a reset.
