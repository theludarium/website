Azul vs Sagrada comes up because the two games sit next to each other in every store, look like they belong in a museum gift shop, and arrived within a year of each other. Both are drafting games. Both end with a colorful pattern in front of you. Both play in under 45 minutes and both get bought as gifts for people who "like pretty things."

They are not the same game. Azul is a sharp, interactive puzzle where you can hurt the person next to you. Sagrada is a gentle, personal puzzle where the main opponent is your own earlier decisions. Here is how they differ and which one to buy.

## How does each game work?

**Azul** <!-- AFFILIATE --> puts a ring of factory displays in the middle of the table, each holding four tiles in five colors. On your turn you pick one color from one factory, take every tile of that color there, and push the rest into the center pool. Then you place the tiles you took into one of five rows on your board; each row holds only one color and a fixed number of tiles. Tiles you cannot fit go to your floor line and cost points. When all tiles are drafted, each full row moves one tile to your wall, scoring points for adjacent tiles. The first player to complete a horizontal row on the wall triggers the end. Publisher specs list 2 to 4 players, ages 8 and up, 30 to 45 minutes ([Next Move Games](https://www.nextmove-games.com/en/azul/azul-game/)).

**Sagrada** <!-- AFFILIATE --> gives each player a stained-glass window card with a 4-by-5 grid of colored and numbered restrictions. Each round, dice are drawn from a bag and rolled; players take turns drafting one die at a time, in snake order, and placing it in their window. Placement rules are strict: a die must match the color or number printed on its square if there is one, must touch a previously placed die, and may never sit next to a die of the same color or same number. Tool cards let you bend the rules for a price. After ten rounds you score for your private objective (a color), shared public objectives, and lose points for empty squares. Publisher specs list 1 to 4 players, ages 10 and up, 30 to 45 minutes ([Floodgate Games](https://floodgate.games/products/sagrada)).

## Which one is easier to learn?

Azul, slightly. The teach is: take all of one color from one factory, place them in a row, overflow goes to the floor, full rows score at the end of the round. The one idea people need to see rather than hear is how the wall scoring chains adjacent tiles. After one round, everyone has it.

Sagrada has more placement rules, and they are the kind that are easy to forget mid-game. No same color adjacent, no same number adjacent, must touch an existing die, must match the printed square. Add tool cards, each of which breaks a different rule, and the first game includes a few "wait, can I put this here?" moments. It is still a light game, and the rules are printed on your window card, but it asks for a little more attention.

Neither will stop a family. Azul's box age of 8 is realistic. Sagrada's 10 is too.

## Which one is more interactive?

Azul, and this is the real dividing line.

In Azul, every tile you take changes what is left for everyone else, and you can see exactly what your neighbors need. If the player to your left needs two blue tiles to finish a row, and you take the blues, they do not get them. Worse, if the center pool has six reds and nobody wants reds, the player who is eventually forced to take them eats a floor-line penalty. A sharp player sets those traps on purpose. [The Azul strategy guide](/articles/azul-strategy/) is mostly about this.

Sagrada's interaction is softer. You draft from a shared pool of dice, so you can take the one someone wanted, and in the last pick of a round the choice is often forced. But you cannot push bad dice onto anyone, and nobody can wreck your window. Your mistakes are your own. Some players find this restful. Others find it lonely, because you could almost be playing four separate games.

If your table enjoys a bit of friction and the occasional groan, Azul delivers it. If your table includes someone who hates being targeted, Sagrada removes the possibility.

## Which one has more luck?

Sagrada. Dice are rolled fresh each round, and if the numbers you need do not come up, there is little you can do except burn a tool card. Over ten rounds the swings average out somewhat, and clever players keep their windows flexible to absorb bad rolls. But a late-game round with no usable dice is a real and common frustration.

Azul's luck is in which tiles come out of the bag each round, and it is gentler because the information is public before anyone picks. You can see all the factories before your turn. Bad luck in Azul usually means a bad position, not a bad roll, and bad positions are often someone else's doing.

## Which one feels better to finish?

Sagrada, if you care about the object. A completed window, 20 dice arranged by color and number with no empty squares, is a pleasure to look at, and people photograph them. Azul's wall is pretty too, but it rarely fills, and the game ends when someone else decides it does.

Azul feels better to win. Because the game is interactive, a win in Azul means you outplayed people. A win in Sagrada means you built the best window, which is satisfying in a quieter way.

## Which one works at two players, and solo?

Both are good at two. Azul at two is a tense duel where every draft matters, and many players think it is the best count. Sagrada at two is calm and quick.

Sagrada has a solo mode in the box, where you play against a target score with a shrinking pool of dice. It is a decent puzzle for [an evening alone](/articles/best-solo-board-games/). Azul's base game has no solo rules.

## Verdict: which should you buy?

**Buy Azul if:**

- Your group likes interaction and does not mind being blocked or dumped on.
- You want the faster teach and the game that is harder to play well.
- You play at two a lot and want a sharp head-to-head game.
- You want a game that can be a little competitive without being long.

**Buy Sagrada if:**

- You or your group prefer a calm, personal puzzle where nobody attacks anyone.
- You want a solo mode in the box.
- You like dice, and the small pleasure of a finished, colorful object.
- You have a player who shuts down when targeted.

**Buy both if** you want the two best-looking games on your shelf and host different moods. Azul is the game for when the table is lively. Sagrada is the game for when it is tired. They cost about the same and neither takes much space. Both will be on the shelves at the Indianapolis lounge, probably next to each other, where they belong.

## FAQ

### Is Azul or Sagrada better for two players?

Both play well at two. Azul at two is tighter and more cutthroat, and many players consider it the best count. Sagrada at two is quick and calm.

### Is Sagrada good for kids?

Yes from about 10. The placement rules are strict but printed on each window card, and the dice and colors appeal to children. Azul's simpler rules work from about 8.

### Which is more strategic, Azul or Sagrada?

Azul. Because you can see every tile available and anticipate what others need, skilled play matters a lot. Sagrada rewards planning too, but dice rolls limit how much any one player can control.

### Do I need the Azul expansions or other Azul versions?

No. The original Azul is complete on its own. Azul: Summer Pavilion and Azul: Stained Glass of Sintra are separate games with the same look and different rules.
