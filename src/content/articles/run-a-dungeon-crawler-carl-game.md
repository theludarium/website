You do not need a new system to run a game that feels like Dungeon Crawler Carl. The series runs on a handful of devices that any game master can borrow: an announcer who talks to the players, achievements for doing ridiculous things, loot boxes, a dungeon floor that closes on a timer, and an audience that rewards a good show.

**The quick answer:** keep your usual rules and add five things. Announce achievements out loud when players do something memorable. Hand out loot in boxes instead of treasure piles. Put a visible timer on each floor. Give the party sponsors who send gifts for entertaining play. And play the announcer as a character who enjoys every disaster. If you want the official version, see the [Dungeon Crawler Carl RPG](/articles/dungeon-crawler-carl-rpg/).

## 1. Play the announcer, not just the world

In the books, the dungeon talks. It announces deaths, achievements, rule changes, and new floors, and it clearly has opinions. At your table, give that voice to the game master as a separate character. Use a different tone for it, or read its lines from index cards. When something big happens, stop and announce it.

It does two jobs. It makes the rules part of the fiction, and it gives you a way to comment on the players' choices without breaking character.

## 2. Award achievements for memorable play

An achievement is a short, funny title for something a player did: killing a monster with a door, surviving a fight with one hit point left, or talking a shopkeeper into a terrible deal. Announce it, write it on an index card, and hand it over.

Most achievements should be worth nothing but bragging rights. A few can come with a small reward, like a loot box or a one-time reroll. Players will start chasing them, which is exactly the point.

## 3. Replace treasure with loot boxes

Instead of describing a pile of coins, hand out a sealed box with a quality level. Players open it when they reach a safe room. Keep a short table of box contents for each level, and include a few items that are useless, strange, or funny. A loot box adds a small moment of suspense to every reward, and opening them becomes a ritual the table looks forward to.

| Box | What is inside |
| --- | --- |
| Plain | Consumables: healing, light, rations |
| Fine | One useful item and one strange one |
| Rare | A real upgrade, with a catch |
| Legendary | Something that changes how the character plays |

## 4. Put a timer on every floor

Each floor of the World Dungeon closes after a set time, and anyone still on it when it does is in trouble. A timer changes how players think: they stop searching every corner and start making choices. Track it in real time for short sessions, or in in-game hours for longer ones. Announce the countdown at dramatic moments.

## 5. Add sponsors and an audience

The crawlers in the books are on television, and the audience matters. Pick two or three sponsors with clear tastes, like one who loves big explosions and one who loves clever solutions. When a player does something that would please a sponsor, the sponsor sends a gift. It rewards players for playing to the crowd and gives quieter players a reason to try something bold.

## 6. Make safe rooms matter

Safe rooms are where the dungeon cannot hurt you. Use them for the scenes a dungeon crawl usually skips: shopping, opening loot boxes, arguing about the plan, and talking about what just happened. A good safe room scene is where the party becomes a team.

## Keep the tone right for your table

The books are crude and violent, and also warm. Decide as a group how much of each you want before the first session, and use a simple safety tool like the X-card so anyone can steer away from content they do not want. The heart of the series is two characters looking out for each other, and that is the part worth keeping no matter how dark the jokes get.

## FAQ

### Can you run Dungeon Crawler Carl in D&D?

Yes. Keep the D&D rules and add the series' devices: an announcer, achievements, loot boxes, a floor timer, and sponsors. None of them needs new mechanics beyond a few index cards.

### Do the players need to have read the books?

No. These ideas work for any group. Players who have read the books will catch the references, and the rest will simply have a fun, fast dungeon crawl.

### What is a good first floor?

Something small: a few rooms, one safe room, one boss, and a timer of about two hours of real time. It teaches the rules the same way the tutorial floors do in the first book.

### How many achievements should I give out?

Two or three a session is plenty. Too many and they stop feeling special.
