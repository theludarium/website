Three of the most popular board games of the last decade are about birds, forest animals, and Pacific Northwest wildlife. The Wingspan vs Everdell question comes up constantly, and Cascadia belongs in the same conversation. All three are beautiful, all three are calm, and they are not the same game. One is an engine builder, one is a worker placement game with a story, and one is a tile puzzle you can teach in three minutes.

This guide compares them on the things that matter for a real table: how long they take, how hard they are to learn, how much players interact, and who each one suits.

## What do these three games have in common?

They share a look and a mood. Each has artwork you would frame, a theme that does not involve war or trade routes, and a pace that invites conversation. None of them has direct attacks. You cannot burn down someone's forest or steal their eggs.

That shared gentleness is why they are often lumped together, and why people assume that liking one means liking all three. It does not. The underlying games pull in different directions, and the right pick depends on your group.

## How do Wingspan, Everdell, and Cascadia compare?

| Game | Players | Time | Weight (1–5) | Why it works |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wingspan | 1–5 | 40–70 min | 2.5 | Build a bird engine, feel it hum by round three |
| Everdell | 1–4 | 40–80 min | 3 | Worker placement with a city you watch grow |
| Cascadia | 1–4 | 30–45 min | 2 | Tile and token puzzle, teaches in three minutes |

### Wingspan

Each turn you do one of four things: play a bird card into a habitat, gain food, lay eggs, or draw cards. <!-- AFFILIATE --> Birds you play add powers that trigger every time you use that habitat, so your board becomes an engine that produces more each round. By the final round, a single action can cascade through five birds.

Wingspan's strength is the feeling of growth. Its weakness is that the first two rounds can feel slow to a new player, and the game has little interaction. You mostly tend your own aviary. It plays well at two and three, and slows at five.

### Everdell

A worker placement game set under an enormous cardboard tree. <!-- AFFILIATE --> You send critters to gather resources, then spend them to build a city of cards. Buildings let you play matching creatures for free, so the city develops its own logic. Four seasons pass, and each one gives you back your workers and a few more.

Everdell's strength is the sense of story. Your city looks like a place by the end. Its weakness is the teach. There are a lot of card types and some fiddly rules about who can play what for free. Expect a longer first game. The tree is decorative and takes up most of the table.

### Cascadia

Draft a habitat tile and a wildlife token together, then place both in your growing landscape. <!-- AFFILIATE --> Score for large connected habitats and for arranging animals in patterns set by scoring cards that change each game. That is the whole rulebook.

Cascadia's strength is accessibility. A table of five new players can be scoring by minute four. Its weakness is that it can feel like a solo puzzle, since the only interaction is taking the tile someone else wanted. It won the Spiel des Jahres, and it earns the award by making a deep game out of two simple actions.

## Which one should you buy first?

It depends on who is sitting down.

- New to modern games: Cascadia. It is the lightest, the shortest, and the easiest to teach. Nobody bounces off it.
- Likes building something that grows: Wingspan. Players who enjoy Splendor and want more will find Wingspan's engine deeply satisfying.
- Likes a world and a longer arc: Everdell. The best pick for a group that already plays worker placement and wants theme on top. Our [worker placement explainer](/articles/worker-placement-explained/) covers the format.
- Plays mostly at two: Wingspan or Cascadia. Both are excellent at two. Everdell works at two but loses some of the tension over shared spaces.
- Plays with kids under ten: Cascadia. Wingspan is possible with a patient twelve-year-old. Everdell is a stretch.
- Wants to talk while playing: Cascadia or Wingspan. Everdell asks for more attention.

If you are buying one game to anchor a shelf, Wingspan is the safest bet. It has the broadest appeal, the best solo mode, and enough expansions to stay fresh for years.

## How do they differ in play time and setup?

Box times are optimistic for all three, which is normal. In practice:

- Cascadia sets up in two minutes and plays in 30 to 45 minutes regardless of count. It is the only one of the three you can play twice in an evening without planning for it.
- Wingspan sets up in five minutes and runs 40 minutes at two, closer to 70 at four or five. The final round, where every engine fires at full speed, takes longest.
- Everdell sets up in ten minutes, including assembling the tree, and runs 40 minutes at two and 80 or more at four with new players.

For a weeknight, Cascadia. For a Saturday afternoon, Everdell. Wingspan fits either.

## Which one has the most replay value?

All three have expansions, and all three hold up over dozens of plays. The differences are in where the variety comes from.

Wingspan's variety is in the 170 bird cards. Every game you see a different slice of the deck, and the European and Oceania expansions add more. The bonus cards and end-of-round goals change what you are aiming for.

Everdell's variety is in the card combinations and the expansions, which add a lot. The base game alone can start to feel familiar after fifteen plays, which is still a great run.

Cascadia's variety is in the scoring cards. Five animals, each with four or five scoring patterns, so the puzzle changes every game. It is the lightest of the three and arguably the most replayable per dollar.

## What do these games get right about table presence?

They look good, and that matters more than hobbyists admit. A game that looks beautiful on the table invites people to sit down. Wingspan's egg miniatures and bird feeder dice tower, Everdell's tree, Cascadia's chunky hex tiles all do the same job: they make a stranger say "what is that?"

We wrote more about this in [board game components and table presence](/articles/board-game-components-table-presence/). The short version is that components are part of the design, not decoration, and these three designers understood it.

## FAQ

### Is Wingspan or Everdell better for beginners?

Wingspan. It has four actions and a clear structure. Everdell has more card types and more exceptions, which makes the first teach longer.

### Is Cascadia too simple for experienced players?

No. The scoring patterns create real decisions every turn, and competitive players find plenty to optimize. It is simple to learn, not simple to master.

### Which plays best at two players?

Wingspan and Cascadia are both excellent at two. Everdell works but is better at three or four.

### Can you play Wingspan, Everdell, or Cascadia solo?

Yes. All three include solo modes. Wingspan's Automa is the most developed, and Cascadia's solo puzzle mode is quick and satisfying.
