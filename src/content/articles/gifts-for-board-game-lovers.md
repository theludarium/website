Gifts for board game lovers are hard for one reason: they have already bought the games. Any title you can find in a store, they either own, have played, or have decided against. Buying them a game is buying them a decision they already made.

So do not buy them a game. Buy them the things around the game: the upgrades they keep postponing, the time to play, and the people to play with. This guide is organized that way, with prices checked at publisher or major retailer pages at the time of writing.

## Why is buying a game for a gamer a bad idea?

Three reasons, from experience on both sides of the wrapping paper.

1. **Duplication.** The odds that a serious hobbyist owns any specific popular game are high. Returns are awkward and a duplicate Wingspan is a chore.
2. **Taste.** Gamers have strong, specific preferences. They may dislike exactly the kind of game you picked, and they will be polite about it, which is worse.
3. **Backlog.** Most collectors have a shelf of unplayed games already. Another box adds guilt, not joy.

The exceptions are a game they have specifically named, or a [brand-new release](/articles/best-board-games-2026/) they have not had time to buy. If you have either, go ahead. Otherwise, read on.

## What do gamers never buy for themselves?

The pattern we see is that hobbyists spend freely on games and cheaply on everything else. The gift sweet spot is the upgrade they would enjoy and feel silly buying.

### Upgrades to games they already love

**Premium card sleeves for a favorite game.** Dragon Shield matte sleeves, the standard for card games, run about $8 to $13 per hundred at the time of writing. <!-- AFFILIATE --> [Stomping Grounds lists a 100-count pack at $8](https://www.stompinggroundstcg.com/products/dragon-shield-game-sleeves-matte) with a market price of about $12.49. Ask what game they play most, look up its card sizes, and buy enough to sleeve all of it. This is dull to describe and consistently one of the best-received gifts we know.

**A box insert.** Foam-board inserts from Folded Space turn a box of baggies into a tray that sets up in a minute. <!-- AFFILIATE --> The [Wingspan insert at Miniature Market is $15.99](https://www.miniaturemarket.com/fdswingv2.html) at the time of writing; most are $15 to $30. You need to know which game. The payoff is that they will think of you every time they open the box, which for a favorite game is weekly.

**Metal coins or upgraded tokens.** Many publishers sell metal coin sets for games that ship with cardboard ones. Prices run about $24 to $35 at the time of writing; Stonemaier's [80-coin Scythe set is $29](https://store.stonemaiergames.com/products/80-metal-coins-expeditions-and-scythe) and its [Viticulture lira set is $24 at Miniature Market](https://www.miniaturemarket.com/stm304.html). Splendor already has heavy poker-chip tokens, so skip it; Scythe and Viticulture are the common targets, and Brass: Birmingham's official [Iron Clays chips](https://www.miniaturemarket.com/rox505.html) are the $90 deluxe version of the idea. Check that they own the game.

### Table upgrades

**A neoprene playmat.** A large mat dampens dice, makes cards easy to pick up, and protects the table. Expect about $30 to $60 at the time of writing depending on size and print; [Miniature Market lists a 3-by-3-foot neoprene-backed mat at $33.99](https://www.miniaturemarket.com/battle-systems-gaming-mat-grassy-fields-3x3-bstxx002.html). The 36-by-36-inch size suits most games; go larger only if you know their table.

**A dice tray or tower.** Folding trays start under $10 and [Ultra Pro's magnetic folding tray is $15.99 at Miniature Market](https://www.miniaturemarket.com/vivid-dice-tray-magnetic-foldable-black-upr16332.html); towers run from about $20 for acrylic to $60 or more for resin or hardwood ([a polyresin dragon tower at Noble Knight is $59.95](https://www.nobleknight.com/P/2148113631/Old-School-Dice---Dragons-Fury-Dice-Tower---Green)) at the time of writing. If they play D&D or any dice-heavy game, this gets used every session.

**Good lighting.** No gamer buys a lamp. A warm, bright, adjustable table lamp changes how every game looks, and it is the upgrade visitors notice first. Our piece on [game room setup](/articles/game-room-setup/) has the specifics.

## What about experiences instead of objects?

For a collector, the scarce resource is not games. It is time at the table with people who want to play. Gift that.

**A convention badge.** [Gen Con](https://www.gencon.com/) in Indianapolis bills itself as the largest and longest-running annual tabletop gaming convention, held each summer; the 2025 show drew a [record 72,000 attendees](https://www.axios.com/local/indianapolis/2026/07/27/gen-con-2026-rolls-into-indianapolis-with-momentum). Badge prices and dates for the next year are published on gencon.com; the [2026 four-day badge was $164](https://gamingtrend.com/news/gen-con-announces-2026-registration-and-badge-prices/), with single days from $41 to $112, and the four-day badges sold out well before the show. Smaller regional conventions cost far less and are often a better first gift.

**A hosted game night.** Book a table at a board game cafe, invite their friends, and pay for the evening. The gift is the gathering. When our Indianapolis lounge opens, this will be one membership away.

**A commitment to play.** The cheapest and most appreciated gift on this list: a card that says "one game night a month, my house or yours, I will learn whatever you want to teach." Collectors are starved for willing opponents. Mean it and schedule the first one.

**A Kickstarter pledge they mentioned.** If they have talked about a crowdfunding campaign, pledge for them or cover the pledge. This is the one case where a game is safe, because it is a game they want and cannot buy yet.

## What should you avoid giving a board gamer?

- **A game you picked without asking.** Covered above. The odds are against you.
- **Novelty "gamer" merchandise.** Meeple-shaped earrings, "I'd rather be playing" t-shirts, mugs with dice jokes. They have received these. They are in a drawer.
- **Generic organizer bins.** Serious hobbyists are specific about storage. Ask before buying anything that is meant to hold their collection.
- **A used game of unknown completeness.** Thrift store finds are fun for you and a component audit for them.

## The question that solves this every time

If you can ask one question without giving away the gift, ask this: "What game do you play most?" Then buy sleeves and an insert for that game. It is specific, it is useful, and it says you noticed what they love rather than what they collect.

Gamers do not need more games. They need more nights at the table and a better table to sit at. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### What do you get a board gamer who has everything?

Upgrades for a game they already play often (sleeves, an insert, metal coins), or an experience: a convention badge, a hosted night at a board game cafe, or a standing commitment to play with them.

### Are card sleeves a good gift?

Yes, as long as you know which game they are for. Dragon Shield matte sleeves run about $8 to $13 per hundred at the time of writing and are the brand most hobbyists use.

### What is a good gift for a Dungeons & Dragons player?

A dice tray, a set of metal or handmade dice, or a miniature of their current character. For a Dungeon Master, a dry-erase battle mat or a session-prep notebook.

### Should I buy a board game expansion as a gift?

Only if you have confirmed they own the base game and do not already own the expansion. Expansions are the most-duplicated gift in the hobby.
