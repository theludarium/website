A Kickstarter board game campaign looks like the obvious path for a designer with a finished prototype. Tabletop projects had raised [close to $700 million](https://www.tabletopgaming.co.uk/?p=1536) on the platform by 2019, some campaigns for millions of dollars each, and the stories of first-time designers who did it are everywhere. What is less visible is the designer who funded, spent a year on fulfillment, and finished with a garage full of boxes and a smaller bank account than when they started.

This article is not an argument against crowdfunding. It is an attempt to lay out what it actually involves, so you decide with clear eyes.

## What does a Kickstarter board game campaign really involve?

Crowdfunding is not a pre-order button. It is three jobs in sequence, each of which is a full-time occupation for a while.

**Before launch:** finishing the design, commissioning art and graphic design, getting manufacturing quotes, building a mailing list, producing a video and page, sending prototypes to reviewers, and running preview coverage. Experienced campaigners spend six to twelve months here.

**During the campaign:** answering comments daily, posting updates, managing stretch goals, handling press, and watching the funding curve with a knot in your stomach. Usually three to four weeks.

**After funding:** collecting money, finalizing files, paying the manufacturer, waiting for production, arranging freight, clearing customs, and shipping to backers through fulfillment partners in several regions. Six to eighteen months, and this is where most projects go wrong.

Designers who have done it describe the campaign itself as the easy part.

## What does it cost?

Figures vary enormously by game size, print run, and component quality, so treat all of these as rough categories rather than quotes.

| Cost category | What it covers | Typical pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Art and graphic design | Illustration, iconography, layout, box | Often the largest pre-launch expense |
| Prototypes and review copies | Short-run printed copies for reviewers and previews | Dozens of copies, each costing far more than the final unit |
| Manufacturing | Printing, components, assembly, packaging | Per-unit cost drops sharply with run size |
| Freight and customs | Shipping pallets from the factory to regional hubs | Volatile; has swung widely in recent years |
| Fulfillment | Warehousing and shipping each copy to a backer | Charged per unit plus storage |
| Platform and payment fees | Kickstarter's cut plus payment processing | [5 percent platform fee plus 3 percent and 30 cents per pledge](https://www.kickstarter.com/help/fees) in the US, so roughly a twelfth of what you raise |
| Marketing | Ads, previews, convention demos | Easy to underestimate; easy to overspend |
| Taxes | Income tax on funds raised, sales tax or VAT in some regions | Frequently forgotten until it is due |

The number that surprises first-timers most is not any single line. It is the gap between the funding goal and the real budget. Many campaigns set a low goal to make funding look certain, then rely on exceeding it. If they barely fund, the designer makes the game at a loss or cancels.

The second surprise is shipping. Backers expect shipping costs that look like retail, and a heavy box to Australia does not cost what a retail order costs. Campaigns that locked in shipping before freight prices moved were badly hurt in 2021, when publishers reported [container costs running three to four times](https://stonemaiergames.com/?p=18664) pre-pandemic levels and some creators [went back to backers for more money](https://www.tabletopgaming.co.uk/news//the-real-costs-of-the-shipping-crisis-in-board-games). Most now collect shipping afterward in a pledge manager.

## Who actually succeeds on Kickstarter?

The tabletop category funds at a higher rate than most of Kickstarter, [almost two-thirds of tabletop campaigns in 2018](https://www.tabletopgaming.co.uk/?p=1536) against [well under half](https://thehustle.co/kickstarter-crowdfunding-chances-of-success) of projects overall, but that figure hides a split. Established publishers with large followings launch campaigns that are effectively pre-orders for games already in production. First-time creators with no audience face much longer odds.

The projects that fund from a standing start share a few traits.

- A game that has been played, blind, by many groups and has visible evidence of it.
- A mailing list built over months before launch, with people who asked to be told when it went live.
- A clear, playable preview in reviewers' hands weeks before launch.
- A sharp hook that explains the game in one sentence.
- A modest, realistic goal and a budget that works at that goal.
- A page that shows the game being played rather than only rendered.

Almost none of this is about the campaign page itself. It is about the year before.

## What are the risks nobody mentions?

Beyond money, three risks deserve attention.

**The fulfillment trap.** Once you have taken money from a thousand people, you owe them a product. A manufacturing delay, a freight spike, or a quality problem is now your crisis, with a thousand public commenters. Some designers have spent years in this phase and sworn off the hobby afterward.

**The design freeze.** Once a campaign launches, the game is done, whether it is or not. If a blind test in month eight reveals a broken strategy, you fix it in a hurry or ship it broken. Our guide to [playtesting a board game](/articles/how-to-playtest-a-board-game/) explains why the testing should be finished long before the campaign is written.

**The opportunity cost.** The eighteen months spent on one campaign are eighteen months not spent designing the next three games. For a designer whose real goal is to design, self-publishing can be a detour.

## What are the alternatives?

Crowdfunding is one route among several, and new designers often skip the comparison.

**Pitch to a publisher.** You give up most of the revenue and most of the control, and in exchange someone else handles art, manufacturing, shipping, and sales. For a first game this is often the better trade. Our article on [how to pitch a board game](/articles/how-to-pitch-a-board-game/) walks through the process.

**Release as print and play.** Zero cost, immediate distribution, and real feedback from real players. A strong print-and-play following is also the best possible evidence to bring to a publisher or a later campaign.

**Small print-on-demand runs.** Services exist that print single copies of card and board games on order. The per-unit cost is high and the quality is middling, but there is no inventory, no shipping pallet, and no fulfillment crisis. For a niche game with a small audience, it may be the right size.

**Other crowdfunding platforms.** Gamefound and similar sites focus on tabletop and bundle pledge-manager tools. The platform matters less than the audience you bring to it.

**Wait.** A second or third design is often better than the first. Many successful campaigners say their first game, the one they almost crowdfunded, was not good enough and they are glad they held it back.

## How do you decide?

A few questions that tend to settle it.

1. Have at least twenty groups you do not know played this game from the rulebook alone and asked to play again? If not, the game is not ready for a campaign regardless of the art.
2. Do you have a list of people who have asked to be told when it launches? If the list is under a few hundred, you are relying on the platform to find your audience, and it usually does not.
3. Can you afford to lose the entire budget? Campaigns fail, and funded campaigns can still lose money on fulfillment.
4. Do you want to run a small publishing company for the next two years? That is what a campaign is. If the honest answer is "no, I want to design games," pitch to a publisher.
5. Would you be proud of this game if it were the only one you ever released? Because for many designers, it is.

If you answered yes to all five, crowdfunding may be right for you, and the work above becomes a checklist. If you hesitated on any, that hesitation is information.

## What does success look like beyond the funding total?

It is tempting to measure a campaign by its total. The designers who seem happiest afterward measure something else: a game that reached tables, backers who played it and told friends, and a reputation that makes the next project easier.

A campaign is one way to get a game in front of people. It is not the only one, and it is not the goal. The goal is a table of people enjoying something you made. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### How much does it cost to Kickstart a board game?

It varies enormously, from a few thousand dollars for a small card game to six figures for a large game with miniatures. Art, manufacturing, freight, and fulfillment are the major lines, and shipping is the most often underestimated; one publisher's [early budgeting walkthrough](https://stonemaiergames.com/?p=495) put a modest 1,000-copy run near $40,000 before shipping.

### What percentage of board game Kickstarters succeed?

The tabletop category has historically funded at a higher rate than Kickstarter overall, [roughly two-thirds versus under four in ten](https://www.tabletopgaming.co.uk/?p=1536), but established publishers account for much of that. First-time creators without an audience fund far less often.

### Should a first-time designer self-publish or pitch to a publisher?

Most experienced designers recommend pitching first. A publisher takes on the cost and risk, and the designer keeps designing. Self-publishing suits people who want to run a small business.

### How long does fulfillment take after a board game Kickstarter?

Typically six to eighteen months from the end of the campaign to delivery, longer if manufacturing or freight is disrupted.
