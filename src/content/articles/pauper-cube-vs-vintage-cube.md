A pauper cube is built only from commons and costs about what a nice dinner for two costs. A vintage cube is built from the most powerful cards ever printed and can cost more than a car, unless you proxy it. They are the two poles of the cube hobby, and the right one for your group depends less on your budget than on what kind of evening you want to have.

I own both. The pauper cube gets drafted more. The vintage cube gets talked about more. Here is how to decide.

## What is a pauper cube?

Pauper is Magic's name for formats restricted to common-rarity cards. A pauper cube is a drafting cube, usually 360 to 540 cards, where every card has been printed at common at least once.

That restriction sounds limiting. In practice it produces one of the best draft environments in the game. Commons are the cards designed to make limited formats work: efficient creatures, clean removal, simple card draw. A pauper cube is Magic with the filler removed and the bombs removed too. What remains is a game decided by curve, combat, and tempo.

Typical features:

- Games are decided by creatures and combat more than by single spells.
- Removal is plentiful and cheap, so no creature is safe.
- Archetypes are classic: flyers, tokens, aggro, control, graveyard.
- Power is flat. The best card in the cube is not that much better than the twentieth best.
- Cost to build is low: about $100 to $150 from singles, often less from a collection.

If you want to build one, [how to build your first cube](/articles/how-to-build-a-cube-mtg/) walks through the process.

## What is a vintage cube?

A vintage cube, sometimes called a powered cube, includes the most powerful cards from the full history of Magic. That means the Power Nine: Black Lotus, the five Moxen, Ancestral Recall, Time Walk, and Timetwister. It usually also means the original dual lands, fast mana, degenerate combo pieces, and the strongest planeswalkers ever printed.

Drafting a vintage cube feels like being handed the keys to the whole game. Turn-one plays can end the game by turn three. Decks reanimate huge creatures on turn two or storm off with a dozen spells in a turn.

Typical features:

- Games can be fast and swingy. Some end before combat matters.
- Archetypes include things no other format allows: Storm, Reanimator, artifact combo, mono-color ramp with Moxen.
- Power is extremely uneven. A pack with a Mox in it is a pack with an obvious first pick.
- Mana fixing is excellent, so three-color decks work.
- Cost to build with real cards runs into the tens of thousands. The list [includes the Power Nine and the original dual lands](https://mtg.wiki/page/Vintage_Cube), and an [Unlimited Black Lotus](https://www.mtggoldfish.com/price/Unlimited+Edition/Black+Lotus) alone listed above $20,000 at the time of writing (October 2026). Most owners proxy some or all of the expensive cards.

The question of proxies deserves its own treatment, which it gets in [proxies, ethics, and playing Magic without going broke](/articles/mtg-proxies-ethics/).

## How do the two compare at the table?

Here is the side-by-side that matters when you are choosing.

| | Pauper cube | Vintage cube |
|---|---|---|
| Cost to build | About $100 to $150 | Tens of thousands, or proxies |
| Typical game length | 10 to 20 minutes | 5 to 25 minutes, high variance |
| What decides games | Combat, tempo, curve | Explosive starts, bombs, combo |
| Draft difficulty | Moderate; picks are close | High ceiling; early picks are obvious, later ones are hard |
| New player friendliness | Excellent | Poor to fair |
| Experienced player excitement | Good | Very high |
| Variance between games | Low | High |
| How often it gets stale | Slowly | Faster; the broken starts repeat |
| Maintenance | Cheap to update with each new set | Expensive or proxy-dependent |

## Which cube is better for new players?

Pauper, and it is not close.

A new player at a pauper cube sees cards that do one thing each. "Deal 3 damage to any target." "Destroy target creature." "Flying." They can read a pack and understand their options. The games they play are decided by choices they can see: whether to attack, whether to block, when to cast the removal.

A new player at a vintage cube sees Time Walk and does not know why it is good. They get killed on turn three by a reanimated creature and never got to cast a spell. They conclude, reasonably, that the game is not for them. Why that matters is the subject of [why cube is the best way to teach Magic](/articles/how-to-teach-magic-the-gathering/).

If your group mixes experienced and new players, build or borrow a pauper or peasant cube first.

## Which cube is better for experienced players?

Here is where it gets interesting. Vintage cube is the one experienced players ask for by name. It is Magic's greatest hits. Drafting a Black Lotus, even a proxy, is an event. The decks you build do things you cannot do anywhere else.

But experienced players who draft both regularly often say the pauper cube produces better games. Decisions matter more because no single card dominates. Comebacks are possible. The deck you drafted well beats the deck that got lucky.

The honest split, in my experience, is this. Vintage cube is a better spectacle. Pauper cube is a better game. Which your group wants on a given night varies, and that is a good argument for owning or borrowing both.

## What about the middle ground?

The two poles are not the only options. Several cube styles sit between them, and one of them may fit your group better than either extreme.

- **Peasant cube.** Commons and uncommons. A noticeable step up in power from pauper, with real build-around cards and the occasional bomb, still cheap to build. The most common recommendation for a first cube.
- **Budget legacy cube.** Rares and mythics included, but nothing from the reserved list or above a price ceiling the owner sets. Powerful games without the famous price tags.
- **Unpowered legacy cube.** Everything but the Power Nine and a few other broken cards. Most of the vintage feel at a fraction of the variance.
- **Set cube or themed cube.** All the cards from one beloved set, or a cube built around a mechanic. Power and cost depend on the theme.

A group that drafts every week often ends up with a pauper or peasant cube for regular nights and a powered cube for occasions.

## How do you decide for your group?

Four questions settle it.

1. **Who is at the table?** More than two new players, go pauper or peasant. All veterans, either works.
2. **How often will it be drafted?** Weekly, pauper or peasant holds up longer. Monthly, vintage stays special.
3. **How does the group feel about proxies?** If nobody minds, a vintage cube costs the price of printing. If some people do mind, vintage is off the table for most budgets.
4. **What do you want games to feel like?** Grindy, tactical, decided in combat: pauper. Explosive, dramatic, decided by big plays: vintage.

Whichever you pick, the hosting side is the same, and [how to run a cube night](/articles/how-to-run-a-cube-draft/) covers it.

## FAQ

### What is a pauper cube?

A pauper cube is a Magic draft cube built only from cards that have been printed at common rarity. It is cheap to build and plays as a tight, combat-focused format.

### What is a vintage cube?

A vintage cube includes the most powerful cards in Magic's history, including the Power Nine and original dual lands. It is expensive unless proxied and produces fast, high-variance games.

### Is pauper cube good for beginners?

Yes. Commons do simple things, games are decided by visible choices, and no single card dominates, which makes it the best teaching environment in cube.

### Which cube should I build first?

Most owners should build a pauper or peasant cube first. It is cheap, it gets drafted often, and it teaches you what you want from a second, more powerful cube.
