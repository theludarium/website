You can make your own board game with index cards, a pen, and a few borrowed dice. Start with the experience you want players to have, pick one or two mechanics that create it, and build an ugly paper prototype you can play this week. Then playtest it, fix what breaks, and play it again. Art, rulebooks, and publishing all come later, and most of them come much later than new designers expect.

**The quick answer:** decide what the game should feel like, build the smallest version that delivers that feeling, play it alone, then with friends, then with strangers. Write the rules once the game stops changing every session. Choose a path to publishing only after people who owe you nothing ask to play it again.

This guide is the map. Each step links to a deeper Ludarium article when you are ready to go further.

## Where do board game ideas come from?

Almost anywhere. Some games begin with a theme: "I want a game about running a bird sanctuary." Some begin with a mechanic: "What if your deck was a bag of tokens?" Some begin with a component, like a tile with a road on it, or a single interesting card. None of these starting points is the right one. Our piece on [theme vs mechanics](/articles/theme-vs-mechanics/) looks at how well-known games began from each side.

What matters more than the source is turning the idea into a sentence about the player. Try this:

> In this game, players will feel ___ because they have to ___.

"Players will feel clever because they have to plan three turns ahead with limited actions." "Players will feel nervous because they have to decide when to stop drawing." That sentence is your north star. Every later decision, from scoring to components, gets checked against it.

Write the sentence before you write any rules. If you can't fill in the blanks, keep playing with the idea until you can.

## How do you design a board game around the player experience?

Board game design is mostly the craft of building a feeling out of rules. Three questions shape that feeling early.

### Who is at the table?

Families, two-player couples, a heavy-strategy group, a party of eight? Decide now, because it changes everything: length, complexity, how much players can hurt each other. Our guide to [player count design](/articles/player-count-design/) explains why a game that sings at four can fall apart at two or six.

### How long should it take?

A twenty-minute filler and a three-hour epic are different products. Pick a target and hold it. If your playtests keep running long, the game is telling you something, and our piece on [why box play times run short](/articles/board-game-play-time-accuracy/) is worth a read before you print a number on anything.

### What is the core loop?

The core loop is what a player does on a typical turn, over and over. Take an action, gain something, spend it, score. If you can describe your loop in one breath, you have a game to prototype. If it takes a paragraph, cut until it doesn't.

## How do you choose mechanics for your board game?

Mechanics are the reusable parts of game design: worker placement, deck building, drafting, tile placement, and dozens more. You don't need to invent a new one. Most great games combine familiar mechanics in a fresh way, or point them at a theme nobody tried.

Pick mechanics that serve your sentence. Want tension over shared space? Look at [worker placement](/articles/worker-placement-explained/) or [area control](/articles/area-control-board-games/). Want a sense of growth? Try [engine building](/articles/engine-building-games/) or [deck building](/articles/deck-building-games-explained/). Want quick, social decisions? [Card drafting](/articles/card-drafting-explained/) might fit. A [roll and write](/articles/roll-and-write-games/) is one of the cheapest formats to prototype at all.

For a tour of the common ones, with a good first game for each, see our field guide to [board game mechanics explained](/articles/board-game-mechanics-explained/). Then pick one main mechanic and, at most, one supporting mechanic. Restraint here saves months.

Two design questions are worth settling early because they shape every mechanic you add:

- **How much luck?** Dice and card draws create drama and keep new players in the game. Too much, and choices stop mattering. Our pieces on [luck vs skill](/articles/luck-vs-skill-board-games/) and [balance math](/articles/board-game-balance-math/) help you tune it.
- **What happens to the player who falls behind?** A game where the leader runs away by turn three is no fun for anyone else. Our guide to [catch-up mechanics](/articles/catch-up-mechanics-board-games/) covers the tools designers use to keep a game close.

## How do you build a board game prototype?

Fast and ugly. A board game prototype exists to be played and changed, not admired. Index cards, a marker, a sheet of paper for the board, and pieces borrowed from games you already own will carry you a long way. Do not open a graphics program yet.

Build only what you need for one full game. Ten cards that test your core loop beat a hundred cards that test nothing yet. Then play it alone, taking every seat, until it reaches an ending. That solo run will break half your ideas, which is exactly what it is for.

Our step-by-step guide on [how to prototype a board game](/articles/how-to-prototype-a-board-game/) walks through a full weekend, from core loop on Saturday morning to first playtest on Sunday. If you want to share your prototype widely later, a [print and play](/articles/print-and-play-board-games/) version lets anyone test it for the cost of paper.

## How do you playtest a board game?

Playtesting is where a game is actually designed. Everything before it is a guess.

Start with friends, but don't trust their verdicts. Friends are kind, and they will tell you it was fun. Watch what they do instead: where they hesitate, where they lean in, where they reach for their phones. Those moments are honest. Our guide on [how to playtest a board game](/articles/how-to-playtest-a-board-game/) explains what to watch, what to ask, and what to ignore.

Then move to blind playtests: people who have never met you learning the game from your written rules, while you sit silently and take notes. This is humbling and essential. Every place they stumble is a place your rules or your design needs work.

Designers also test each other's games. Protospiel is a network of playtesting events held in cities around the US, including Indianapolis, where designers bring prototypes and play each other's in return. Local design groups and game stores often run smaller versions. Our guide to [board game conventions in the Midwest](/articles/board-game-conventions-midwest/) covers some options in the region.

A simple rule for revisions: change one thing at a time, and play at least twice before you decide whether it worked.

## When should you write the board game rulebook?

Once the game stops changing every session. Before that, keep a short rules sheet, a page or two, and update it after each test. Writing a polished rulebook for a game that is still moving is wasted effort.

When it is time, write it in the order a new player needs it: goal, components, setup, a turn, the end, scoring. Add examples. Then hand it to a blind playtest group and watch where they get stuck. Our guide on [how to write a board game rulebook](/articles/how-to-write-a-board-game-rulebook/) goes deep on structure and testing, and our evergreen guide to [learning any game from its rulebook](/articles/how-to-learn-a-board-game/) shows the problem from the reader's side.

## When should you add art to your board game?

Later than you think. If you plan to pitch to a publisher, you usually don't need final art at all; publishers typically bring their own artists and graphic designers. Clear graphic design matters more than illustration in a prototype: readable text, consistent icons, colors that tell pieces apart.

If you plan to self-publish, art becomes a major cost and a major part of what sells the game. Even then, commission it only after the design is locked, so you never pay to redraw a card you cut. Our piece on [board game components and table presence](/articles/board-game-components-table-presence/) covers how the physical game helps sell itself.

## How do you publish a board game you made?

There are three common paths, and each suits a different designer.

### Pitch it to a publisher

You license the design; the publisher pays for art, manufacturing, and distribution and pays you a royalty or fee. You keep designing instead of running a business. Many publishers take submissions, and conventions like Gen Con host pitch meetings. Start with our guides on [how to pitch a board game](/articles/how-to-pitch-a-board-game/) and [pitching a game at Gen Con](/articles/pitch-board-game-at-gen-con/).

### Crowdfund it

Platforms like Kickstarter and Gamefound let you raise money from backers before you manufacture. You become a publisher: art, manufacturing, freight, and shipping are your problems. Our sober look at [running a Kickstarter board game campaign](/articles/kickstarter-board-game/) lays out the real costs and risks before you commit.

### Self-publish small

Print-on-demand services such as The Game Crafter print games one copy at a time, with no minimum order. That makes them useful both for polished prototypes and for selling a small game without a print run. Free print and play files are another way to build an audience before anything else.

Whichever path you choose, the game has to be tested and loved first. That is the work.

## Do you need to protect your board game idea?

Most new designers worry about this more than they need to. The US Copyright Office says copyright does not protect the idea for a game, its name, or the method of playing it. It can protect the specific written rules text and the artwork. In practice, publishers see many submissions and have little reason to steal a prototype. Your real protection is being the person who can finish it. This is general information, not legal advice; talk to a lawyer if you have a specific concern.

## FAQ

### How do I make my own board game at home?

Start with index cards, paper, a pen, and pieces borrowed from games you own. Build the smallest playable version of your core idea, play it alone to find the obvious problems, then test it with friends. You don't need software or a printer to begin.

### How long does it take to design a board game?

It varies widely. A light card game can reach a solid prototype in weeks. A large strategy game can take years of playtesting before it is ready to pitch or publish. Plan for many more test sessions than feel necessary.

### What is the first step in board game design?

Write one sentence describing what players will feel and why. Then design the core loop, the action a player repeats every turn, and build a prototype that tests only that.

### Do I need art to pitch a board game?

Usually not. Publishers generally commission their own art. Clean, readable graphic design on your prototype matters more than illustrations.

### Can I make money making board games?

Some designers do, through royalties, crowdfunding, or their own small publishing. Most first games don't earn much. Treat your first design as a way to learn the craft, and let income come from later, stronger games.
