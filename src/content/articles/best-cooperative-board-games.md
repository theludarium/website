The best cooperative board games solve a problem that competitive games create. Someone at your table does not like losing, and someone else does not like beating them. A co-op puts everyone on one side, so the game becomes the opponent and the friendships stay out of it.

This list is for that table. Fifteen games, sorted roughly from lightest to heaviest, with a note on who each one fits. Every game here is real, widely available, and has been played by thousands of groups. Rules are described at a general level; check the box for exact player counts and times.

## What makes a cooperative board game good?

Three things, in our experience.

1. **A real chance of losing.** A co-op you always beat is a group activity, not a game. The good ones win maybe half the time at a fair difficulty.
2. **Decisions that belong to individuals.** If one experienced player can dictate every move, the rest are spectators. The best designs give each person information or cards nobody else can see.
3. **A clear story of why you lost.** "The outbreak chain in [Pandemic](https://link.amazon/B0eHP5GCg) got us" is a story you tell on the drive home. "We ran out of points" is not.

If you want to understand the second point in depth, we wrote about the [quarterback problem in cooperative games](/articles/quarterback-problem-cooperative-games/) and why designers work so hard to avoid it.

## The 15 games

### 1. Just One

A party co-op for five to seven people. One player guesses a secret word; everyone else writes a one-word clue, but duplicate clues are removed before the guesser sees them. Teaches in one minute. Great opener.

### 2. The Mind

A deck of numbered cards. Play them in ascending order across all players' hands without speaking. That is the whole game. It sounds like nothing and plays like a group meditation with occasional panic.

### 3. Forbidden Island

The gentlest entry point to the Pandemic family. Collect four treasures from a sinking island and escape before the tiles disappear. Short, cheap, and good for children from about age eight alongside adults.

### 4. Hanabi

You hold your cards facing away from you, so everyone sees your hand except you. Clues are limited. You are building fireworks in sequence. [Hanabi](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01MXTGT21?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) is the purest hidden-information co-op ever made and it is the reason the genre moved past "talk it out."

### 5. The Crew: Mission Deep Sea

A trick-taking game where the team must win specific tricks in specific ways, mission after mission, with almost no talking allowed. We think it is the best teaching game ever published, and we said so at length in our [guide to The Crew](/articles/the-crew-card-game/).

### 6. Codenames: Duet

The two-player (or two-team) version of Codenames. Both sides give one-word clues to find agents on a shared grid before the time runs out. Excellent for couples, and it scales up with a house rule or two.

### 7. Pandemic

The one that made the genre mainstream. Four diseases, a world map, and a team of specialists trying to cure them before outbreaks cascade. Still the standard by which co-ops are measured, and still worth owning.

### 8. Horrified

Classic movie monsters attack a town; the players defend it. Each monster has its own defeat condition, so Dracula plays differently from the Mummy. Lighter than Pandemic and easier to teach to a mixed family table.

### 9. Flash Point: Fire Rescue

Firefighters rescue people from a burning building that spreads unpredictably. The rescue theme lands with almost everyone, and the family rules are a fair fight for new players while the advanced rules satisfy veterans.

### 10. Mysterium

One player is a ghost who can only communicate through dreamlike art cards. The others are psychics trying to identify a murderer. The ghost cannot speak, so the quarterback problem never appears. Beautiful on the table.

### 11. Sky Team

Two players land an airliner by placing dice on a shared cockpit board, with no talking during the dice phase. Tense, short, and one of the best two-player co-ops we have seen.

### 12. Burgle Bros

Pull off a heist across three floors of a building without being caught by patrolling guards. Each player has a specialist role, and the guard movement is just visible enough to plan around and just random enough to punish arrogance.

### 13. Robinson Crusoe: Adventures on the Cursed Island

Castaways survive on an island through a scripted scenario. Heavy, punishing, and story-rich. For groups who want a long evening and do not mind losing to the weather.

### 14. Spirit Island

Players are spirits defending an island from colonizers. Complex powers, deep planning, and a difficulty that climbs as far as you want it to. Widely considered the heavy co-op for people who found Pandemic too simple.

### 15. Gloomhaven

The campaign co-op, with dozens of scenarios, character retirement, and a box you will need two hands for. Not for everyone, and we wrote an [honest guide to whether Gloomhaven is worth it](/articles/is-gloomhaven-worth-it/) for busy adults.

## How do you pick a first co-op for a competitive group?

Start lighter than you think. Competitive players often assume they want a heavy challenge, then spend the first game arguing about the rules instead of the enemy. Just One or Forbidden Island gets the habit of shared decisions started without friction.

Then move to something with hidden information. Hanabi or The Crew forces each person to own their choices. That is where the group learns to trust each other instead of deferring to the loudest voice.

Save Pandemic for the third or fourth session. By then your group has a shared language for "I think we should" versus "you have to," and the game will be a proper fight.

## Do cooperative games actually reduce arguments?

Mostly, yes. The arguments move from "you took my spot" to "should we have cured blue first." The second kind is easier to leave at the table because nobody was wronged.

There is one exception. If one player takes over, the resentment is worse than in a competitive game, because the others cannot even play against them. Watch for it, name it kindly, and pick games that structurally prevent it.

## FAQ

### What is the best cooperative board game for beginners?

Forbidden Island for families and Pandemic for adults. Both teach in under ten minutes and lose often enough to feel like a real game.

### Are there cooperative board games for two players?

Yes. Sky Team, Codenames: Duet, and Hanabi all play well at two, and most of the Pandemic family scales down to two without losing much.

### Can you play cooperative board games solo?

Many of them. Spirit Island, Burgle Bros, Horrified, and Gloomhaven all include solo rules, and some players prefer them that way.

### How do you make a co-op harder once you win every time?

Most co-ops ship with difficulty settings, usually by adding threat cards or removing resources. Pandemic uses the number of epidemic cards; Spirit Island uses adversary levels.
