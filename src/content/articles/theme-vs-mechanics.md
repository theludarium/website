The theme vs mechanics debate in board game design has been running for as long as hobbyists have argued on forums, and it is mostly a false fight. Designers have started great games from a theme, from a mechanism, from a single component, and from a feeling they wanted a table to have. The useful question is not which should come first but what each is for, and how to tell when the two have stopped talking to each other.

This article looks at what theme and mechanics actually do, how several well-known games began, and how to choose a starting point for your own.

## What do theme and mechanics each do?

**Mechanics** are the rules: what you can do on your turn, how pieces move, how points are scored. They determine what decisions the game offers.

**Theme** is the story the game tells about those rules: you are a bird enthusiast, a plague doctor, a Viking, a tile-layer in a Portuguese palace. It determines how players understand the decisions.

The common framing treats them as rivals. The better framing treats theme as an interface. A good theme makes a mechanism easier to learn, because the rule matches an intuition the player already has. A bad theme makes it harder, because the rule contradicts the story.

Pandemic is the clearest example. The rule "a city with three cubes that receives a fourth triggers an outbreak into every neighboring city" would be hard to teach as abstract math. As a disease spreading, nobody needs it explained twice. The theme is doing the teaching.

## How do real designers start?

The record shows every possible starting point.

| Game | What came first | How the other half followed |
|---|---|---|
| [Pandemic](https://link.amazon/B0eHP5GCg) | Theme: a cooperative game about fighting disease | Cubes spreading across a map followed from the story |
| [Azul](https://link.amazon/B0bGRYHex) | A mechanism: drafting tiles from shared factories | Portuguese azulejo tiles were chosen afterward for beauty |
| [Dominion](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01LYLIS2U?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) | A mechanism: a deck that builds itself | The medieval kingdom theme was added and is lightly held |
| [Root](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07F454YF3?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) | A feeling: asymmetric factions at war in one forest | Each faction's rules were built to express its character |
| [Wingspan](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07YQ641NQ?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) | Theme: the designer's love of birds | Engine-building was chosen because it suited the subject |
| [Codenames](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B014Q1XX9S?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) | A mechanism: one-word clues linking several targets | Spies and agents arrived as a thin layer for flavor |
| [Ticket to Ride](https://link.amazon/B0jh8RB7f) | A mechanism and a constraint: a simple train game anyone could learn | The route map gave the mechanism its tension |

Note the range. Azul and Dominion are lightly themed and nobody cares, because their mechanisms are so clean. Pandemic and Root are deeply themed and the theme is load-bearing. Both approaches produced classics.

What they share is fit. In each case the theme and the mechanism ended up pointing the same direction, whichever came first.

## What does a pasted-on theme look like?

Players use "pasted on" for a theme that does not change how the game feels. Swap the art and nobody would notice. That is not always a problem. Azul could be about any tiles, and it is still beautiful. But a pasted-on theme becomes a problem in three situations.

**When the theme promises something the mechanics do not deliver.** A game about pirates where you never board a ship, or a horror game where nothing is ever at risk, disappoints players who came for the promise.

**When the theme makes rules harder to remember.** If the "farming" action produces stone and the "mining" action produces wheat, players will get it wrong every time. The theme is fighting the rule.

**When the theme does work the mechanics should be doing.** Lavish art and flavor text can hide a decision system that is thin. Players feel it by the third game.

The test is simple. Describe the game without naming the theme. Then describe it without naming any mechanics. If the first description is complete and the second is empty, the theme is decoration. That might be fine. Just know it.

## Why do themes help new players more than veterans?

Experienced gamers often say they do not care about theme, and they half mean it. They have learned hundreds of rulesets and can absorb a new one abstractly. New players cannot. For them, theme is the handrail.

This matters a great deal for anyone designing for a wide audience. A gateway game with a strong, intuitive theme is teachable in five minutes because the teacher can say "you are collecting train cards to claim routes" and the player already understands most of the game. Our article on [how to teach a board game](/articles/how-to-teach-a-board-game/) covers how far a good theme carries the teach.

The lesson for designers is not to pick a dramatic theme. It is to pick one where the rules you need are rules the player already expects.

## How do you know when theme and mechanics have come apart?

Signs show up in testing.

- Players keep asking "why" about a rule. The theme is not explaining it.
- Players use their own words for actions instead of yours. Your names do not match what the action feels like.
- Players are surprised by what wins. The theme told them one thing mattered; the scoring says another.
- Players describe the experience in terms that have nothing to do with the theme. "It is a set-collection game" rather than "we were building a zoo."
- The flavor text is the only place the theme appears.

When you see these, the fix can go either way. Sometimes a rule changes to match the story. Sometimes the story changes to match the rule. Wingspan's designer has described adjusting bird powers so that what the card does matches what the real bird does, which made both the theme stronger and the cards easier to remember.

## How should you choose your own starting point?

Three honest questions.

**What excites you enough to work on for two years?** A game takes a long time to finish. If the mechanism bores you after a month, no theme will save it. If the theme is a passing interest, the mechanics will have to carry it alone.

**What do you already know?** A designer who understands trick-taking deeply should start there and find the theme that fits. A designer who knows beekeeping should start with the bees and find the mechanism that captures what makes beekeeping interesting.

**What is the one feeling you want players to have?** Dread, triumph, delight, tension, laughter. Name it. Then ask whether a theme or a mechanism gets you closer, and start from that side.

Whichever you choose, start the other side early. A mechanism-first designer should pick a provisional theme by the second prototype, because the theme will reveal rules that feel wrong. A theme-first designer should get a playable loop on the table by the second week, because an unplayed theme is a novel, not a game. ## What about abstract games?

Some games have no theme at all. Chess has a faint one, Go has none, and plenty of modern abstracts like [Hive](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000EN5ZLW?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) or [Santorini](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DWTZ1313?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) wear theirs lightly. Nobody argues that these are worse for it.

What abstracts give up in teachability they regain in elegance. Every rule has to justify itself because there is no story to lean on. Designers who work on abstracts for a while often find their themed games get tighter too, because they have learned to cut any rule that only exists for the story.

## Why does the argument persist?

Because people are describing their own preferences and calling them principles. Some players love being transported. Some love a clean puzzle. Both are right about what they enjoy and wrong about what every game should be.

A designer's job is not to settle the argument. It is to make the theme and the mechanics agree, so that a table of mixed tastes can sit down together and each find something to love. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### Should I design the theme or the mechanics first?

Start wherever your energy is, then bring the other side in early. Neither order is correct; what matters is that the two end up reinforcing each other.

### What is a pasted-on theme?

A theme that could be swapped for another without changing how the game plays or feels. It is only a problem when it misleads players or makes rules harder to remember.

### Do board game players care about theme?

New players rely on it heavily to learn. Experienced players say they care less, but they still notice when a theme fights the rules.

### Can a game have no theme?

Yes. Abstract games like Go, Hive, and Santorini have little or no theme and succeed on the clarity of their rules alone.
