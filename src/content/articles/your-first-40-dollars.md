The first board game to buy is not the best game you have ever played. It is the one your people will actually sit down to most often. We call it the anchor: one game, about $40, chosen to fit the table you really have rather than the table you imagine. Get this one right and everything you add later has something to attach to.

This is the second article in Building Your Library on Purpose, our series on growing a collection from $0 to a lifetime library. If you have not spent your [$0 months playing first](/articles/play-before-you-pay/), start there. The anchor is chosen from a taste log, not a wish list. The full shelf plan lives in [The Starter Shelf](/articles/starter-shelf-40-to-300/).

## What makes a board game a good anchor?

An anchor does one job: it gets played. Not admired, not displayed, played. Four qualities predict that better than anything else.

**It fits your most common player count.** Not your biggest night of the year. The number of people who are at your table on an ordinary week. If that is two, an anchor that peaks at five is a mistake, no matter how good it is.

**You can teach it in 10 minutes.** The anchor will be the game you reach for when someone new is over. A 10-minute teach means it hits the table instead of staying in the box while you explain.

**It survives repeat plays.** You will play this game 20, 30, 50 times. Variable setups, hidden information, or enough decisions that no two games feel alike all help. A game that is fun once and solved by the third play cannot anchor anything.

**You would be proud to bring it.** When your group meets at someone else's house, this is the box under your arm. It should feel like a contribution, not an apology.

Notice that "my favorite" is not on the list. Your favorite might be a three-hour engine builder you adore. That can come later. The anchor is for the table, not for you.

## A scoring rubric for your first board game to buy

Pull up your taste log and score each candidate on the four qualities. Use one to five. Be honest about the player count; it is the one people fudge most.

| Quality | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fits my usual player count | Only works at a count I rarely have | Works at my count but better elsewhere | Shines at exactly my usual count |
| Teach time | 30+ minutes, rules questions all game | 15 to 20 minutes, a few lookups | Under 10 minutes, first-timers competing by turn three |
| Replayability | Solved after a few plays | Fun for a dozen plays | Fresh every time; players want to try new strategies |
| Pride factor | I would hide it | Fine | I would bring it anywhere and teach it to anyone |

A score of 16 or higher is anchor material. Anything under 12 is a later purchase, not a first one. If two games tie, choose the one more people in your group have already asked to play again. That request is the strongest signal you will get.

One more check before you buy: does anyone in your group already own it? If so, the [One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/) applies. Their copy serves the group already. Pick your anchor from the games your group loves but nobody owns, and you have taken the first step toward [claiming a lane](/articles/claim-a-lane/).

## Anchor candidates by situation

The right anchor depends almost entirely on who sits at your table. These are examples, not rankings, and each one is a well-known title that typically lands in the $25 to $45 range.

### Couples and regular two-player nights

Two players is the most common table in the country and the most under-served by big-box games. Look for games built for two or that scale down cleanly.

- **Patchwork**, a quilt-building puzzle for exactly two, with a 15-minute teach.
- **Jaipur**, a quick trading duel that plays in about 20 minutes.
- **Hive**, an abstract with no board, portable enough for a coffee shop.
- **Love Letter**, a tiny bluffing game that also stretches to four when company comes over.

### Families with kids

You want a game that an eight-year-old can play without a handicap and a parent can enjoy without pretending.

- **Ticket to Ride**, route building that kids grasp in one round and adults keep thinking about.
- **Sushi Go!**, card drafting in 15 minutes with pictures of sushi; a near-perfect first game.
- **Azul**, tile placement that is beautiful, quick, and scales from two to four.
- **Carcassonne**, tile laying where the map grows every turn, easy to teach and gentle on competitiveness.

### A friend group of four or five

This is the classic game night. You want tension, table talk, and a game that runs under 90 minutes so you can fit two in.

- **Catan**, trading and building; still the standard gateway for good reason, and about $40.
- **Ticket to Ride**, also excellent here, with a little more edge at five.
- **Splendor**, chip-collecting engine building with a five-minute teach.
- **Dominion**, deck building; endlessly replayable, and the base box is enough for years.

### Big parties of six or more

If your usual gathering is a crowd, your anchor should be a party game, and it should work with people who do not think of themselves as gamers.

- **Codenames**, word clues in teams, scales to a whole room.
- **Just One**, cooperative word guessing where everyone writes a clue; impossible to lose friends over.
- **Wavelength**, a guessing game on a spectrum that gets a room arguing happily.

## What not to buy first

Some games are wonderful and still wrong as a first purchase. Three categories cause most of the regret we see.

**Heavy strategy games.** Terraforming Mars, Spirit Island, Twilight Imperium. Long teaches, long plays, and a table that needs everyone bought in. They are great third or fourth lane purchases once your group has a rhythm. As an anchor they turn game night into homework.

**Campaign games.** Gloomhaven and its cousins need the same group, repeatedly, over months. If your group is still forming, you will stall at scenario four and the box becomes a [monument on the Shelf of Shame](/articles/shelf-of-shame-infinite-cost/).

**Niche games.** Anything you love for a reason your group does not share. A war game, a deep deck builder, a licensed game for a show only you watch. Buy it later when you know it will reach the table. Your anchor is a group purchase that happens to live at your house.

**Anything with a Kickstarter timeline.** You cannot anchor a shelf on a box that arrives in 14 months. Save the [Kickstarter question](/articles/kickstarter-question/) for the curator stage.

## How to spend the $40 well

A few practical habits make the first purchase go further.

1. **Buy the base game only.** No expansions, no upgrade kits, no sleeves. See whether it earns a second purchase first. [Expansions are worth it](/articles/expansions-worth-it/) only once a game has proven itself with your group.
2. **Consider used.** A well-kept used copy often runs 30 to 50 percent below retail, and a gateway game with 20 plays on it is proof it holds up. Our guide to [buying used board games](/articles/how-to-buy-used-board-games/) covers what to check.
3. **Log the first play.** Keep the taste log going. Note the real length, the count, and who asked to play again. This becomes your cost-per-play record, and it will tell you when the anchor has earned company.
4. **Tell your group.** Say out loud, "I have Catan now." That one sentence is the start of a [Shared Shelf](/articles/one-copy-rule/). It means nobody else needs to buy it.

If you do all four, your first $40 buys a game that gets played 30 times in its first year. In illustration, that is about $1.33 per play before you divide by the four people at the table. Nothing else you spend money on this year will come close.

## What the anchor sets up

Once the anchor is in place, you have a reference point. Everything you add afterward is chosen because it is different from the anchor: shorter, or for a different count, or cooperative where the anchor is competitive. That is how a shelf of six ends up covering nearly every occasion, and it is the subject of the next article.

The anchor also tells you something about yourself. If the game you chose was a party game, you are probably the host in your circle. If it was a two-player game, your library will grow around your partner. Either way, the anchor is the first entry in a lifetime library, and it deserves a little ceremony. Teach it well the first time.

## FAQ

### What is the best first board game to buy?

The best first board game is the one that fits your most common player count, teaches in under 10 minutes, stays fresh over dozens of plays, and that nobody in your group already owns. For a group of four or five, Catan or Ticket to Ride are common anchors. For couples, Patchwork or Jaipur. For parties, Codenames. Choose from games you have already played and enjoyed.

### How much should I spend on my first board game?

About $40. Most well-regarded gateway games retail between $25 and $45, and Catan's base game lists at roughly $40 at major retailers. Spending more on a first purchase usually means buying a heavier game that is harder to get to the table. Buy the base game only and let it prove itself before you add expansions or a second title.

### Should I buy a board game my friend already owns?

Not as your first purchase. A play group needs one copy of a game, not one per household, because that copy already serves everyone at the table. Spend your first $40 on a game your group enjoys but nobody owns yet. That adds a new game to the group's shared shelf instead of duplicating one that is already there.

### What board games should a beginner avoid buying first?

Avoid heavy strategy games with long teaches, campaign games that require the same group for months, niche games your friends do not share an interest in, and crowdfunded games that will not arrive for a year. All of these can be excellent later purchases. As a first game they tend to sit unplayed because they need a settled group that a new collector does not yet have.
