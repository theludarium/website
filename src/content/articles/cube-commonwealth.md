Cube Commonwealth is a Magic: The Gathering drafting community built around a single shared cube. One owner, one box of cards, a group of people who come back to draft it, and a set of habits that have turned a card game into something closer to a standing dinner party. This is the story of how it works, what we got wrong early, and why we think one stack of cards is a better foundation for a community than a thousand individual collections.

It is also, in a quiet way, the prototype for everything The Ludarium wants to build. Before there was a lounge idea there was a cube and eight chairs.

## Why build a community around a cube at all?

Most Magic communities form around a store or a format. Store communities depend on the store. Format communities depend on everyone keeping up with the format, which means buying cards. Both have a cost of entry that rises over time, and both tend to lose the players who cannot or will not keep spending.

A cube community has a different shape. The cards belong to one person. Everyone else shows up empty-handed and leaves empty-handed. There is nothing to keep up with. The card pool changes only when the owner changes it, and the owner announces the changes.

That does three things for the group.

**It removes money from the table.** Nobody is behind. Nobody is flexing. A fifteen-year-old with no collection and a forty-five-year-old with a vault full of duals have the same forty-five picks.

**It creates shared memory.** Everyone drafts the same cards for years. Specific cards acquire reputations. "The elf" means a particular elf. A good card stolen at pick six is remembered the following month.

**It makes the owner a host rather than a player.** The person with the cube is responsible for the evening going well, which changes how they play and what they value. The cube is the reason people come, so the owner starts caring about the people more than the matches.

If the format itself is new to you, [what a Magic cube is](/articles/what-is-a-magic-cube/) explains the mechanics.

## How did Cube Commonwealth start?

The way most of these things start: a cube, a few friends, and a Thursday that kept happening.

The first version was a budget cube and a kitchen table. The group was whoever answered the text. Some nights it was four people and a Winston draft. Some nights it was eight and the kitchen was too small. The cube got better slowly, a few swaps at a time, and the group got more regular at about the same pace.

The name came later, when it stopped feeling like one person's game night and started feeling like a shared thing. "Commonwealth" was the right word. The cube belongs to one person on paper and to everyone in practice. Everyone has opinions about what should be in it. Everyone has a card they lobby for and a card they want gone.

## What does a Cube Commonwealth night look like?

The structure is close to what we describe in [how to run a cube night](/articles/how-to-run-a-cube-draft/), with a few habits of our own.

- **Packs are dealt before anyone arrives.** The owner's job starts an hour early. Players sit down to a pack already in front of them.
- **Seating is drawn, not chosen.** Names from a cup. It stops the same two people from fighting over blue every week.
- **The change log is read aloud.** Before pack one, the owner announces what came in and what went out since last time, and takes heckling about it.
- **Draft, build, play two or three rounds.** Pairings are random for the first round and Swiss after. No prizes. There is a trophy of sorts, which is a running joke that has outlasted most of the cards.
- **The debrief.** Five minutes at the end. What did you draft, what would you do differently, what card should be cut. Half the cube's changes come out of this conversation.
- **End on time.** The night ends when it was supposed to end, even when people want one more round. Especially then.

New players get a slower first draft, a veteran on their right, and open-handed games in round one. We wrote about why that works in [why cube is the best way to teach Magic](/articles/how-to-teach-magic-the-gathering/).

## What did we get wrong early on?

Several things, and the mistakes are more useful than the successes.

**We let the cube get too powerful too fast.** Early on the owner chased power. The cube got more exciting and the games got worse for anyone who was not a strong player. New players bounced. We stepped the power level back down and the group grew again.

**We did not fix the night.** "Whenever we can get eight" produced a lot of weeks with five. Picking a fixed cadence and sticking to it, even when turnout was thin, is what made it a habit instead of an event.

**We tolerated slow drafting for too long.** One player taking ninety seconds a pick turns a forty-five-minute draft into seventy. Setting a gentle norm fixed it in one week and nobody was offended.

**We did not announce changes.** The owner swapped cards quietly and players felt like the cube was shifting under them. The change log solved it, and turned out to be one of the most-loved parts of the evening.

**We played too many rounds.** Four rounds felt proper. It also ended at midnight on a work night. Two or three rounds and a hard stop made people more willing to come back.

## What makes a shared cube hold a group together?

Here is what we think is going on, as plainly as we can say it.

A group needs a reason to gather and a thing to gather around. The reason can be weak; "it's Thursday" is enough once the habit exists. But the thing needs to be shared, concrete, and slightly alive.

A cube is all three. It is shared because nobody owns the night's deck, only the owner owns the box, and even the owner takes suggestions. It is concrete because it is a physical object that sits in the middle of the table and gets passed hand to hand. It is alive because it changes a little every time, and everyone has a say in how.

Compare that with a group that meets to play Commander with their own decks. The decks are private property. The power levels drift. The politics, as we covered in [the Commander problem](/articles/commander-politics/), can turn personal. There is no shared object, only a shared room.

The cube gives the group something to be loyal to that is not any one person. That turns out to matter a lot when the organizer has a bad month or someone moves away. The cube keeps going.

| | Shared cube group | Individual-collection group |
|---|---|---|
| Cost to join | Zero | A deck or collection |
| What changes over time | The cube, visibly, by group input | Each player's deck, privately |
| Shared object | Yes, the box | No |
| Power level | Set by the owner for the group | Drifts with each player's spending |
| When a player is away | Nothing changes | Their deck and their table are gone |
| Who is responsible for the night | The owner, as host | Nobody in particular |

## How does this connect to The Ludarium?

The Ludarium is a game lounge venture starting in Indianapolis, built on the idea that adults need a place to play together regularly, the way coworking spaces gave remote workers a place to work together. We call the practice "togethering," which is a clumsy word for a simple thing: people coming together, in person, to share life, with a game as the medium.

Cube Commonwealth is where we learned that this works. One shared object, a fixed night, a host who cares more about the people than the matches, and rituals that make the evening feel like it belongs to everyone. That is the lounge, in miniature. The cube taught us that the game is a pretext and the gathering is the product.

When the lounge opens, Cube Commonwealth will have a table in it. The cube will sit on a shelf where anyone can draft it. That is the plan, anyway, and plans with a cube at the center have worked out so far.

## FAQ

### What is Cube Commonwealth?

Cube Commonwealth is a Magic: The Gathering drafting community run by The Ludarium's founder, built around one shared cube that members draft on a regular schedule.

### Do I need to own Magic cards to join a cube community?

No. The cube is the collection. Players show up with nothing and draft from the shared pool each night.

### How is a cube group different from a Commander group?

A cube group drafts from one shared box and plays one-on-one matches, so nobody needs their own cards and there are no four-player politics. Commander groups rely on each player's own decks.

### Can new players join a cube group?

Yes. Cube is one of the best formats for learning Magic, and most cube groups, ours included, slow the first draft down for newcomers.
