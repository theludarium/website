Good family game night rules are not about the games. They are about the five minutes before and after: who picks, where the phones go, what happens when someone argues a rule, and when it ends. Families that settle those once, out loud, and write them on the fridge, stop having the same fight every week. The ten rules below have been tested at a lot of tables, including ones with a five-year-old and a teenager at the same time.

Treat them as a starting draft. The best house rules are the ones your family writes together, and the writing is half the value.

## Why does family game night need rules at all?

Because a game already has rules, and the fights are never about those. They are about everything around the game. Who got to pick last time. Whether Dad was looking at his phone. Whether the youngest gets a do-over. Whether "one more game" means one more game.

Without house rules, a parent makes those calls in the moment, under pressure, and the calls are inconsistent. Kids notice inconsistency faster than anything. A rule decided on a calm Tuesday is fair. The same rule invented mid-tantrum on Saturday is a punishment.

A small set of agreed rules also lets a parent step out of the referee role and play. That matters. A game night where one adult is managing the whole time is not a game night for that adult.

## The 10 family game night rules

### 1. The picker rotates, youngest first

One person picks the game each week, from the family shelf. Nobody vetoes. The picker goes in a fixed order and the youngest starts the rotation, because the youngest has the least power everywhere else. Post the order on the fridge.

This one rule removes about half the arguments. The teenager who hates [Dragomino](https://link.amazon/B09WKyU19) knows their week is coming.

### 2. Phones go in the basket

All of them, parents included, in a basket in another room. Not face down. Not "I'm just checking the time." The point of the hour is attention, and a phone on the table leaks it even when nobody touches it. We have a whole piece on [the phone basket](/articles/phone-free-game-night/) and why it matters more than it seems.

### 3. The teach is short, and everyone listens once

Whoever knows the game best explains it in under five minutes: goal, what you do on a turn, how it ends. Everyone listens without interrupting. Questions after. If the teach runs long, the game is too big for tonight. Our guide to [teaching a board game in five minutes](/articles/how-to-teach-a-board-game/) is the method.

### 4. The first game is a practice game

For any new game, the first play does not count. Mistakes are free, rules can be looked up mid-turn, and nobody keeps score. This removes the fear of learning in front of siblings and makes the second play far smoother.

### 5. Rules disputes get thirty seconds and then a coin

If two people disagree about a rule, someone checks the rulebook for thirty seconds. If it is not clear, flip a coin and play on. The ruling holds for tonight. Nobody relitigates it after the game ends.

### 6. No take-backs after the next player moves

You can undo a move until the next person acts. After that it stands. Simple, fair, and it stops the "wait, wait, wait" that slows everything down. Younger kids get a bit more leeway here, and that is stated openly, not hidden.

### 7. Good game, both directions

The loser says "good game." The winner says "good game." No gloating, no sulking, no explaining why you would have won. The winner puts the game away. This is the rule that takes longest to stick and does the most good. More in [teaching kids to lose gracefully](/articles/teaching-kids-to-lose-at-games/).

### 8. Everyone plays to the end

Nobody quits a game because they are losing, and nobody is eliminated, which means picking games without elimination. Every game on our list of the [best family board games](/articles/best-family-board-games/) passes that test. If a game has to stop early for bedtime, it stops for everyone.

### 9. Help is allowed when asked for

A younger player can ask anyone for advice on their turn. Nobody gives advice unless asked. This keeps older siblings and parents from running the younger kid's game while leaving a door open when they are stuck.

### 10. It ends when it ends

Set the end time before you start. When it arrives, finish the current game and stop, even if everyone wants more. Especially then. "Again next week" is the feeling to leave on the table.

## Which rules matter most for which age?

Not every rule pulls equal weight at every stage. Here is roughly where the emphasis goes.

| Age of youngest | Rules to enforce hardest | Rules to relax |
|---|---|---|
| 4 to 6 | Picker rotates, practice game, help when asked, ends on time | Take-backs (give them a few), rules disputes (just decide) |
| 7 to 9 | Good game both ways, no quitting, phones | Practice game (they learn fast now) |
| 10 to 12 | Rules disputes by coin, no take-backs, short teach | Help when asked (they want to do it alone) |
| Teens | Phones, picker rotates (their pick counts), ends on time | Nearly everything else. Treat them as a peer. |

The phone rule is the only one that never relaxes. If anything it gets stricter as the kids get older, and parents are the ones who most often break it.

## How do you handle the kid who argues every rule?

Make the argument boring. Thirty seconds of rulebook, a coin flip, done. Do not debate. Do not explain your reasoning at length. The kid who argues rules is usually testing whether arguing works, and the only answer that ends it is a calm, consistent process that does not reward it.

If it is persistent, let that child be the rules-reader for a few weeks. Give them the rulebook and the authority. Kids who argue rules often love rules, and being the official expert redirects the energy. The same approach works for adult tables, where the arguing is quieter but no less tiresome.

## Should parents play to win?

Yes, with judgment. Playing at full strength against a five-year-old at a pure skill game is pointless. Playing to win at [Kingdomino](https://link.amazon/B08l49zvA) against a nine-year-old is respect. Children know when they are being handed a game, and it devalues the win.

The adjustment is in the game choice, not the effort. Pick games with enough luck that the kids win a real share. Then play properly. The day your kid beats you fair and square at [Ticket to Ride](https://link.amazon/B0jh8RB7f) is a day they will remember, and it only means something if you were trying.

## What if the rules themselves cause a fight?

Then write them together. Sit down on a non-game night, say "we keep having the same arguments, let's fix it," and let each person propose one rule. Vote. Write the list. Stick it on the fridge.

Kids follow rules they helped write far more reliably than rules handed down. And the conversation itself is useful. A seven-year-old explaining why the picker should rotate is doing something most adults never practice: making a case for fairness in a group they belong to.

Revisit the list every few months. Rules that made sense with a five-year-old may be insulting to an eight-year-old, and a rule nobody breaks anymore can come off the fridge.

## FAQ

### What are good rules for family game night?

Rotate who picks, put phones in another room, keep the teach under five minutes, treat the first play of a new game as practice, settle rules disputes with a coin, say "good game" both ways, and end at a set time.

### How do you stop fights during family game night?

Decide the rules in advance on a calm day, write them down, and apply them the same way every week. Most fights come from inconsistent calls made mid-game.

### Should kids be allowed to pick the game?

Yes, in a fixed rotation with the youngest first. A child who picked the game is invested in it going well, and the rotation ends the argument about whose turn it is to choose. If the youngest is five, give them two choices from our [board games for 5-year-olds](/articles/board-games-for-5-year-olds/).

### How long should family game night last?

About an hour for families with young kids, up to two for older ones. Set the end time before you start and keep it.
