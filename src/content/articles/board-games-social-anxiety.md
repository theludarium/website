People who look for board games for social anxiety are usually looking for something more specific than a game. They want a way to be around other people that does not require constant eye contact, improvised small talk, or being the center of attention. A game table happens to provide exactly that. This article explains why, which games tend to be easier starting points, and which to avoid at first.

A note before anything else. Social anxiety is a real condition, and this is not a treatment article. Games are not therapy. If social situations cause you distress that gets in the way of your life, a therapist or doctor can help, and the approaches they use are well studied. What follows is about why many people, anxious or not, find a game table easier than a party, and how to use that.

## Why is a game table easier than a party?

A party asks you to generate the interaction yourself. Who do I talk to? What do I say? When do I leave this conversation? Is it my turn to speak? Each of those is a small decision under observation, and for someone whose mind treats observation as threat, the decisions pile up.

A game removes most of them. The structure of a game answers the questions that make unstructured socializing hard.

- **Who do I talk to?** The people at the table. The seating is set.
- **What do I say?** Something about the game. The topic is provided.
- **Is it my turn to speak?** The game tells you. Turn order is a social rule everyone can see.
- **Where do I look?** At the board, the cards, the tiles. Eye contact becomes optional instead of expected.
- **How long do I have to stay?** Until the game ends. There is a built-in exit.
- **What do I do with my hands?** Hold cards. Move pieces. Nobody notices.

None of these are small. Researchers who study social anxiety often describe a cycle of [self-focused attention](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/18449): the person monitors their own behavior so closely that they have less attention left for the actual conversation, which can hurt their performance in it and blocks the outside evidence that might have calmed the worry. A game pulls attention outward, toward a shared object, and gives the conversation an external subject.

## What does the research say?

It is worth being careful here, because it would be easy to overstate things. There is a solid, broad literature on the health associations of social connection generally. The [U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf) on loneliness and isolation summarized social connection as associated with better physical and mental health outcomes across the lifespan. That is about connection in general, not games.

On games specifically, the evidence is thinner and mostly about related things: structured group activities, shared-attention tasks, and "side-by-side" interaction, where people engage with an object together rather than facing each other. These formats come up repeatedly in discussions of how people, especially those who find direct social interaction effortful, build comfort with others, though we have not found a study that tests board games specifically.

What we can say with confidence is modest and useful: a game provides structure, shared focus, defined roles, and a clear ending. For many people, those features make the difference between showing up and staying home. Whether that counts as "help" for a particular person's anxiety is something only they and, if they have one, their clinician can judge.

## Which games are the easiest starting points?

Not all games are equal here. The best ones for an anxious first-timer share a profile: no spotlight, no performance, low stakes for mistakes, and plenty of shared focus on the table.

| Game | Why it is a gentle start |
|---|---|
| [Azul](https://link.amazon/B0bGRYHex) | Quiet, everyone looks at the tiles, mistakes are minor |
| [Ticket to Ride](https://link.amazon/B0jh8RB7f) | Long turns of looking at a map, little direct talk needed |
| [Carcassonne](https://link.amazon/B012Rr5ed) | Place a tile, done; conversation is optional |
| [The Crew](https://link.amazon/B099S9RKf) | Cooperative, talking is restricted by rule, nobody is singled out |
| [Hanabi](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01MXTGT21?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) | Cooperative, structured clues, team wins or loses together |
| [Cascadia](https://link.amazon/B0g1HYFt4) | Solitaire-feeling puzzle at a shared table |
| [Patchwork](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FPXTLZ21?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) | Two players, calm, almost no interaction |

**Azul** is the most common recommendation and for good reason. It is beautiful enough that everyone looks at the board instead of each other, the rules take five minutes, and a bad move costs a few points rather than the game.

**The Crew** and **Hanabi** are cooperative games that limit communication by rule. This is a gift. In most social settings, the question "should I say something?" never shuts off. In The Crew, you literally cannot discuss your cards, so the question is answered for you. You play, the team wins or loses together, and nobody's mistake is on display.

**Ticket to Ride** is good for a longer session because most of the game is quiet planning. There is a shared map to stare at and long stretches where no one expects you to talk.

For someone who wants to be in the room with people without being at the center of anything, these are the games. ## Which games should you skip at first?

Some games are built around exactly the things social anxiety makes hard. They are great games. They are bad first games.

- **Social deduction games** (Werewolf, The Resistance, Avalon, Secret Hitler). These require lying to a table of people and being accused in return. Later, maybe. Not first.
- **Performance games** (charades, Cranium, most drawing or acting games). Being watched while doing something badly is the specific thing to avoid.
- **Spotlight word games** (Codenames as the clue-giver, Wavelength as the psychic). These are fine as a team member, and uncomfortable in the lead role. If you play them, ask to be a guesser.
- **Elimination games.** Being knocked out early and sitting apart from the table is isolating.
- **Negotiation-heavy games** (Catan's trading phase, Diplomacy). Unstructured talk under pressure.

This is not a permanent list. Many people who start with Azul end up loving Werewolf a year later. The point is sequence. Build comfort at the table first.

## How do you actually show up the first time?

Knowing the right game does not get you through the door. A few things that make the first session easier:

1. **Go with one person you already know.** A single familiar face changes the room.
2. **Pick an open game night at a store or library over a private party.** Public events have lower social obligations. Nobody expects you to stay, mingle, or come back. You can leave after one game without explanation.
3. **Arrive a few minutes early, not late.** Walking into a game already in progress means being watched while you join. Arriving early means you are part of the setup.
4. **Ask to learn a game you do not know.** This gives you a role (student) and the other people a role (teacher). It also lowers expectations for your play.
5. **Set a time limit in advance.** "I'll stay for one game" is a plan. Plans are easier than open-ended exposure. You can always stay longer.
6. **Expect the first ten minutes to be the worst.** Almost everyone reports that the discomfort drops once the game starts. The structure takes over.

If your anxiety is severe enough that even this list feels impossible, that is important information, and it is a good reason to talk to a professional rather than to try harder on your own. Therapists who work with social anxiety often use graded, structured exposure, and some may see a game night as a reasonable step. Ask them.

## FAQ

### Are board games good for social anxiety?

Many people with social anxiety find game tables easier than unstructured socializing because the game provides topics, turn order, shared focus, and a clear ending. They are not a treatment, and a therapist can help if anxiety interferes with your life.

### What is the best board game for someone with social anxiety?

Azul is the most common starting point: quiet, visual, low stakes, and quick to learn. Cooperative games like The Crew and Hanabi also work well because no one is singled out.

### Should someone with social anxiety avoid party games?

At first, yes, especially games that require performing, lying, or being the center of attention. Many people enjoy them later once they are comfortable at the table. Sequence matters more than avoidance.

### Is a board game cafe a good place for an anxious person to socialize?

Often, yes. Public game spaces have low obligations: you can play one game and leave, and staff will usually teach rules so you do not have to. See [what is a board game cafe](/articles/what-is-a-board-game-cafe/) for what to expect.
