A workplace game club is the cheapest culture program most companies never run. It costs a shelf of short games and one hour a week. It needs no budget approval beyond a few hundred dollars and no facilitator beyond whoever is willing to send the first calendar invite. And it does what expensive programs struggle to do: it gets people from different teams talking to each other, on purpose, every week.

This guide is for HR leads, office managers, and the employee who wants to start one. It covers the format, the games, the pitfalls, and how to keep a club alive after the first month.

## Why a lunch hour, and not after work?

After-work events exclude people. Parents, commuters, caregivers, and anyone with a second job cannot stay. A lunch hour is already in everyone's day. It asks nothing extra.

Lunch also sets a natural limit. Sixty minutes, hard stop. That limit shapes which games work, and it is a good thing: short games, quick teaches, and people leaving while they still want more.

A few teams run it as a half-hour. That is tight but workable with games like Sushi Go! or Just One. Forty-five to sixty minutes is the sweet spot.

## What does a workplace game club need to start?

Less than you think.

- **One person who will show up every week.** Not an organizer, exactly. Just someone reliable. Clubs die when the first person stops coming.
- **A table.** A conference room with a four-top is fine. A spot in the break room is better, because people walking past will stop.
- **Five to eight games.** Short, easy to teach, four to six players each. More on which ones below.
- **A recurring calendar invite.** Same day, same time, same room. Make it optional and say so.
- **One sentence of permission from leadership.** Not sponsorship. Just "yes, this is fine." It removes the worry that playing a game at noon looks bad.

Optional but helpful: a Slack channel for "who's coming today," and a small budget for one new game a month.

## Which games work for a lunch-hour club?

The constraints are strict: teach in under five minutes, play in under forty, support whoever shows up, and handle people joining late. Here is a starter shelf of eight.

| Game | Players | Time | Why it works at lunch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Codenames | 4–8+ | 15 min | Two or three rounds in an hour; absorbs latecomers |
| Just One | 3–7 | 20 min | Cooperative, zero pressure, fits a shy crowd |
| Sushi Go! | 2–5 | 15 min | Teaches in one sentence, plays while eating |
| Azul | 2–4 | 35 min | A real game that still ends on time |
| The Crew | 2–5 | 20 min | Short missions; stop after any one |
| Wavelength | 4–12 | 30 min | Scales up when the room gets crowded |
| Cascadia | 1–4 | 40 min | Quiet and thoughtful for a calmer day |
| Skull | 3–6 | 15 min | Bluffing in a tiny box; a reliable hit |

Avoid games over forty-five minutes, games with long setups, and anything that needs a dedicated teacher who knows a sixty-page rulebook. Save Gloomhaven for the weekend. For a broader list with more context, see [team-building board games that don't feel like team building](/articles/team-building-board-games/).

Keep food in mind too. Card games and greasy sandwiches are enemies. Sleeve cards or steer the room toward games with chunky tiles on sandwich days.

## How do you run the hour?

A format that keeps things moving:

1. **Arrive and pick (first 5 min).** Whoever is there by five past picks a game based on headcount. Do not wait for stragglers.
2. **Teach (5 min).** The person who knows it teaches. If nobody does, pick a different game. Reading a rulebook aloud kills the hour.
3. **Play (30–40 min).** One game, or two short ones.
4. **Late arrivals.** Seat them for the next round, or start a second table if there are enough. Never make someone wait twenty minutes.
5. **End on time (last 5 min).** Pack up at five to the hour even if the game is not done. People need to trust that the club respects their afternoon.

Rotate who teaches. The person who learned Azul last week teaches it this week. That spreads the knowledge and keeps the club from depending on one expert.

## How do you get people to come?

The first week, you may have three people. That is fine. Here is what grows it.

- **Invite individuals, not the all-hands.** "Hey, we're playing Codenames at noon Thursday, want in?" works. A company-wide email does not.
- **Play somewhere visible.** A game in the break room draws curiosity. A game in a closed conference room draws nobody.
- **Recruit one person from each department.** Game clubs that are all engineers stay all engineers. Ask the finance person. Ask the receptionist.
- **Keep the games light at first.** A new visitor who gets handed a heavy strategy game will not come back. Start them on Just One.
- **Post a photo once in a while.** Not every week. Just enough that people know it exists.
- **Let it be imperfect.** Some weeks two people will play a two-player game. That is still a club.

## What problems come up, and how do you handle them?

Every workplace game club hits the same few snags.

**The same six people every week.** Not a problem in itself, but it caps the value. Fix it by assigning a "bring someone new" week once a month.

**The competitive person.** One player who takes winning too seriously changes the room. Steer toward cooperative games for a few weeks, and quietly ask them to teach, which shifts their focus.

**Managers who hover.** A director who drops in to watch makes everyone self-conscious. Invite them to play or ask them, politely, to let it be. For more on the dynamics, see [why psychological safety shows up faster at a game table](/articles/psychological-safety-team-activities/).

**Lost pieces and sticky cards.** Have a rule: the person who opens the box closes it. Keep a small kit of sleeves and a damp cloth nearby.

**The founder leaves.** Clubs often die when the person who started them changes jobs. Prevent it early by having two or three people who each own one week a month.

## Is a game club worth it for the company?

For the cost of a bookshelf and some calendar space, a company gets a weekly cross-functional meeting that nobody dreads. People from teams that never collaborate learn each other's names and, more importantly, each other's thinking. When those two people land on a project together, the first awkward week is already behind them.

It will not show up in a dashboard. It shows up in a slightly easier email, a faster answer to a question, a new hire who found a friend in week two. Gallup's [engagement research](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/397058/increasing-importance-best-friend-work.aspx) links having a best friend at work to higher engagement and a lower likelihood of leaving, and finds that only about two in ten U.S. employees have one. A game club is one of the few ways to build that on purpose without making it weird.

The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### How do you start a board game club at work?

Pick a weekly lunch slot, buy five to eight short games, send a recurring optional invite, and show up every week. Invite individuals directly rather than announcing it company-wide.

### What are the best board games for a lunch-hour game club?

Codenames, Just One, Sushi Go!, Azul, and The Crew. All teach in minutes and play in under forty.

### How much does it cost to start a workplace game club?

A starter shelf of eight games runs a few hundred dollars. After that, one new game a month is plenty.

### What if nobody comes?

Start with two or three people and play anyway. Visibility and personal invitations grow it over a month or two. Clubs that start with a big announcement and an empty room rarely recover.
