Most adults stop playing somewhere in their twenties and never notice. Work fills the hours, then family, then the quiet exhaustion of being responsible for things. Play gets filed under childhood, like braces. That is a mistake, and the benefits of play for adults are large enough that it is worth undoing on purpose.

The psychiatrist Stuart Brown, who founded the National Institute for Play, has a line that frames the whole topic: the opposite of play is not work, it is depression. He meant it as an observation from decades of interviews, not a diagnosis. But it points at something true. Play is not a break from a serious life. It is part of what makes a life feel like one.

## What counts as play for an adult?

Play is any activity you do for its own sake, where the doing is the point and you lose track of time while doing it. Brown's working definition includes a few features: it is apparently purposeless, voluntary, attractive, free from the pressure of time, and it makes you want to keep going.

By that measure, a lot of adult life has play in it already, just unlabeled. Pickup basketball. Cooking something ambitious on a Sunday with no guests. A long conversation that goes nowhere useful. A game of [Catan](https://link.amazon/B0h8BDGR2) that runs late.

What most adults lack is not the capacity but the permission. Board games are useful here because they come with permission built in. Nobody has to justify sitting down to [Azul](https://link.amazon/B0bGRYHex). The game supplies the reason, and the people supply the rest.

## What does the research suggest about the benefits of play for adults?

The science here is less tidy than the science on, say, exercise, partly because "play" is hard to define in a lab. But several lines of research point the same way.

Stress and mood. Play tends to produce states researchers associate with lower stress, including absorption, laughter, and a sense of control. Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's work on flow, the state of complete absorption in a task, describes it as arising from a balance between the challenge of the activity and the person's skill; a [2025 study of more than 500 board game players](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10902-024-00846-4) found flow was highest when both the challenge and the player's skill were high, which describes a good game almost exactly.

Social connection. The [U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf) on loneliness pulled together research linking social isolation to a wide range of health problems, including heart disease, stroke, dementia, and premature death, and urged people to make time for connection, such as sharing a meal or listening without a phone in hand. Games are one of the few adult activities that are social by default. You cannot play [Codenames](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B014Q1XX9S?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) alone.

Cognition. Long-term studies like the [Lothian Birth Cohort in Scotland](https://www.ed.ac.uk/research-innovation/latest-research-news/games-can-protect-thinking-skills-in-older-age) have found that people who play non-digital games regularly in their 70s score better on memory and thinking tests, and that those who play more over the decade show less decline, though the researchers call the association small and are careful not to claim cause. We cover that evidence in more detail in [what the research says about board games and dementia](/articles/board-games-and-dementia/).

Creativity and problem-solving. Studies of playfulness in adults, including [work by René Proyer](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01440/pdf) at Martin Luther University Halle-Wittenberg, have linked a playful disposition to a more active lifestyle and better self-reported health, and the earlier research that work builds on connects playfulness with coping, creativity, and wellbeing. The associations are modest and the designs are mostly cross-sectional, but the research suggests, roughly, that people who play more also handle hard things more flexibly.

None of this means a board game is medicine. It means play seems to do several good things at once, and most adults are getting very little of it.

## Why do adults stop playing?

Three reasons come up again and again.

**Time scarcity.** Adults feel busy, and play looks optional. But the research on leisure tends to find that people who protect time for unstructured enjoyment report feeling less time-poor, not more, because the time they do have feels like theirs.

**Status anxiety.** Play involves looking a little foolish. You lose at [Wavelength](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07T446163?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) in front of your friends. You guess wrong in [Wingspan](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07YQ641NQ?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) and everyone sees. Adults who have built an identity on competence find that uncomfortable, which is exactly why it is good for them.

**No structure.** Children have recess. Adults have nothing that says "now you play." Without a container, play doesn't happen. This is the problem board games solve better than almost anything else. A game has a start, an end, rules, and a table. It is a container you can schedule.

## How is play different from entertainment?

This distinction matters more than it sounds. Entertainment is something done to you. Play is something you do.

| | Entertainment | Play |
|---|---|---|
| Your role | Audience | Participant |
| Attention | Passive, often split | Active, absorbed |
| Social shape | Parallel (together but separate) | Interactive |
| Afterward | Often a little drained | Often a little lighter |
| Example | Scrolling, streaming | A game of [The Crew](https://link.amazon/B099S9RKf), a pickup game |

Both have a place. But a lot of adults think they are getting play when they are only getting entertainment, and then wonder why the evening didn't do what they hoped. An hour of [Ticket to Ride](https://link.amazon/B0jh8RB7f) with three people does something that an hour of television with the same three people doesn't, and most people can feel the difference by the end of the night.

## How do you get more play into an adult life?

The practical answer is to stop waiting for it to happen and build a container.

1. **Pick a recurring slot.** Weekly is better than monthly. The research on habits is clear that fixed cues beat good intentions, and "second Thursday" is not a cue. "Thursday" is.
2. **Pick people, not a game.** The game can rotate. The group is what makes it stick. Three regulars and an open chair is a good shape.
3. **Lower the bar.** A 30-minute game on a weeknight counts. Azul, The Crew, and Codenames all fit in the time it takes to watch one episode of something.
4. **Let yourself be bad at it.** The first few plays of anything new are clumsy. That is the play. Being new at something is the part adults are most starved for.
5. **Go where play already happens.** Game cafes and lounges exist so you don't have to host. If you don't have a group yet, a [public game night](/articles/board-game-night-near-me/) is the fastest way to find one.

The LEV Institute, which we work alongside, treats play as a longevity practice in the same category as sleep and movement: not optional, not childish, and not something you can outsource. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### What are the main benefits of play for adults?

The research suggests play is associated with lower stress, better mood, stronger social ties, more flexible thinking, and, in later life, slower cognitive decline. The effects are associations, not guarantees, but they point consistently in one direction.

### Is playing board games good for mental health?

Board games combine several things research links to wellbeing: absorption in a challenge, in-person social contact, and laughter. That doesn't make them a treatment for anything, but as a regular habit they check more boxes than most leisure options.

### How much play do adults need?

There is no established dose. A reasonable starting point is one protected session a week that you look forward to, with other people, doing something for its own sake.

### What if I feel silly playing games as an adult?

That feeling is common and it fades fast. Most people find that a few plays in, the self-consciousness is gone and what's left is the fun. Starting with a cooperative game like [Pandemic](https://link.amazon/B0eHP5GCg) or The Crew removes the pressure of winning and losing.
