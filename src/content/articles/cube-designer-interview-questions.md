A good cube designer interview is not about which cards are in the cube. The list is public; anyone can read it. The interview is for the things the list cannot tell you: what the designer was trying to do, what they tried that failed, how they decide, and what they would tell themselves five years ago. These twenty questions are built for that, grouped in five sets of four, with a note on what each answer tends to reveal.

Use them for a podcast, a member spotlight, a Discord AMA or a long conversation at the bar after the draft. Ask them in roughly this order. The early ones are easy and build trust; the later ones only work once the designer has relaxed.

## Set 1: Origin

These are warm-ups, and they are also where the best stories live.

**1. What was the first version of this cube, and what was wrong with it?**
Everyone's first cube was a pile of good cards. The interesting part is the moment they noticed it was a pile and not a format. Listen for the specific draft that made them see it.

**2. What was the cube for when you started, and what is it for now?**
Lucky Paper's [first design question](https://luckypaper.co/articles/the-first-four-questions-cube-designers-should-ask/) is "How will your cube be played?" and the answer usually changes. A cube that started for a college dorm and now runs for a monthly group of parents has been redesigned whether the owner says so or not.

**3. Who was the first person other than you to draft it, and what did they say?**
Designers remember this. The first outside reaction is often the first real design feedback, and the owner's response to it tells you how they take criticism.

**4. What is the cube's name, and did the name come before or after the identity?**
A name chosen early is an aspiration. A name chosen late is a description. Both are fine, but the order says something about how the designer works.

## Set 2: Structure

Now the numbers. If you have read the list, as [how to read a cube list](/articles/how-to-read-a-cube-list/) suggests, you already know the answers and can ask about the reasons instead.

**5. Why this size?**
360, 450, 540, 720. Every size is a decision about how much of the cube each eight-player draft sees. The [MTGO Vintage Cube](https://www.mtgo.com/vintage-cube-cardlist) holds 540 through hundreds of swaps; the [Legacy Cube list](https://www.mtgo.com/legacy-cube-cardlist) runs 600. Ask what size the designer tried before this one.

**6. How did you set the color counts, and are they even?**
Even counts say "every color is a lane." Uneven counts, like the Vintage Cube's 80 blue to 56 green, say the designer has a view about which colors do the heavy lifting. Either is a design statement. Ask them to defend it.

**7. How much fixing is in the cube, as a percentage, and how did you arrive at it?**
Most designers have never calculated it. Lucky Paper's [land study](https://luckypaper.co/articles/how-many-lands-should-you-include-in-your-cube/) put typical cubes around 8% to 12% while recommending much more for two-color decks at Legacy or Modern power. If the designer knows their number, they are in the minority, and the follow-up is whether their drafters' decks are casting their spells.

**8. What does your gold section do: hints or full archetypes?**
Six per guild is a hint. Fifteen is an archetype with its own payoffs. Ask which, and whether every guild's gold cards agree with each other about the pair's plan.

## Set 3: Decisions

This is the heart of the interview. Slow down here.

**9. Walk me through the last card you added. Why that card, why that slot, why now?**
You want the actual process: where the suggestion came from, what it replaced, whether it was tested first. The MTGO Vintage Cube publishes a design comment for each change; ask the designer to give you theirs out loud.

**10. What is the card you have cut and re-added more than once?**
Every long-running cube has one. The story of why it keeps coming back is a story about what the cube actually values, as opposed to what the primer says it values.

**11. Which archetype have you tried hardest to make work, and has it?**
Designers will tell you about the lane that is humming. Ask about the one they are still fighting with. Listen for whether they blame the cards, the drafters, or the environment around the lane. Our piece on archetype density is about how many lanes a cube can actually hold.

**12. What did you cut that your drafters still complain about?**
This reveals the relationship between owner and group. Some designers hold the line; some reverse. Both can be right. The answer tells you which kind of cube this is.

## Set 4: Power and policing

Careful questions. Designers get defensive about power level because it sounds like a judgment of taste.

**13. How would you describe the power level, in your own words, and what card is just over the line?**
"Unpowered Legacy" or "peasant" is the label. The card just over the line is the real answer. Everyone has one they decided against: Sol Ring, Oko, Mana Drain, Skullclamp.

**14. Is there a card you run that you know is too strong, and why do you keep it?**
Sometimes the answer is nostalgia. Sometimes it is that the card holds an archetype together. Sometimes it is that the group would riot. All three are legitimate, and the designer's honesty here is the best signal in the whole interview.

**15. How much combo do you allow, and how do you decide?**
Supported, tolerated or banned. The MTGO Vintage Cube's [August 2026 update](https://www.mtgo.com/news/hob-vintage-cube-update) said combo support was "higher than it has been in quite some time" and explained why, package by package. Ask the designer for their equivalent explanation.

**16. What is your rule for free spells, fast mana and the other things that end games early?**
The answer is often "I have not written it down." That is useful too. It tells you the cube is governed by instinct rather than principle, which is not wrong, but it is worth the designer hearing themselves say it.

## Set 5: Reflection

End here. These are the questions that produce the quotes people remember.

**17. What is the best draft this cube has ever produced, and what made it good?**
Not the best deck. The best draft: the table, the signals, the game that went long. The answer tells you what the designer is actually optimizing for, which is almost never "the strongest deck wins."

**18. What would you tell yourself the week you started?**
Designers answer this fast. Usual answers: fewer cards, more fixing, write things down, ask the drafters sooner. Whatever they say is the lesson they paid the most for.

**19. Who taught you the most about cube design, and what did they teach you?**
Could be a person, a site, a cube they drafted once. Lucky Paper, the Pauper Cube, a friend's list. The answer tells you where the designer's ideas came from and gives you a thread to pull.

**20. What is the next change, and when?**
End on the future. If they have a date and a card, the cube is alive. If they say "whenever," it may be drifting, and that is worth gently noting. [A cube is never finished](/articles/cube-is-never-finished/) is the piece to send them afterward.

## Running the interview

A few habits that make these questions work.

- **Read the list first.** Count the sections. You will ask better questions and the designer will notice you did the work.
- **Ask one at a time and wait.** The second half of an answer is better than the first. Silence does the work.
- **Follow the card.** Whenever a designer names a specific card, ask why that one. Specifics are where the design thinking lives.
- **Take the quotes down exactly.** If you publish anything, the designer's words appear as said, and they see the draft first. No reconstructed quotes, ever.
- **Stop at twenty.** Forty-five minutes to an hour. Designers can talk about their cubes for days, and the interview is better when it ends with something left over.

|---|---|---|
| Origin | 1 to 4 | How the designer learned; what they were reacting against |
| Structure | 5 to 8 | Whether the numbers are chosen or inherited |
| Decisions | 9 to 12 | The actual process behind changes |
| Power | 13 to 16 | Where the lines are and whether they are written down |
| Reflection | 17 to 20 | What the designer values; the quotable answers |

## FAQ

### What should I ask a cube designer first?

Start with origin: what the first version was and what was wrong with it. It is easy to answer and it usually produces a story.

### How long should a cube designer interview be?

Forty-five minutes to an hour. Twenty questions is enough; stop while there is still something to talk about next time.

### Should I read the cube list before the interview?

Yes. Count the sections and the fixing so you can ask about reasons rather than facts, and so the designer knows you did the work.

### Can I publish the designer's answers?

With their review. Quotes appear exactly as spoken, the designer sees the draft first, and nothing is attributed to them that they did not say.
