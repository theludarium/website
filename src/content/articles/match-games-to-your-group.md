Choosing board games for your group is easy if you are honest about who the group is. It is nearly impossible if you are shopping for the group you wish you had. This article, part of our Running a Play Group series, is about the difference, and about the short test that keeps a game off the Shelf of Shame before you ever pay for it.

Every hobbyist has done this. You read about a sprawling, brilliant, four-hour game. You picture your friends leaning over the map, deep in strategy. You buy it. Two years later it has been played once, by three of you, and one of them left at the halfway point. The game was fine. The group was never going to be that group.

## Why should you buy for reality, not fantasy?

Because a game's value is created at the table, not on the shelf. A play group runs on [The One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/): one copy of each game across the whole group. That rule only saves money if the one copy gets played. An unplayed copy is a Shelf of Shame entry, and the cost per hour of an unplayed game is infinite. It does not matter how good it is.

So the question before any purchase is not "is this a great game?" It is "will this group play this game, with these people, on the nights we actually meet?" Groups are stubbornly themselves.

A group of four friends who like to talk, eat, and play two forty-minute games is a wonderful group. It will not become a four-hour strategy group because you bought a four-hour strategy game. You can nudge a group one step at a time, which is why the starter shelf has a stretch role. You cannot jump it three steps.

## What if your group won't learn heavy games?

Then it won't, and that is information, not a problem to solve.

Some groups love the moment a new rulebook opens. Most do not. Most groups tolerate about ten minutes of teaching before attention drifts, and they would rather play a game they know well than learn one they might love. This is not laziness. It is what people want from an evening with friends after work.

If your group is a light group, go deep instead of heavy. A light game played thirty times, with house variants and running jokes and a standings sheet on the fridge, gives more togethering than a heavy game played once. Codenames, Ticket to Ride, and Azul all reward repeated play, and none asks anyone to study.

If you personally want heavy games, find a second table for them. Many hobbyists keep one group for the long games and one for the friends. A game store's open play night exists partly for this: it is where you find the three other people who also want to play the big box. Just do not buy the big box for the group that has told you, by its behavior, that it does not want it.

If the group has turned down "we should try something meatier" three times, stop offering. Keep the game in the stretch slot of your lane, bring it once a quarter, and let it find its moment or not.

## What is player count reality?

The box says 2 to 5. Your group is six people on a good night and three on a normal night. Which number did you buy for?

Most groups have a real count and a fantasy count. The fantasy count is everyone who has ever shown up. The real count is the number at the table on an average night. Buy for the real count and keep one party game for the fantasy count.

A few practical rules:

- **Count the regulars, not the chat.** If the chat has nine people and five show up on an average night, your count is five.
- **Check the game's best count, not its range.** A game that says 2 to 5 often sings at 3 and limps at 5. Ask the person teaching it, or play it first at a game night.
- **Have one game that scales to everyone.** A party lane game like Just One, Codenames, or Wavelength covers the night when all nine show up. That is the only "fantasy count" purchase a group needs.
- **Have one game for two.** The night when only two show up is coming. Patchwork, Jaipur, or Hive turns a cancelled night into a session.

Player count is also why [claiming a lane](/articles/claim-a-lane/) matters. The person who collects two-player games is the insurance policy. The person who collects party games is the insurance for the big nights. Without those lanes, the group owns five games that all play best at four and nothing else.

## How long is your group's real session?

Session length is the second number people lie to themselves about. The box says 90 minutes. The box assumes four experienced players who are not eating, talking, or checking on a toddler.

Measure it. For one month, note when cards actually hit the table and when the last game ends. Subtract food and setup. That number, not the box number, is your session. For most weeknight groups it is two to two and a half hours of play, spent on two or three games, not one.

That means:

| Group's real play window | What fits | What doesn't |
|---|---|---|
| Under 90 min | Two or three fillers and gateways | Anything over 60 min on the box |
| 90 min to 2.5 hrs | One 60 to 90 min game plus a filler | Games listed at 2 hrs or more |
| 3 hrs or more | One long game, or two mid-weight | Nothing, if you can protect the time |

Attention span matters as much as the clock. A group that starts checking phones at the 70-minute mark has a 70-minute attention span regardless of the end time. The signal is not whether people finish the game, but whether they ask to play it again.

If the group wants longer games, change the night before you change the shelf. One group, two cadences: a weeknight for the short shelf, one weekend afternoon a month for the long one.

## What's the test before buying any game?

Two questions. If you cannot answer both, do not buy it yet.

**Who will teach it?** Name the person. Not "someone can learn it." A specific person who will read the rules, set it up once alone, and run the teach at the table. If that person is you and you have three games in the queue already, the answer is "nobody," and the game should wait.

**When will it hit the table?** Name the session. "Dana's hosting in three weeks and it fits her lane" is an answer. "Sometime this winter" is not. Games bought without a date are bought for the shelf, and the shelf does not play games.

There is a cheaper version of this test, and it is the one the hobby has always recommended: play it before you pay for it. Game nights, cafés, a friend's copy. One play tells you more about whether your group will love a game than any amount of reading. Our [Play Before You Pay](/articles/play-before-you-pay/) article walks through the $0 version of collecting, and it is the single best habit for keeping a group's shelf honest.

If the game passes both questions and you have played it once, buy it. If it fails either question, it is a fantasy purchase, and the cost per play math will eventually tell you so.

## The reality checklist

Print this, or pin it in the group chat. Run it before every purchase that is meant for the group.

1. **What is our real player count on an average night?** Does this game play well at that number, not just allow it?
2. **What is our real play window?** Does this game, with food and teaching, fit inside it?
3. **Has this group played a game of this weight before and asked for it again?** If not, is this one step up or three?
4. **Who teaches it?** A named person who has agreed.
5. **When does it hit the table?** A named session on the calendar.
6. **Has anyone in the group played it?** If no, can we play it somewhere first?
7. **Does someone in the group already own it or something that fills the same role?** Check the shared shelf. One copy per group.
8. **Does it fit the buyer's lane?** If not, is the lane owner the one buying it?

Eight yeses means buy it. Six or seven means borrow it or try it first. Fewer than six means you are shopping for the fantasy group, and the fantasy group does not show up on Wednesdays.

The point is not to be strict. It is to make sure every box the group owns has a night attached to it. A shelf where every game has a reason to be there is a shelf that gets played.

## FAQ

### How do I choose board games for my group?

Buy for the group's real player count, real session length, and real appetite for rules, not the group you imagine. Before any purchase, name who will teach the game and which session it will hit the table. If you cannot answer both, play it somewhere first or wait. Keep one party game and one two-player game as insurance for unusual nights.

### What board games are best for casual gamers?

Games that teach in five to ten minutes and reward repeat play: Codenames, Ticket to Ride, Azul, Sushi Go!, Just One, Carcassonne. A casual group gets more out of a light game played thirty times than a heavy game played once, so favor depth through familiarity over complexity on the box.

### What if my game group won't play heavy games?

Accept it as information about the group, not a problem to fix. Move one step up at a time, offer a heavier game once a quarter, and stop pushing if it is declined three times. If you personally want heavy games, find a second table for them at a game store's open play night rather than buying them for the group.

### How many players should I buy board games for?

Buy for the average number at your table on a normal night, not the number of people in the group chat. Check the game's best player count, not just its listed range. Then keep one party game that scales to everyone and one two-player game for the nights when only two show up.
