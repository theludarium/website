A starter board game collection does not need to be big. It needs to be complete. Six games, each filling a different role, will cover nearly every night you host for years, and you can build the whole shelf for somewhere between $40 and $300. This is the starter shelf, and it is the center of our series, Building Your Library on Purpose.

The idea is borrowed from how good libraries work. A library does not buy twelve copies of the same bestseller; it buys one of each thing a reader might need. Your shelf should do the same. One gateway, one filler, one two-player game, one party game, one cooperative game, and one stretch. No two games fill the same role.

If you have not already, read [Play Before You Pay](/articles/play-before-you-pay/) and [Your First $40](/articles/your-first-40-dollars/). The starter shelf assumes you have a taste log and an anchor. If you skipped those, the shelf will still work, but you will be guessing where you could be choosing.

## Why a starter board game collection should be built by role

Most collections grow by impulse. You buy what caught your eye, and after a year you own four medium-weight strategy games for four players, all about 90 minutes, all competitive. They are good games. They are also the same game, from the point of view of the table. On the night your partner wants something quick, or eight people show up, or a friend is having a hard week and wants to play on the same side, the shelf has nothing to offer.

Building by role fixes this. Before each purchase you ask one question: which occasion does my shelf not yet cover? The result is a shelf where every box has a job and every common night has a box.

It also keeps you out of the biggest money trap in the hobby, which is owning three games that do the same thing. The third 90-minute four-player strategy game creates almost no new plays; it just splits the plays the first one was getting. That is the [cost-per-play](/articles/cost-per-play-explained/) logic behind the whole series, applied to your own shelf instead of your group's.

## The six roles on a starter shelf

Here is what each role covers and what it needs to do.

### Gateway

The gateway is your anchor, the game you teach to anyone. It fits your most common player count, teaches in ten minutes, and holds up across dozens of plays. Catan, Ticket to Ride, and Carcassonne are the classic examples; Azul and Splendor fill the role for groups that like something quicker. If you followed the [first $40 article](/articles/your-first-40-dollars/), you already own this one.

### Filler

A filler plays in under 20 minutes, including the teach. It fills the gap while you wait for the last guest, closes the night after the main event, or saves an evening when nobody has the energy for a big box. Sushi Go!, Love Letter, and Jaipur are fillers. A good filler gets more plays than anything else on the shelf, which is why it is the second role to buy, not the last.

### 2-player

The most common table in most homes is two people, and most big games are tuned for three or four. The 2-player role covers the nights when it is just you and one other person. Patchwork, Jaipur, and Hive are purpose-built for two. If your taste log shows most of your plays are at two, this role may deserve the biggest budget on the shelf.

### Party (6+)

Sooner or later eight people are in your living room, half of whom do not think of themselves as gamers. The party role has to scale to a crowd, teach in two minutes, and make the room laugh. Codenames, Just One, and Wavelength all do this. No strategy game does, no matter how good it is.

### Co-op

A cooperative game puts everyone on the same side against the board. It changes the social temperature of the table: no one loses to a friend, quiet players get pulled in, and a mixed group of competitive and gentle people can all have a good night. Pandemic is the standard; The Crew is a shorter, card-based option. Every shelf needs one.

### Stretch

The stretch is one step up in complexity from your gateway. It is the game you grow into, the one that gives your group somewhere to go once the gateway feels familiar. Wingspan, 7 Wonders, and Dominion are typical stretches. Not Twilight Imperium, not Gloomhaven. One step, not five.

## No two games fill the same role

This is the rule that makes a shelf of six work, and the one people break first. Six games in six roles cover nearly every occasion. Six games in two roles cover two occasions six times over.

Before you buy, write the role on an index card and ask: which game on my shelf currently covers this? If the answer is "one already does," you are not buying for the shelf; you are buying for yourself. That is fine at the [core library stage](/articles/core-library-300-to-1000/), where a second game in a role becomes a real question. At the starter stage, it is the one thing that will stall you.

A quick test for role overlap: if the two games would be played at the same player count, on the same kind of night, for roughly the same length, by the same mood of group, they are in the same role, whatever their themes say.

## A sample $300 starter shelf

The table below is an illustration, not a shopping list. Prices are typical US retail ranges for games of each type; your actual choices and costs will differ, and used copies can bring the total down.

| Role | Example game | Typical price range | Player count it covers | Length |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gateway | Catan | About $40 | 3 to 4 | 60 to 90 min |
| Filler | Sushi Go! | $12 to $20 | 2 to 5 | 15 min |
| 2-player | Patchwork | $25 to $35 | 2 | 20 to 30 min |
| Party (6+) | Codenames | $15 to $25 | 4 to 8+ | 15 to 30 min |
| Co-op | Pandemic | $35 to $45 | 2 to 4 | 45 to 60 min |
| Stretch | Wingspan | $55 to $65 | 1 to 5 | 60 to 90 min |
| **Total** | | **About $180 to $230** | | |

That leaves room under $300 for the inevitable: a game your group fell in love with at a café, a used copy that turned up at the right price, or a swap when one of the six did not land. A starter shelf built this way lands comfortably in the $200 to $300 range at full retail, and well under $200 if you buy two or three of the six used.

Note what the table covers: two players, four players, eight players, 15 minutes, 90 minutes, competitive, cooperative, easy, and one step harder. That is nearly every night you will host.

## What order should you buy the starter shelf in?

Order matters because each purchase should cover the biggest gap left. Here is the sequence we recommend for most people, with the reasoning.

1. **Gateway.** This is your anchor and your first $40. Everything else is chosen to be different from it.
2. **Filler.** Cheapest purchase on the list and the one that gets played most. It also rescues nights that were going to end early.
3. **The role your taste log says you are missing most.** For many people this is 2-player, because most weeknights are two people. For hosts, it is party. Look at your log and count the nights the shelf could not serve.
4. **Co-op.** Once you have the competitive bases covered, add the game that changes the table's mood.
5. **The other of 2-player or party.** Whichever you did not buy at step three.
6. **Stretch.** Last, on purpose. Buy it when your group has played the gateway enough to want more, not before. A stretch bought too early is a [Shelf of Shame](/articles/shelf-of-shame-infinite-cost/) candidate.

Give yourself six to twelve months to complete the six. Buying slowly means each game gets played before the next arrives, and your taste log keeps improving the next choice.

## How six games cover nearly every occasion

Run the shelf against a year of real nights and it holds up surprisingly well.

- **Weeknight with your partner:** 2-player, or the filler.
- **Friday with the regular four:** gateway, then the filler to close.
- **Holiday with the extended family:** party, then co-op for the smaller group that stays late.
- **New friends over, nobody has played anything:** gateway or party, depending on the count.
- **One friend is having a rough week:** co-op. Nobody loses.
- **The regulars want something meatier:** stretch.
- **Fifteen minutes before dinner:** filler.

The occasions this shelf does not cover are real but rare: a solo evening, a six-hour epic, a campaign that runs for months. Those belong to the [core library](/articles/core-library-300-to-1000/) and the [curator](/articles/curator-1000-and-beyond/) stages, when you have the plays to justify them.

## Coordinate your six with your group's six

Here is the part that separates a shelf from a library. If you and three friends each build a starter shelf without talking, you will end up with four copies of Catan, four of Codenames, and no one owning a co-op. Four shelves, twenty-four games, maybe eight distinct titles. Most of the money bought duplicates.

The [One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/) says a play group needs one copy of a game, not one per person. Applied to starter shelves, it means your six and your friends' six should not overlap. Before each purchase, ask the group: does anyone already own a game in this role? If Sam has Pandemic, you do not need a co-op. Buy something else and play Pandemic at Sam's.

Taken further, this becomes [claiming a lane](/articles/claim-a-lane/). Each person in the group takes a role or a genre and goes deep there. One person owns the party games, one the co-ops, one the two-player library, one the stretches. Four people, four shelves of six, twenty-four distinct games, and a [Shared Shelf](/articles/one-copy-rule/) that covers more ground than any one of you could afford alone.

A simple way to start: share your taste logs. If your group keeps the same four-column notes recommended in [Play Before You Pay](/articles/play-before-you-pay/), a ten-minute comparison shows who owns what and where the gaps are. The Ludarium app, in development, is being built to make this group view automatic; it is coming soon. A shared spreadsheet does the job today.

## Common mistakes at the starter shelf stage

A few patterns we see over and over.

**Buying the stretch first.** The heavy game is the exciting one. It is also the one that needs a settled group and a free Saturday. Buy it sixth.

**Skipping the party game because you are "not a party game person."** You will host a crowd eventually. A $20 party game played twice a year for ten years is one of the cheapest things you will ever own per hour.

**Buying expansions before the shelf is complete.** An expansion deepens a role you already cover. Until all six roles are filled, that money covers a gap instead. Our guide to [when expansions are worth it](/articles/expansions-worth-it/) has the full argument.

**Treating the shelf as a ranking.** The six are not your six favorite games. They are six jobs. Your favorite game might not be on the starter shelf at all, and that is fine.

**Buying all six in a month.** You lose the feedback loop. Each game should be played a few times before the next arrives, so the taste log can correct your next choice.

## When the starter shelf is done

You will know the shelf is complete when a month goes by and every game night was served by something you or your group already owns. No scramble, no "I wish we had something for this." At that point, your collection has stopped being a pile and started being a library.

The next stage, from $300 to $1,000, is about going deeper where your group actually plays: first expansions, a second game in your busiest role, and buying for specific people. But most people are surprised by how long the starter shelf lasts. Six well-chosen games, shared across a group that coordinates, can carry a play group for years. The money you did not spend is the point.

## FAQ

### How many board games do you need for a starter collection?

Six. One gateway game, one filler under 20 minutes, one two-player game, one party game for six or more, one cooperative game, and one stretch game a step up in complexity. Each covers a different occasion, so six games handle nearly every night you host. More than six at the start usually means duplicates in the same role rather than more coverage.

### How much does a starter board game collection cost?

Roughly $180 to $300 at US retail for six games, depending on which titles you choose. Fillers and party games often run $12 to $25, gateway and co-op games about $35 to $45, and a stretch game $55 to $65. Buying two or three of the six used can bring the total under $200. The whole shelf is best built over six to twelve months rather than at once.

### What are the essential types of board games to own?

A gateway game you can teach to anyone, a filler that plays in under 20 minutes, a dedicated two-player game, a party game that scales to a crowd, a cooperative game where everyone plays together, and one heavier stretch game. These six roles cover the most common player counts, lengths, and moods a host runs into. No two games on a starter shelf should fill the same role.

### Which board game should I buy first?

Buy your gateway first. It should fit your most common player count, teach in about ten minutes, and stay fresh over many plays. Catan, Ticket to Ride, and Carcassonne are common choices. Then buy a filler, since it is cheap and gets played most. Save the heaviest game for last, once your group has played the gateway enough to want more.

### Should friends in a game group buy the same games?

No. A play group needs one copy of a game, not one per household, because that copy already serves everyone at the table. Coordinate so each person's shelf fills different roles or genres. Four friends with six games each and no overlap share a 24-game library. The same four friends buying independently often end up with four copies of the same three titles.
