A kid who hates losing does not need fewer games. They need more games, chosen so that losing is small, frequent, and survivable, and a parent who refuses to either throw the game or turn it into a lecture. The plan is to shrink the stakes until the child can tolerate a loss, then slowly raise them. It takes a few months. It works for most kids.

This is a specific situation, different from a child who occasionally cries over a loss. This is the kid who flips the board, who cheats openly, who cannot let a game end without winning, and whose reaction is big enough that the family has quietly stopped playing. That last part is the real cost, and it is the thing to fix.

## Why does a child need to win so badly?

Usually one of a few things, and often more than one.

- **Losing feels like a statement about them.** For a child who has not lost much, each loss reads as "I'm not good," and some kids feel that more intensely than others.
- **They are perfectionists.** Kids who hold themselves to a high standard everywhere else bring it to the table. A loss is a failure, and failure is unbearable.
- **They win too rarely.** A younger sibling who loses to an older one every time, or a child who only plays skill games against adults, never gets to feel what winning normally feels like. Every game becomes a desperate attempt to finally get it.
- **Big feelings, small regulation.** The ability to feel disappointment without acting on it develops through childhood and well past it. The [American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry](https://www.aacap.org/AACAP/Families_and_Youth/Facts_for_Families/FFF-Guide/The-Teen-Brain-Behavior-Problem-Solving-and-Decision-Making-095.aspx) notes that the frontal cortex, the part that helps us think before we act, keeps maturing into adulthood, while the emotional, reactive amygdala is up and running much earlier. Some kids are simply slower on that curve.
- **It has worked.** If a meltdown has ever ended with a do-over, a changed rule, or a parent conceding, the child has learned that a big enough reaction changes the result.

None of these mean the child is spoiled or bad. They mean the current setup is too hard. Change the setup.

## What should you stop doing?

Three habits make it worse, and most parents have done all three.

**Stop letting them win.** It feels kind. It teaches that winning is the only acceptable outcome and that adults will arrange it. It also means the child never practices the thing they most need to practice. Kids also figure it out, and a hollow win is worse than an honest loss.

**Stop lecturing mid-meltdown.** Nobody learns anything while crying. "This is why nobody wants to play with you" is a sentence that gets remembered for decades and changes nothing tonight.

**Stop quitting games as a family.** The instinct is to avoid the trigger. But a child who plays once a month gets no practice, and every loss stays a shock. Play more, with smaller games.

## The plan: shrink the stakes, then grow them

Four stages. Move to the next when the current one is going smoothly most of the time. Expect each to take a few weeks.

### Stage 1: Cooperative games only

Outfoxed!, Forbidden Island, My First Castle Panic, The Crew. Everyone wins or loses together. The child gets to experience losing as a shared event with no target, and winning as a team effort rather than a personal triumph. This is the stage that breaks the "winning equals I'm good" link.

### Stage 2: Short, luck-heavy competitive games, played in sets

Sushi Go, Dragomino, Rhino Hero, Zingo!, Monza. Ten minutes each. Play three or four in a row, and treat the set as the event, not any single game. A child who loses the first, wins the second, and loses the third has just had a normal evening. Luck-heavy means they win a real share without help.

### Stage 3: Games with hidden scores

Kingdomino, Ticket to Ride, Azul. Nobody knows who is winning until the end, so there is no slow build of panic as the child watches themselves fall behind. The loss arrives all at once, briefly, and the game is over.

### Stage 4: Normal games

Catan, Carcassonne, whatever the family likes. By now the child has lost dozens of times in low-stakes settings and has a script for it. Some losses will still sting. That is fine. That is everybody.

## What do you say when it happens anyway?

Because it will. Here is a script that works more often than not.

1. **Pause the game.** Do not finish around them, do not pack up dramatically. Hands off the pieces.
2. **Name it, briefly.** "You really wanted that one. It's disappointing." Then stop talking.
3. **Wait.** Thirty seconds feels long. Wait anyway. Most kids come down faster without input.
4. **Offer two doors.** "When you're ready, we can finish this game, or start a new one." Not "or we can stop," because stopping should not be a reward for the reaction.
5. **If they cannot come back, end it quietly.** Put the game away without comment. Try again tomorrow or the next day, not next month.

Later, when things are calm, one sentence: "That was a hard one. Next time you lose, what could you do instead?" Let them answer. Then drop it.

## What about cheating?

Treat it as a symptom of the same thing. A kid who cheats at a game is a kid who cannot tolerate losing it, and the fix is the same plan above. In the moment, do not accuse. "Hang on, I think that card goes back." Fix it and move on.

If it keeps happening, play cooperative games for a while. Cheating is pointless when everyone is on the same team, and it gives the child a break from the pressure that drives it. Then come back to competitive games with the luck-heavy, short ones.

Address it directly only when calm, and keep it short: "When you move your piece extra spaces, the game stops being real. I want to play real games with you." Most kids respond to that better than to a rule.

## Which games help and which make it worse?

| Helps | Why | Makes it worse | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outfoxed!, Forbidden Island, The Crew | Cooperative. No one to lose to. | Monopoly, Risk | Elimination and slow, visible collapse. |
| Sushi Go!, Dragomino, Rhino Hero | Short and lucky. Many results per hour. | Chess, checkers | Pure skill. The adult always wins or obviously throws it. |
| Kingdomino, Ticket to Ride, Azul | Hidden scores. No slow dread. | Uno, Sorry!, Trouble | Direct attacks. The child gets targeted and feels it personally. |
| Telestrations, Just One | Nobody really wins or loses. Just laughs. | Any game over an hour | One big loss after a long investment. |

Avoid anything where players can target each other directly. A kid who has to win experiences a "take that" card as a personal attack, and the sibling who played it knows exactly what they are doing. ## How long does this take?

A few months to real improvement, with twice-weekly play. The first sign is not an absence of meltdowns. It is shorter ones. A child who used to need twenty minutes to recover and now needs five is most of the way there.

The second sign is that they ask for a rematch instead of quitting. The third is the day they lose a close game of Kingdomino, say "ugh, good game," and start resetting the tiles. When that happens, say nothing about it. Just deal the next hand.

The broader skill is the one we covered in [teaching kids to lose and win gracefully](/articles/teaching-kids-to-lose-at-games/), and the kid who struggles most with it is often the one who, with practice, ends up best at it. Intensity about winning is not a flaw. It is energy that has not yet learned where to go.

## FAQ

### Should I let my child win if they hate losing?

No. Pick games with enough luck that they win a real share on their own, and play cooperative games when the pressure is too high. Handing them wins delays the skill they need.

### What games are good for a child who can't stand losing?

Cooperative games like Outfoxed! and Forbidden Island first, then short luck-heavy games like Sushi Go and Dragomino, then games with hidden scores like Kingdomino.

### Is it normal for a 7-year-old to cry when they lose a game?

Yes, for most kids. Struggling with losing is common through the early elementary years, and the intensity varies a lot from child to child. In our experience it improves with practice and usually eases as kids get older, though there is no fixed age it switches off.

### How do I handle my child cheating at board games?

Correct it calmly in the moment without accusing, switch to cooperative games for a while, and talk about it briefly once things are calm. Cheating usually fades as losing gets easier.
