Every play group needs a small set of board games you can teach in five minutes to anyone who walks through the door. We call it the Teacher's Shelf. It is the shelf that rescues a night when the count changes, the main game runs short, or a new person shows up. This article, part of our Running a Play Group series, covers what goes on it, how to teach from it, and why only one person in the group needs to own it.

The Teacher's Shelf is not a beginner's shelf. Experienced groups use it more than new ones, because they know the first fifteen minutes of a session set the tone for the next three hours.

## What belongs on a Teacher's Shelf?

The test is strict: can you explain the goal, the turn, and one example in under five minutes, to someone who has never played a modern board game, and have them playing competently by the second round? If any of those fails, the game is a gateway game, which is fine, but it is not a Teacher's Shelf game.

Here is a working shelf. Eight games is more than enough; five is plenty.

- **Codenames**: two teams, one-word clues, find your spies before the other team finds theirs.
- **Just One**: everyone writes a one-word clue, duplicates get erased, one player guesses.
- **Sushi Go!**: pick a card, pass the hand, collect sets, three quick rounds.
- **Love Letter**: one card in hand, draw one, play one, last player standing wins the round.
- **The Crew**: a trick-taking co-op where the table has to win specific tricks together.
- **Wavelength**: guess where a hidden target sits on a spectrum from a teammate's clue.
- **Azul**: draft tiles, fill rows, score your wall, avoid the penalty floor.
- **Ticket to Ride**: collect colored cards, claim train routes, connect your cities.

Notice the spread. Two party games, two card fillers, one co-op, one team game, two light strategy games. That mirrors the roles on a starter shelf, scaled down to the five-minute teach. A Teacher's Shelf should let the group play at any count from two to ten without a second thought.

Notice also what is missing. Nothing with a tech tree. Nothing where the first game is a "learning game." Nothing that needs a reference card per player. Those games can be wonderful, and they live in other lanes.

### Why these and not others?

Each game on the list has one rule that carries the whole design. Codenames is "one word, one number." Love Letter is "draw one, play one." Sushi Go! is "pick and pass." When a game has one load-bearing rule, you can teach the rule, show one example, and let the rest emerge at the table. Games with three or four interlocking systems cannot be taught that way, no matter how good the teacher is.

## How do you teach a board game in five minutes?

Most people teach games in the order the rulebook presents them: components, setup, turn phases, scoring, exceptions. That order is written for a reader alone at a desk. It is the wrong order for a table of people who want to start.

Use this order instead. It works for every game on the Teacher's Shelf and most gateway games too.

### 1. Goal first

One sentence. "You are trying to connect these two cities with your trains." "Your team is trying to find all your spies before the other team." If people know what winning looks like, every rule you explain afterward has a place to land. Skip this step and every rule sounds arbitrary.

### 2. Turn structure

What a player does on their turn, in order, in as few words as the game allows. "On your turn, you take one of these three actions." "Draw a card, then play a card." Say it, then point at the table while you say it again. Do not explain the scoring yet. Do not explain the exceptions.

### 3. One example

Take a turn yourself, out loud, with your real cards. "I'm drawing. I got a Guard. I'll play it and guess that Priya has the Priest. She doesn't. Next player." A single concrete turn teaches more than five minutes of abstract rules, because now the table has seen what a turn looks like, how long it takes, and what the choices feel like.

### 4. Start playing

Say "we'll figure out the rest as we go" and deal. Answer questions when they come up, at the moment they matter, to the person who asked. The first round is the teach. If the game has scoring that only matters at the end, explain it when the end is near. If someone makes a mistake in round one, let it stand and move on.

That is the whole method. Goal, turn, example, go. Five minutes, often less.

### Things that make a teach slower

- Explaining edge cases nobody has hit yet
- Reading from the rulebook
- Teaching strategy ("you want to do this early")
- Apologizing for the length of the teach, which adds to the length of the teach

If you are the group's teacher, set the game up once alone and run two turns against yourself before the session. Five minutes at home saves ten at the table.

## Why does every group need one Teacher's Shelf?

Because the Teacher's Shelf is the group's shock absorber, and every group gets shocked.

**The count changes at the door.** Five said yes, three came, and the planned game needs four. Pull Sushi Go! and play it while the fourth person texts that they are ten minutes out.

**A new person shows up.** Someone brought a partner, a roommate, a visiting cousin. That person did not sign up for a forty-minute teach. Give them Just One, and by the second round they belong at the table.

**The main game ends early.** The planned 90-minute game ran 60. Everyone has half an hour left and nobody wants to go home. Love Letter fills thirty minutes exactly.

**The main game flops.** It happens. Instead of grinding through a game nobody enjoys, call it, pull Codenames, and salvage the night. The group remembers the salvage, not the flop.

**The host cancels and someone else opens their door on short notice.** The fill-in host may not have their lane ready. The Teacher's Shelf travels in one tote bag and covers the whole night.

A group without a Teacher's Shelf handles all of these by standing in the kitchen debating. A group with one handles them in sixty seconds. That is the difference between a group that feels easy and one that feels like work.

There is a quieter reason too. New members join groups through these games. The person who learned Codenames on their first night and won is the person who comes back.

## Why does only one person need to own it?

Because that is how [The One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/) works, and the Teacher's Shelf is its clearest case.

The rule says a group needs one copy of each game, not one per person. A copy's value is already shared by everyone at the table. A second copy of Codenames in the same group does not create a single extra play; the first copy was never busy when the second one would have been needed. The money spent on it could have bought a game the group does not have.

So in a group running the [Shared Shelf](/articles/one-copy-rule/), the Teacher's Shelf is a lane, and one person claims it. That person is usually the group's natural teacher: the one who reads rules for fun, who sets games up early, who notices when someone at the table is lost. Give them the lane. They buy the eight games over a year, they keep them in one bag, and the bag comes to every session no matter who hosts.

A few practical notes on running the lane:

- **It travels.** Unlike the other lanes, which stay in the owner's home and rotate in when they host, the Teacher's Shelf comes to every session. Our [rotating hosts](/articles/rotating-hosts-rotating-shelves/) article explains why the other lanes stay put.
- **It is small on purpose.** Five to eight games, small boxes, one bag. If it stops fitting in a bag, something on it has drifted out of the five-minute category.
- **It is the one lane where a second copy might pass the test.** If the group regularly splits into two tables of six and both want Codenames, the second-copy test may say yes. Our article on [when a second copy is worth it](/articles/when-a-second-copy-is-worth-it/) walks through that case. For nearly every other group, one bag is enough.
- **The owner is not the only teacher.** Anyone can teach from the shelf. The owner just makes sure it is there.

If your group is new and nobody has claimed lanes yet, claim this one first. It is the cheapest lane to stock, the most-played lane in every group, and the lane that lets the rest of the library exist. A group that can always start a game in five minutes is a group that always starts.

## FAQ

### What board games can you teach in five minutes?

Codenames, Just One, Sushi Go!, Love Letter, The Crew, Wavelength, Azul, and Ticket to Ride all teach in five minutes or less. Each has one load-bearing rule you can explain with a single example, so new players are competent by the second round. Together they cover player counts from two to ten.

### How do you teach a board game quickly?

Teach in this order: the goal in one sentence, the turn structure in as few words as possible, one real example turn played out loud, then start playing and answer questions as they come up. Skip edge cases, strategy, and the rulebook. Set the game up once alone before the session so the teach is rehearsed.

### What is a Teacher's Shelf for a game group?

A Teacher's Shelf is a small set of five to eight games anyone can teach in five minutes. It covers the nights when the player count changes, a newcomer arrives, the main game ends early or flops, or the host cancels. One person in the group owns it and brings it to every session in a single bag.

### Does everyone in a game group need to own easy games?

No. Under The One Copy Rule, a group needs one copy of each game, not one per person. One member claims the Teacher's Shelf as their lane, stocks it, and brings it to every session. A second copy of an easy game in the same group rarely creates a play that would not have happened anyway.
