Cube draft attendance is the one problem every cube owner has and almost nobody writes about. The cube can be perfect. If five people show up, you are playing a Winston draft and apologizing to the three who drove over for a pod. For about three years, Cube Commonwealth seated 12 to 16 players every week in Indianapolis, from a Discord of roughly 300 mostly local members. We did not start there. We started with "whoever answers the text," and some weeks that was four.

This is what moved us from four to a reliable two pods, in the order we would do it again.

## Why is eight the number that matters?

A booster draft wants eight. Eight players, three packs each, every pack seen by the whole table once, and a clean three-round Swiss where everyone plays. Seven is fine. Six is a draft with thin signals. Five or fewer is a different format.

So the goal is not "a big group." The goal is a group whose worst week still produces eight. That reframes everything: you are not recruiting players, you are building a floor. A floor of eight in a bad week usually means 12 to 16 in a good one, which is where two pods come from. We covered how to run those in [running two draft pods at once](/articles/running-two-draft-pods/).

## What actually moved our attendance?

Looking back, five decisions did most of the work. None of them were about the cube.

### 1. We fixed the night and never moved it

For the first stretch we scheduled around availability. "Can everyone do Thursday this week?" produced a group chat of twenty messages and a night of five players. The fix was to pick one night and hold it whether or not it was convenient for any particular person, including the host.

A fixed night does something a flexible one cannot: it lets people build their week around it. Partners know. Work knows. Within a couple of months, the question changed from "is there cube this week?" to "I can't make cube this week," which is a different relationship with the event. <!-- EDITOR: add detail (our night) -->

### 2. We ran the night even when it was thin

The hardest rule and the most important. When four people showed up, we played. Two-headed giant, a Winston draft, a four-player grid draft, anything. We did not cancel, and we did not say "let's skip this week and try again next week."

Cancelling teaches people that the event is conditional. Playing with four teaches them it is permanent. Our worst stretch had three straight weeks of five or six. The three weeks after that were eight, nine, and eleven, and we believe the first three bought the second three.

### 3. We made the weekly post boring and identical

Every week, same day, same time, the same post in the same Discord channel: the night, the address, the start time, a "react if you're in" line. No creativity. People do not read creative posts, they recognize familiar ones. The reaction count became a public, visible headcount, and once it showed seven, the eighth person came to make the pod. The channel layout is simple: one channel, one post, nothing else in it.

### 4. We asked people to commit, then let them off the hook

The react-to-RSVP system worked because it was low-stakes. But low stakes cut both ways: a reaction is not a seat. So we added one soft norm. If you reacted and could not come, you un-reacted or said so by the afternoon. No penalty, no guilt, just information. That one habit made the count trustworthy, and a trustworthy count is what tells the sixth and seventh players it is worth the drive.

### 5. We counted on a core of six, not a crowd of thirty

Our 300-member Discord sounds like the asset. It was not. The asset was six people who came every single week unless they were sick. Six regulars plus a trustworthy headcount means you only need two more from the long tail of occasional players, and the long tail reliably produces two.

If you have three true regulars, your real job is to turn three into six. Everything else is secondary.

## How do you turn occasional players into regulars?

The long tail becomes the core one person at a time, and the pattern was consistent enough that we can describe it.

- **The second visit is the one that counts.** First visits are curiosity. Second visits are habit forming. We made a point of messaging first-timers before the next week, personally, not in the channel.
- **Seat them next to someone kind.** A new player's first draft is confusing. Put a patient veteran on their right and tell the veteran what you are doing. We wrote up the whole approach in [new-player cube night](/articles/cube-for-new-magic-players/).
- **Give them a role.** The person who brings the playmats, the person who tracks records, the person who runs the second pod. A role is a reason to come that survives a bad draft.
- **Learn what they drafted.** "You took the red deck again" is a small thing. It tells someone they are part of a story, not a headcount.
- **Never make them feel like filler.** If someone only comes when you are at seven, they will eventually notice. Invite people when you are at ten too.

## What did not work?

A short list, because the failures were instructive.

| What we tried | What happened |
|---|---|
| Moving the night to suit a popular player | Lost two regulars, gained the one. Net loss, and the message was that the night was negotiable. |
| Prize support to boost turnout | A spike for two weeks, then back to baseline, with a slightly more competitive table. We replaced it with a traveling trophy of no value. |
| Posting in big regional Magic groups | A trickle of one-time visitors who wanted a store event. Local, personal invitations outperformed every broadcast. |
| Themed weeks every week | Fun, but novelty became expected and a plain draft felt like a letdown. We kept themes to about one a month. |
| Starting late to wait for stragglers | Punished the people who came on time. Starting on the minute, every time, was better for attendance than waiting ever was. |

## What should the host do differently from everyone else?

The host's week is different from a player's week. Ours looked roughly like this.

1. **Two days before:** the standard post goes up. Same words as last week.
2. **The day before:** a personal message to anyone new from the last month, and to any regular who has missed two in a row. Not a guilt trip. "Hope to see you Thursday."
3. **The afternoon of:** a one-line headcount in the channel. "Nine in so far, room for a second pod."
4. **The night:** packs dealt before the first player arrives. Start on time. End on time.
5. **The day after:** records posted, one or two photos, one sentence about a memorable deck. This is the post that makes the people who missed it want to come next week.

None of this takes more than an hour a week. All of it compounds.

## What about when the host cannot make it?

This is the question that decides whether you have a group or a hobby. For most of our three years, the night depended on one person and one cube, and when that person had a rough season, the night went quiet. We paused. We are now relaunching as a branch of The Ludarium, and the first thing we changed was that the night no longer depends on one person's calendar. How we did that is its own article: [relaunching a game community](/articles/relaunching-a-game-community/).

The short version: name a second host early, give them a key or a cube, and let them run a night while you are still around to help. Do it before you need it.

## FAQ

### How many players do you need for a cube draft?

Eight is ideal for a booster draft. Six or seven works. With four or five, switch to a two-player or small-group format such as Winston or grid draft rather than cancelling.

### How often should a cube group meet?

Weekly, on a fixed night, if you can sustain it. A fixed weekly night becomes a habit; a floating schedule becomes a negotiation every week.

### Should I cancel a draft night if only five people show up?

No. Play something smaller. Cancelling teaches the group that the night is conditional, and attendance drops further the following week.

### How big should a cube group's Discord be?

Size matters less than the core. A reliable six regulars plus a trustworthy weekly headcount produces eight more often than a large, passive server does.
