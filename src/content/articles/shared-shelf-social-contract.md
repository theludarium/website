A shared board game collection runs on trust, and trust runs better with a few things said out loud. Once your group adopts the One Copy Rule, your games stop being four private collections and become one library in several homes. That library needs a short, plain social contract so that a spilled drink or a cross-country move does not turn into a grudge.

This is the fourth article in The Shared Shelf series from The Ludarium. It covers the awkward questions groups avoid until they have to answer them: who stores the big game, what happens to lost pieces, who pays for the ruined board, what to do about the friend who never hosts or the one who always buys, and how to handle it when someone leaves. At the end there is a sample charter you can copy.

If you are new to the idea, [The One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/) explains why a group only needs one copy of each game. This article assumes you are already sharing.

## Who stores the big games?

Big boxes should live where they get played. If one person hosts most of the group's game nights, the heavy strategy and campaign games belong at their place, regardless of who paid for them. [Gloomhaven](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01LZXVN4P?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) does not want to ride in a trunk every other Thursday.

That raises an ownership question: can a game live at a house that is not its owner's? Yes, and it is normal. Ownership and storage are two different things on a Shared Shelf. Write down both. The audit template in [How to Audit Your Group's Game Shelf](/articles/audit-your-group-shelf/) has a column for each.

Two habits keep this clean:

- The owner can take a game home at any time, no explanation needed. It is still theirs.
- The host keeps the games they store in playable condition: dry, upright, and not under the cat's bed.

Small and portable games (fillers, two-player titles) usually stay with their owners and travel in a bag. That is one reason [Claim a Lane](/articles/claim-a-lane/) pairs those lanes with the people who host least.

## What happens when pieces go missing?

Pieces go missing. A meeple falls behind the couch, a card ends up in the wrong box, a token goes home in someone's pocket. This is not a crisis, and the contract should say so.

The practical approach:

1. **Whoever notices, says so.** Silence is how a missing piece becomes a missing set.
2. **Whoever hosted the night it went missing does the first sweep.** Under the table, in the couch, in the other boxes.
3. **If it does not turn up, the group replaces it.** Most publishers sell replacement parts cheaply, and a lot of pieces can be substituted from another game or a hobby store.
4. **Nobody pays alone for a lost piece.** Pieces are a cost of play. The group splits it, or the owner eats it, but no one gets handed a bill for a single meeple.

A group that argues over a lost token will not share a shelf for long. A group that laughs and orders a replacement will share one for a decade.

## Who pays when a game gets ruined?

Lost pieces are small. A full glass of red wine across a [Wingspan](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07YQ641NQ?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) board is not. The contract needs a line for real damage.

The fair rule is simple: **the person who caused the damage offers to replace the game, and the owner decides whether to accept.** Most owners say "do not worry about it" for a bent card and "yes, please" for a soaked board. The offer is what matters. It signals that the group takes the library seriously.

Two refinements:

- For a game that was already old and well-loved, replacement can mean a used copy or a group chip-in rather than one person buying new.
- For an expansion-heavy game, replacement means the base game only, unless the expansions were also ruined.

And one prevention rule that costs nothing: drinks go on a side table, not the game table. Every group learns this once. The contract just saves you the lesson.

## What about the friend who never hosts?

Every group has one. They always show up, they are a joy at the table, and their apartment has never once held a game night. Sometimes the reason is a tiny place, roommates, kids, or a long commute. Sometimes it is just habit.

The contract should separate the two things people conflate here: hosting and contributing. A member who cannot host can still contribute in ways that matter.

- **Own the portable lanes.** Fillers and two-player games travel. The non-host can be the one who always has three small games in a bag.
- **Be the sweeper.** Arrive early to help set up, stay late to help sort pieces back into the right bags.
- **Fund an expansion.** If the host owns the group's [Carcassonne](https://link.amazon/B012Rr5ed), the non-host buys the next expansion for it. This is the heart of [Gifting as Library Strategy](/articles/gifting-as-library-strategy/).
- **Bring the snacks.** Not a joke. Hosting costs money and time, and groceries are a real contribution.

Say this out loud so the non-host does not feel guilty and the host does not feel used. That conversation, once, saves months of quiet resentment.

## What about the friend who always buys?

The opposite problem is the member who buys everything. They love the hobby, they have the budget, and every month there is a new box at their house. On a Shared Shelf this can go two ways. Handled well, the group has a generous curator. Handled badly, three people feel like guests in one person's collection.

A few lines in the contract fix this:

- **Buying does not buy votes.** What gets played is decided by the table, not by who owns the most boxes.
- **The heavy buyer still follows the lanes.** If they own the strategy lane, they buy strategy games. They do not also buy the party games and the co-ops, because that leaves nothing for anyone else to contribute.
- **The group thanks them.** Explicitly, regularly. A generous buyer who feels appreciated keeps being generous. One who feels taken for granted stops.

## What happens when someone moves or the group dissolves?

This is the question groups most want to skip, and the one most worth answering early.

**When one person moves away:** their games go with them. Full stop. They bought them, they own them. The group loses that lane and decides whether to replace it. Under the One Copy Rule this is the expected cost of sharing: one replacement copy later is still cheaper than four copies up front. Two things make the departure easier:

- The leaver can offer to sell or gift games the group plays constantly and they rarely will. Many do.
- Any game that was bought as a group gift, or with a chip-in, should be discussed. The default is that it stays with whoever the gift was given to, but the group can agree otherwise.

**When the group dissolves:** everyone takes their own games home. Group-funded games and expansions go to whoever contributed most, or get sold with the money split. Do this with a list, not from memory. If you have been keeping the pooled shelf up to date, the list already exists.

The bigger point is that a Shared Shelf gives a departing friend a reason to stay in touch. "Can I borrow [Spirit Island](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01MUHP51S?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) when you are in town?" is a text people are happy to get.

## A sample Group Shelf Charter

Here is a short charter you can copy, adapt, and paste into the group chat. Seven agreements is plenty. More than ten and nobody reads it.

1. **One copy per group.** We buy one copy of a game for the group, not one each. Before buying, we check the pooled shelf.
2. **Ownership stays with the buyer.** Whoever paid for a game owns it and can take it home whenever they like. Storage and ownership are separate; we track both.
3. **Big games live at the host's.** Heavy boxes stay where we usually play. Small games travel with their owners.
4. **Lost pieces are a group cost.** Whoever notices speaks up, the host sweeps, and we replace parts together. Nobody pays alone for a meeple.
5. **Real damage gets an offer.** If you ruin a game, you offer to replace it. The owner decides whether to accept.
6. **Everyone contributes something.** Hosting, owning a lane, funding expansions, or bringing food all count. Not hosting is not a failing.
7. **Buying does not buy control.** The table picks what we play. The heaviest buyer still follows their lane.
8. **Leaving is clean.** If you move, your games go with you. Group-gifted games stay with the recipient unless we agree otherwise. We keep the pooled list current so a split is never a fight.

Put it at the top of the group chat, or on the first page of your audit. Then forget about it until you need it, which is exactly what a good contract is for.

The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### What are good rules for sharing board games with friends?

Agree on a few plain points: one copy per group, ownership stays with the buyer, big games live at the usual host's place, lost pieces are a group cost, real damage gets an offer to replace, everyone contributes something (hosting, owning a lane, or funding expansions), and games leave with their owner if someone moves. Write it down once.

### Who is responsible if a borrowed board game gets damaged?

The person who caused the damage should offer to replace the game, and the owner decides whether to accept. For minor wear, most owners decline. For a soaked board or a torn box, replacement is fair, and it can be a used copy or a group chip-in. Keeping drinks off the game table prevents most of this.

### What happens to shared board games when someone leaves the group?

Their games go with them, since they paid for them. The group then decides whether to buy one replacement for the lane that left. Games bought as group gifts stay with the recipient by default. A pooled list of who owns what, kept current, makes the split quick and keeps it friendly.

### How do you handle a friend who never hosts game night?

Separate hosting from contributing. A member who cannot host can own the portable lanes (fillers, two-player games), arrive early to set up, fund expansions for games the host owns, or bring food. Say this out loud so the non-host does not feel guilty and the host does not feel taken for granted.
