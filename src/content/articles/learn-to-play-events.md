A learn to play event does one thing a regular game night cannot: it gets twenty people past the hardest part of a game, the first play, in a single evening. Most games that people own and never play were never played because nobody at the table knew the rules and nobody wanted to be the one to read them. A learn-to-play night removes that barrier for a whole room at once.

This guide covers choosing the game, teaching it once to everyone instead of five times to five tables, staffing, and the small design choice that sends people home able to teach it themselves. That last part is the point. A learn-to-play night that produces twenty players is good. One that produces twenty teachers is how a game spreads.

## Which games work for a learn-to-play night?

Not every game. The right game has three properties.

**Medium weight.** Light games do not need a learn-to-play night; people can read the back of the box. Heavy games take too long to teach and too long to play. The sweet spot is a game that takes ten to fifteen minutes to teach and 60 to 90 minutes to play: Wingspan <!-- AFFILIATE -->, Catan <!-- AFFILIATE -->, Ticket to Ride <!-- AFFILIATE --> for a very new crowd, Pandemic <!-- AFFILIATE -->, 7 Wonders <!-- AFFILIATE -->, Carcassonne <!-- AFFILIATE -->, Everdell <!-- AFFILIATE -->, Splendor <!-- AFFILIATE -->.

**Demand.** The game people have heard of and have not played. Wingspan and Catan fill a room; a brilliant game nobody has heard of fills a table. Save the obscure ones for the group that already comes.

**Multiple copies available.** You need one copy per table of four. For a room of twenty, that is five copies. Borrow from the group, ask the local game store, or pick a game the regulars already own.

A learn-to-play night also works for a tabletop RPG, where the "game" is a 90-minute one-shot run at every table by a different game master. That is a different animal with its own guide: see [drop-in RPG nights](/articles/drop-in-rpg-night/).

## How do you teach one game to a whole room?

The mistake is teaching table by table. Five teachers at five tables give five slightly different explanations, start at five different times, and finish teaching twenty minutes apart. Instead, teach once, from the front, with every table set up and following along.

**Set up every table identically before people arrive.** Same starting layout, same components in the same places. When you say "look at the bird card in front of you," everyone is looking at the same thing.

**Teach from a table everyone can see.** Stand at a table near the front of the room with the game set up. If the room is big, a phone camera pointed at the table and cast to a screen works. Describe what you are doing while you do it.

**Follow the standard teaching order.** Our full guide to [how to teach a board game](/articles/how-to-teach-a-board-game/) covers this in depth. In short: what the game is about and how you win, in one sentence; what a turn looks like; the two or three rules people get wrong; then play.

**Play the first two rounds together.** Everyone takes their first turn at the same time, with the teacher calling it. "Everyone, take one action. If you are not sure, play a bird or take food. Go." Then the second round. After that, release the tables.

Total teaching time for a medium game: fifteen minutes from the front plus ten minutes of guided play. Then the tables are on their own for an hour.

## What do the table helpers do?

One helper per two tables. They are not teaching; the front of the room did that. They answer the questions that come up in play, and there will be many.

Three rules for helpers.

1. **Answer the question asked, not the question you wish they had asked.** "Can I do this?" gets a yes or no, not a strategy lecture.
2. **Do not play unless a table is short.** A helper who sits down at one table abandons the other.
3. **Point to the rulebook.** When a question is a real edge case, open the book with the table and find it together. People who have seen the rulebook used once are willing to use it themselves.

Helpers should have played the game at least three times. That is the threshold for knowing which questions are coming.

## How do you make people able to teach it afterward?

This is the step most learn-to-play nights skip, and it is the one that matters.

**Hand out a one-page teach sheet.** One side of paper: the goal, a turn, the three commonly missed rules, and end-game scoring. People take it home. When they open the box with their family two weeks later, they have what they need.

**Say the structure out loud.** "Notice I told you how to win before I told you what a turn is. That is how you teach this to someone else." Naming the method makes it portable.

**End with a debrief, two minutes per table.** What was confusing? What did you get wrong? Those answers go on the next version of the teach sheet.

**Invite them to teach next time.** The best follow-up to a learn-to-play night is a second night where last month's learners sit as helpers. Say it before they leave.

## What does the evening look like?

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0:00 | Doors open; tables already set up; helpers greet and seat people, four to a table |
| 0:15 | Teach from the front, fifteen minutes |
| 0:30 | Two guided rounds, all tables together |
| 0:40 | Open play; helpers float |
| 1:45 | Games end; scoring walked through once from the front |
| 1:55 | Debrief and teach sheets |
| 2:00 | Done, or a second game for tables that want to go again |

Seat people as they arrive, not all at once. A table of four that has been sitting for ten minutes has talked, which makes the next two hours better.

## What goes wrong at a learn-to-play night?

**The teacher who knows too much.** Fifteen minutes becomes thirty-five because every rule gets its exception explained. Fix: the teach sheet is the script, and exceptions wait for a question.

**Tables that start at different times.** One table has played two rounds while another is still finding the bird cards. Fix: identical setup before arrival and guided first rounds.

**Mismatched tables.** Three people who have played before and one who has not, at the same table. Fix: ask at the door and seat new players together. A table of four new players with a helper nearby learns faster than a new player at a table of veterans, because nobody is embarrassed to ask.

**A game that is harder than advertised.** Fix: play it with your helpers a week before and time the teach. If it is over twenty minutes, pick a different game.

## FAQ

### What is a learn-to-play event?

An evening built around teaching one specific board game to everyone in the room at once, with set-up tables, one teacher at the front, and helpers to answer questions during play.

### How many people can learn a board game at once?

Twenty to twenty-four is comfortable: five or six tables of four, one teacher at the front, three helpers floating. Past thirty, split into two sessions or use a screen.

### What games are best for teaching to a group?

Medium-weight games people have heard of: Wingspan, Catan, Pandemic, 7 Wonders, Carcassonne, Everdell. They take ten to fifteen minutes to teach and about an hour to play.

### How long does a learn-to-play night take?

About two hours: fifteen minutes to teach, ten minutes of guided play, an hour of open play, and a short debrief.
