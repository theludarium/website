Cube maintenance is the part of owning a cube nobody warns you about. The build is a project with an end. The upkeep is a practice with none. Six or more sets a year, each with a handful of cards that might be better than something in the list; a group whose tastes shift; a format whose consensus moves under you. The cube that was finished in March is behind by October, and the owner who does not accept that either burns out trying to keep up or quietly stops.

This is about how to run a cube as a thing that is never done, without it becoming a second job. Most of it is about deciding in advance how much the cube is allowed to change, and then holding to that.

## Why is a cube never finished?

Three forces, and none of them stop.

**New cards.** Wizards releases several Standard sets a year plus supplemental products, and to many owners it feels as though each one brings more cube-relevant cards than sets used to. Even an owner who wants only two new cards per set is looking at twelve or more swaps a year, every year.

**The group.** The people who draft the cube change what they want from it. A group that loved the storm deck in year one is tired of it in year three. New regulars arrive with new favorite decks. The eight people who were there at the start are five by year four, and three new ones have different histories with the game. The cube that fit the first group is slightly wrong for the current one, always.

**The owner.** Your own view of what makes a good cube moves. You read something about interaction density, or you draft someone else's cube and come home with ideas. The list you built reflects what you thought two years ago, and you do not think that anymore.

None of these are problems to solve. They are the conditions the cube lives in. The design question is how to respond without the response being the whole hobby.

## What cadence works?

A cadence is the single most useful tool for cube upkeep, because it turns "should I change this" from a constant question into a scheduled one.

A rhythm that works for a weekly group, offered as a house guideline:

| When | What | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| After each draft | Notes only. Which cards wheeled, which never got cast, which deck nobody could build. | 0 swaps |
| Monthly | Review notes. Make swaps that the notes support. | 3 to 8 swaps |
| After each set release | Test two to four cards from the set that fit existing slots. | 2 to 4 swaps |
| Yearly | Full audit: every section against its slot plan, lanes against their counts, the purpose statement against the current group. | 10 to 30 swaps |

The key number is the monthly one. Three to eight swaps is enough to keep the cube moving and few enough that regulars can track what changed. More than ten changes at once, and the drafters lose the cube they learned. The mechanics of the swap itself, keeping sections balanced and the changelog current, are in [how to update a cube](/articles/how-to-update-a-cube/).

Between the scheduled points, resist. A card can go on the watch list the moment it is spoiled. It does not go in the cube until the next scheduled swap, and it does not go in at all until it has been tested against the card it would replace.

## What belongs on the watch list?

The watch list is where ideas go to wait, and it is the thing that makes the cadence survivable. Without it, every good idea feels urgent. With it, every good idea has a place to sit until its turn.

Ours is a spreadsheet with four columns: candidate card, the card it would replace, the reason, and the date added. A few rules for keeping it honest:

1. **Every candidate names its replacement.** "Add Fable of the Mirror-Breaker" is not an entry. "Fable of the Mirror-Breaker for Goblin Rabblemaster, because Rabblemaster has been wheeling and Fable serves the same slot plus the sacrifice lane" is an entry.
2. **Entries expire.** If a candidate has been on the list for six months and has not gone in, it probably should not. Delete it or write down why it is still waiting.
3. **Cuts go on it too.** A card that has been last-picked three drafts running gets an entry as a cut candidate, with the slot it would open. Half the value of the watch list is the cuts, which owners otherwise never make.
4. **The list is not the cube.** Thirty candidates on the watch list is normal. Thirty swaps in a month is not. The list is for thinking; the cadence is for acting.

## How do you handle a new set?

New sets are the main pressure on perpetual design, and the honest answer is that most of each set is not for your cube. A method:

- **Spoiler season: read, do not add.** Put anything plausible on the watch list with its replacement named. Most of these entries will be deleted.
- **Release plus one month: look at the data.** CubeCobra and the public reference lists will have started to show which cards are actually getting adopted in cubes like yours. The spoiler-season hype card and the actually-good cube card are often different cards.
- **Test two to four.** Put them in for a month. Watch pick order and whether they end up in finished decks.
- **Keep what earned it.** Usually one or two. Sometimes zero. Zero is a fine number.

The failure mode is treating each set as a mandatory update. A cube that adds six cards per set adds close to forty cards a year, and in three years it is a different cube, drifted toward whatever the current design philosophy happens to be. This is how cubes lose their identity without anyone deciding to change it.

## When should you freeze a cube?

Sometimes the right answer to perpetual design is to stop. A frozen cube is one the owner has declared finished: no new cards after a chosen date, maintenance limited to replacing lost or damaged cards.

Freezing makes sense when:

- The cube has a purpose that new cards do not serve. A set cube, a pre-Modern cube, a cube whose point is a specific era.
- The owner has found the list they love and wants to stop the drift. "This is the cube" is a legitimate design decision.
- The group is occasional rather than weekly, so freshness matters less than familiarity.
- Maintenance has stopped being fun. A cube the owner resents updating is a cube that will get neglected anyway; better to freeze it on purpose than let it rot by accident.

A frozen cube is not a dead one. It is drafted, enjoyed, and known. Some of the most loved cubes in the hobby are ones that stopped changing. What it gives up is the ability to absorb the group's shifting tastes, which is why a weekly cube for a growing community probably should not freeze, and a cube for eight old friends probably could.

A middle option: freeze the structure and let the cards move. The lanes, the counts, the exclusions, and the purpose are fixed; only the individual cards within slots change. Many long-running group cubes settle here: the skeleton stays for years while the skin changes every month.

## How do you keep upkeep from becoming a job?

A few things that have worked for us and for owners we know.

**Share it.** A changelog that drafters read turns maintenance from a chore into a conversation. Post the month's swaps in the group's channel with one line of reasoning each. People will argue, which is the point.

**Delegate the notes.** Ask one drafter per night to be the person who writes down what wheeled. Rotate. It takes five minutes and it produces better data than the owner's memory.

**Let the cube be imperfect.** The slot that is a little weak, the lane that is 14 cards instead of 15, the card that is probably past its rate. These are fine. The cube does not need to be correct. It needs to be drafted.

**Keep the purpose statement visible.** When the watch list has thirty entries and all of them feel urgent, reread the one sentence that says what the cube is for. Most of the entries will stop feeling urgent. We wrote about that sentence in [what is your cube for](/articles/what-is-your-cube-for/).

## FAQ

### How often should I update my cube?

A workable cadence is notes after every draft, three to eight swaps monthly, two to four tested cards per new set, and a full audit once a year. That is a house guideline, not a standard.

### How many cards should I add from a new set?

Usually one or two, sometimes none. Put candidates on a watch list, wait a month for data, test a few, and keep only what earned a slot.

### Is it okay to stop updating a cube?

Yes. A frozen cube is a legitimate design choice, especially for a set cube, an era cube, or an occasional group. Decide to freeze it rather than letting it drift into neglect.

### What is a cube watch list?

A running list of candidate cards, each paired with the card it would replace and a reason. It lets ideas wait for the next scheduled update instead of forcing constant changes.

## Tend it, do not chase it

A cube is a garden, not a building. It is never done, and it does not need to be. Set a cadence, keep a watch list, let most new cards pass, and decide on purpose whether the list is allowed to move. The game is the reason. The people are the point.
