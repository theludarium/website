Knowing how to run a cube draft well is mostly knowing how to manage time and people. The cards take care of themselves. A good cube night fits eight players into about three hours: forty-five minutes to draft, twenty to build, and three rounds of fifty minutes with a little slack. The host's job is to keep that schedule without anyone feeling rushed, and to make the evening feel like something people want to repeat.

I have hosted cube nights that ran until one in the morning and cube nights that ended at ten with everyone asking about next week. The difference was never the card list. It was the hosting.

## How much time does a cube night need?

Plan for three hours and tell people three hours. Here is a schedule that holds up.

| Segment | Time | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Arrival and setup | 15 min | Packs already dealt, lands already out |
| Draft (3 packs of 15) | 40 to 45 min | About 50 seconds per pick average |
| Deck building | 20 min | Firm; call "two minutes" at eighteen |
| Round 1 | 50 min | Best of three, or best of one for a faster night |
| Round 2 | 50 min | |
| Round 3 | 50 min | Optional; skip if people are fading |
| Teardown | 10 min | Everyone sorts their deck back by color |

That is roughly three and a half hours with three rounds, three hours with two. The fourth round that many tournament players expect is what turns a pleasant evening into a slog. Two or three rounds, then home.

If you want the whole thing shorter, best-of-one matches cut each round to twenty-five minutes. The games are swingier but the night ends at a reasonable hour, which matters more than people admit.

## What should be ready before anyone arrives?

The single biggest time-saver is dealing the packs in advance. Shuffle the cube, deal twenty-four piles of fifteen, and stack them in three sets of eight. When players sit down, pack one is already in front of them.

The rest of the checklist:

- **Basic lands sorted by type**, at least twenty of each, in a spot everyone can reach.
- **Tokens and dice**, a small pile per table.
- **Pen and paper or a pairing app** for match results.
- **Deck boxes or rubber bands** so people can carry their forty cards between rounds.
- **A clock everyone can see.** Phones work, but a visible timer keeps the room honest.
- **Table space.** Eight drafters need a long table or two square ones pushed together. Playing needs four two-person spaces. Our piece on [what makes a great game table](/articles/best-table-for-board-games/) applies here too.
- **Food that does not touch cards.** Dry snacks, lids on drinks, napkins everywhere.

## How do you seat the table?

Seating is the quiet lever hosts forget.

Mix experience levels around the circle rather than clustering. A new player between two veterans gets help passively. Two new players next to each other pass each other confused picks all night.

If you have regulars who always fight over the same colors, separate them by three or four seats. It will not stop the fight, but it will make it less personal.

Change the seating every week. Draft signals depend on who passes to whom, and a table that sits in the same order every time develops habits that feel like rules. Random seating, drawn from a cup, keeps it fresh and removes any suggestion that the host is arranging things.

## How do you run the draft itself?

Call the pick timing out loud for the first few picks so everyone settles into a rhythm. "Pick one. Pick two." After five or six picks, the table finds its pace and you can stop.

Set a norm for slow pickers. A draft where one person takes two minutes per pick while seven people wait is not fun for seven people. The norm I use: forty seconds, then the player to your left can politely say "pass." Most tables never need to enforce it. The existence of the rule is enough.

Enforce the direction change. Pack one passes left, pack two right, pack three left. Someone always forgets. Say it at the start of each pack.

Do not allow talking about picks during the draft. Table talk about what is in the packs ruins the signaling that makes drafting interesting. Talking about anything else is fine and encouraged. If your group is new to all of this, [cube draft strategy](/articles/cube-draft-strategy/) will give them something to think about between picks.

## How do you pair rounds fairly?

Three good options, from simplest to most structured.

**Random pairings each round.** Draw names from a cup. Fast, friendly, no math. You might play the same person twice. Nobody cares at a casual night.

**Swiss pairings.** Round one is random. After that, winners play winners and losers play losers. Everyone plays a close match and there is a clear "winner" at the end. Requires a pairing app or a sheet of paper.

**Pod play.** Split into two groups of four and play a mini round-robin. Each player plays the three others in their pod. Good for a group that wants everyone to play everyone.

Whichever you choose, decide before the night and say it when people arrive. Changing the format mid-evening is the fastest way to make a competitive player feel cheated.

Should there be prizes? For most groups, no. A prize changes the mood from "let's play" to "let's win," and the people who care about winning will care more than the people who came to hang out. If you want something, make it a joke trophy that gets passed around and signed. Bragging rights last longer than a booster pack.

## What rituals make people come back?

A regular cube night is a habit, and habits need anchors.

- **Same night, same time.** Second and fourth Thursdays, seven o'clock. People plan around a fixed point. "Whenever we can get eight" never gets eight.
- **A standing invitation.** Make it clear that showing up with no cards and no experience is fine. The cube is the collection. Nobody needs their own.
- **A post-draft debrief.** Five minutes at the end where people say what they drafted and what they would do differently. It builds shared memory of the cube.
- **The change log.** Announce card swaps before each draft. "Out: the green giant nobody picks. In: a two-mana elf." People love watching the cube evolve.
- **End on time.** Call the last round when you said you would. The night people remember fondly is the one that ended while they still wanted more.

## What goes wrong, and how do you handle it?

**Seven players show up.** Draft seven, with packs of fifteen; the last card in each pack is set aside. Or play pods of four and three.

**Six players.** Draft six with packs of fifteen and let people take two cards per pick for the last few. Or shrink to nine cards per pack and five packs. Both work.

**Four players.** Deal five packs of nine each, or draft with 180 cards and three packs of fifteen.

**Two players.** Winston draft or grid draft. Both are quick and make a fine warm-up.

**Someone is salty.** It happens. Acknowledge the bad beat, move on, make sure they get a good game next round. Salt that lingers past one round is a culture problem, and the host sets the culture.

**A rules dispute.** The cube owner is the judge. Decide quickly, note it for later if unsure, and keep playing. Our guide to [settling board game rules arguments](/articles/board-game-rules-argument/) applies word for word.

## FAQ

### How long does a cube draft take?

The draft itself takes about forty-five minutes for eight players. A full night with deck building and three rounds runs about three and a half hours.

### How many players do you need for a cube draft?

Eight is standard. Four to seven works with adjusted pack sizes, and two players can draft using Winston or grid draft.

### Should a cube night have prizes?

Most casual groups do better without them. Prizes shift the mood toward winning and away from playing.

### How do you pair players for cube draft rounds?

Random pairings are simplest. Swiss pairings, where winners play winners, give closer matches and a clear finish.
