Azul strategy has a reputation for being gentle, because the tiles are pretty and nobody attacks you. The reputation is wrong. Azul is a game of forcing other people to take tiles they do not want, and the player who understands that wins most of the time against players who are just making a nice wall.

This guide covers the original Azul. The sequels (Stained Glass of Sintra, Summer Pavilion, Queen's Garden) share the drafting but score differently. <!-- AFFILIATE -->

## How does Azul scoring work?

You need the scoring to make any of the strategy make sense, and most casual players only half know it.

Each round, you draft tiles from factory displays into pattern lines on your board. The pattern lines hold one, two, three, four, and five tiles. At the end of the round, each full pattern line sends one tile to the matching row of your wall, and the leftover tiles in that line are discarded.

The placed tile scores based on its neighbors on the wall:

- A tile with no adjacent tiles scores one point.
- A tile that is part of a horizontal run scores the length of that run.
- A tile that is part of a vertical run scores the length of that run.
- A tile that is part of both scores both runs added together.

So a tile placed in the middle of a row of five with a column of five above and below it scores ten points on its own. A tile placed in a corner by itself scores one.

End-of-game bonuses: two points per complete horizontal row, seven per complete vertical column, ten per color that appears five times on your wall. The game ends after the round in which any player completes a horizontal row.

Any tiles you cannot or will not place go to your floor line, which costs you points: minus one, minus one, minus two, minus two, minus two, minus three, minus three, for a possible fourteen-point penalty in a single round.

## Why do columns matter more than rows in Azul?

The row bonus is two points. The column bonus is seven. And the first completed row ends the game, often before you want it to.

That is why strong players build columns. Specifically, they build the middle of the wall first, so each new tile touches tiles above, below, and beside it. Adjacency scoring compounds. A wall that grows out from the center in a block scores more per tile every round, while a wall that grows along one row scores a flat two or three per tile.

A rough comparison of two approaches:

| | Row-first player | Column-and-block player |
|---|---|---|
| Early rounds | Fast, simple, few floor penalties | Slower, more careful drafting |
| Per-tile scoring | Two to five points | Four to ten points by mid-game |
| End bonuses | Two per row | Seven per column, ten per color |
| Ends the game | Early, on their terms | Later, when they are ahead |
| Loses to | Anyone who out-scores them before the row finishes | Being starved of the colors they need |

The row-first player can win by ending the game while ahead. That requires being ahead, and it rarely holds up against a block builder past the third round.

## How should you draft in Azul?

Each turn, you take all tiles of one color from one factory display or from the center, and the rest of that display slides to the center. The first player to take from the center in a round also takes the first-player marker, which goes to their floor line as a one-point penalty but gives them the first pick next round.

Drafting principles:

1. **Take exact counts.** A pattern line of three wants three tiles. Taking four means one goes to the floor. Taking two means the line stays open another round and may never fill.
2. **Watch what you leave behind.** When you take two blue tiles from a display holding two blue, one red, and one yellow, the red and yellow go to the center. If the next player needs red, you fed them. If nobody needs red, you have started building a pile that someone will eventually have to eat.
3. **Count the colors.** There are [twenty tiles of each color](https://www.dover.nh.gov/Assets/government/city-operations/library/borrow/library-of-things/azul-rulebook.pdf) in the bag. If you need three red for a line and red has been scarce all game, do not open a four-line in red hoping for the best.
4. **Know what you cannot place.** Once a color is on a wall row, you cannot put that color in that row's pattern line again. Players forget this and end up with a pattern line they can never legally fill.

## When is a floor line penalty worth taking in Azul?

Often. The floor line is a cost, not a disaster, and players who refuse to touch it lose to players who use it on purpose.

Take a penalty when:

- **The first-player marker is worth it.** Going first next round lets you take the best display before anyone else. One point is cheap for that in a tight round, especially when the center has piled up.
- **Taking the hit now prevents a worse one later.** If the center holds six yellow tiles and you are the only player who can still use yellow, you can wait, but the player who gets stuck last with no legal placement eats all six. Take three now, place them, and let someone else face the pile.
- **It completes a scoring line.** Taking five tiles for a four-line, with one spilling to the floor, costs one point and gains the tile placement plus adjacency. That is almost always worth it.

Do not take a penalty for a line that will not complete this round. A pattern line that stays half full across rounds is dead space on your board.

## How does denial work in Azul?

Denial means taking tiles you do not need because an opponent needs them more, or leaving tiles in the center so an opponent is forced to take a penalty.

The direct version is simple: if the player to your left has an open four-line in black and there are exactly four black tiles in one display, take them, or take enough that they cannot fill the line. Put the extras on your floor if you must. A two-point penalty to you that costs them a ten-point placement is a good trade.

The indirect version is more common and more effective. Late in a round, the center fills with whatever nobody wanted. The last player to act often has to take a large pile of one color with nowhere to put it. You can engineer this by:

- Taking small picks early so the center grows.
- Leaving a single color in a large stack that only your opponent cannot place.
- Timing the first-player marker so your opponent ends the round, not you.

At two players, denial is nearly the whole game, because every tile you do not take goes to the one person you are trying to beat. At four, it is a weapon you use once or twice a round, not a plan.

## How do you control the end of the game?

The game ends after the round in which any player completes a horizontal row. That player is not necessarily the winner.

If you are ahead and have a nearly complete row, finish it. If you are behind, do everything you can to keep every row open, including your own, so that the block builder's adjacency keeps growing for another round. If an opponent is one tile from a row, deny that color.

In the last round, every tile placement should be checked against the bonuses. A tile that completes a column is worth seven plus its adjacency. A tile that is the fifth of its color is worth ten plus adjacency. Those two bonuses decide close games far more often than the per-round scoring.

Azul is a thirty-minute game that rewards attention to other people's boards as much as your own, which is a good description of most things worth doing at a table. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### What is the best opening move in Azul?

Take a pick that fills a short pattern line exactly and places a tile near the center of your wall, where future tiles can touch it on multiple sides. Avoid the corners early.

### Should you take the first-player marker in Azul?

Yes, when the one-point cost buys you the first choice of a round where the displays are rich, or when taking from the center also fills a line you need. No, when it is just the least bad option late in a round and you are already losing points.

### How many points is a good Azul score?

There is no published benchmark. Experienced players tend to finish well above beginners because they complete more rows and columns, but beating the score matters less than beating the board; plenty of games are won with modest totals.

### Can you place a tile in a pattern line if its color is already on that wall row?

No. Once a color is on a wall row, that color can never go in that row's pattern line again. Plan your pattern lines around this early.
