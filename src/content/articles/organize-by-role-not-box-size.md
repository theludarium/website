Walk into most gaming homes and you will find the same shelf. Big boxes on the bottom because they are heavy. Medium boxes in the middle. Small boxes jammed in wherever they fit, usually in a pile on top of something else. It looks tidy. It tells you nothing.

If you want to organize board games in a way that changes how often you play, stop sorting by the box and start sorting by the job. Every game on your shelf plays a role on game night: it opens the evening, fills a gap, serves a couple, handles a crowd, or anchors a long Saturday. A shelf arranged by those roles answers the only question that matters at 7 p.m. with four people in the living room: "what do we have for tonight?"

This article is the pillar of our Library Science for Gamers series, where we borrow a few ideas from how libraries think about books and apply them to the shelf in your living room. It builds on the habit at the center of everything we write, [The One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/): a play group needs one copy of a game, not one per person. Organizing by role is how you see what that one copy is for.

## Why organizing board games by box size hides what you own

A shelf sorted by size is sorted by a fact that has nothing to do with play. Box dimensions are a packaging decision made in a factory. They do not tell you how long the game takes, how many people it seats, or whether your brother-in-law will enjoy it.

The result is a shelf you have to scan end to end every time you choose something. Codenames is "somewhere on the small shelf." Pandemic is "one of the square ones." Choosing becomes a memory test instead of a lookup, and memory tests favor the games you already play most. The rest drift toward the [Shelf of Shame](/articles/shelf-of-shame-infinite-cost/), where unplayed games sit at an infinite cost per hour.

There is a second, quieter problem. A size-sorted shelf cannot show you patterns. If you own four light card games that all play in fifteen minutes, they are scattered across the shelf by box shape, and the overlap never jumps out at you. You keep buying a fifth one because nothing told you the role was covered.

Libraries solved this a long time ago. Nobody shelves books by height. They shelve by subject, so the gaps in the collection are visible to anyone walking the aisle. Your shelf deserves the same treatment.

## What are the roles a board game plays on game night?

We use seven roles. The first six come from our [starter shelf](/articles/starter-shelf-40-to-300/) framework, where the rule is that no two games fill the same role. The seventh covers games that span many sessions.

1. **Gateway.** The game you hand to someone who has never played a modern board game. Teachable in five minutes, forgiving, fun on the first play. Ticket to Ride and Carcassonne live here.
2. **Filler.** Under 20 minutes. Played while you wait for the last person to arrive, or as a closer when there is not time for anything else. Sushi Go!, Love Letter, and Jaipur are classic fillers.
3. **2-player.** Games that are built for exactly two, or are at their best at two. Patchwork, Hive, Jaipur again. The role matters because two-person nights happen far more often than most shelves are ready for.
4. **Party.** Six or more players, low rules overhead, lots of talking. Codenames, Just One, Wavelength.
5. **Co-op.** Everyone on the same side. Pandemic, The Crew, Spirit Island. This is the role that rescues a night when someone at the table hates losing.
6. **Stretch.** One step up in complexity from your group's comfort zone. The game that makes the group better. Wingspan or Terraforming Mars for a group that has outgrown Catan.
7. **Campaign.** Games that continue across sessions: Gloomhaven, a Sherlock Holmes Consulting Detective casebook, a long-running Magic Cube. These need their own space because a half-finished campaign is a different object from a game you can pull down on any night.

A game can qualify for more than one role. Jaipur is a filler and a 2-player game. The Crew is a co-op and a filler. That is fine. You shelve it under the role your group actually uses it for, and you note the second role on the label.

## How to label and arrange a role-based board game shelf

The method takes an afternoon. You will need your games, painter's tape or label stock, a marker, and a clear floor.

### Step 1: Pull everything off the shelf

Yes, everything. Sorting in place does not work because the box sizes keep pulling you back toward the old arrangement. While each game is in your hands, ask: when did we last play this? If the answer is "never" or "more than two years ago," set it in a separate pile. You are not getting rid of it yet. You are just noticing.

### Step 2: Assign one primary role per game

Sort into seven stacks, one per role, based on your group's real behavior rather than the box copy. If your group only ever plays Azul as a closer after a long game, it is a filler for you even though the side of the box says 30 to 45 minutes. If you are unsure, ask: when do we reach for this?

Expect friction in two places. Some games will not fit any role cleanly, which usually means you do not play them. And some roles will have a stack three times taller than the others. Both are information.

### Step 3: Give each role a shelf zone and a label

Now put the games back, one role per zone. A zone can be a whole shelf, half a shelf, or a cube in a cube organizer. The label goes on the shelf edge, not on the game, and it should be readable from across the room: GATEWAY, FILLER, TWO, PARTY, CO-OP, STRETCH, CAMPAIGN.

Order the zones by the flow of an evening. Fillers and gateways at eye level near the door of the room, since those are the games you grab first and most often. Party games close by. Co-op and stretch a step lower. Campaigns on the bottom or in a closet, since they come out on planned nights, not spontaneous ones.

### Step 4: Add a one-line label to each box

On a strip of tape on the spine, write the role, the player count your group actually uses, and the real play time in your house: "FILLER / 3-5 / 15 min." The box prints a range. Your label records the truth. This takes 20 seconds per game and is the highest-value part of the whole project.

## The "what do we have for tonight?" test

Here is how you know the system works. Stand at the door of the room with a friend. Give them a scenario: "Five of us, one has never played anything, we have ninety minutes." They should be able to walk to the shelf and pull a reasonable answer in under thirty seconds without asking you a question.

Run it again with a different scenario. "Just the two of us, forty minutes." "Eight people, half of them are tired." "Four regulars who want something meaty."

If any scenario sends them scanning the whole shelf, the labels are not doing their job. Usually a zone is too crowded and needs splitting, a label is too vague, or a game is shelved under a role it does not really serve.

A shelf that passes the test is a shelf that gets played. You stop defaulting to the same three games, and guests stop needing your permission to pick something, because the shelf explains itself.

## How a role-based shelf makes duplicates and gaps visible

This is where organizing by role pays for itself in dollars, not just convenience.

When you sorted in step 2, one stack was probably tall. For most hobby shelves it is the filler or the stretch stack, and it means you have been buying the same role over and over. Five light card games that all play in 15 minutes with 3 to 5 people are not five different games from your group's point of view. They are one role with four understudies.

That is the moment to apply the [second-copy test](/articles/when-a-second-copy-is-worth-it/) in a slightly different form: does this extra game in the role create plays that would not otherwise happen? If the answer is no, it is a candidate for [trading or selling](/articles/trading-and-selling-games/), and the money it brings back can fund a role you are missing.

Because you will also see the empty zones. Most shelves have no real 2-player section and a thin co-op section, while the party zone is overstuffed with gifts. An empty or thin zone is a shopping list. It is also the only kind of shopping list that improves your collection, because you are buying to fill a hole rather than to add another understudy. If you are living with a [shelf cap](/articles/one-in-one-out-shelf-cap/), this is how you decide what comes in and what goes out.

Now extend the idea across your play group. If three friends all organize by role, you can compare zones in five minutes. Two people with a full stretch zone and nobody with a co-op zone is a solved problem: one person claims co-op as their lane, the others stop buying stretch games. That is the whole logic of [Claim a Lane](/articles/claim-a-lane/), and the role-based shelf is what makes it obvious. For a structured way to do the comparison, see [how to audit your group's game shelf](/articles/audit-your-group-shelf/), which includes a template.

## Physical shelf tips that actually matter

The internet is full of storage advice. Most of it is about aesthetics. These are the few things that affect whether games get played.

- **Vertical or horizontal?** Vertical (spine out, like books) uses less space and lets you read labels from across the room. The risk is that components shift and lids pop. Horizontal is safer for games with loose pieces and lets you stack by role. A reasonable compromise: vertical for games with bagged components, horizontal for games with fragile inserts.
- **Bag the components.** A handful of zip bags in a few sizes, sorted by player color or by component type, cuts setup time in half and makes vertical storage safe. This is a one-time twenty-minute job per game and it pays off every single play.
- **Inserts.** Foam or wood inserts are lovely and not necessary. Buy one for a campaign game you play weekly. Skip them for everything else; bags do the same job for a fraction of the cost.
- **Keep the role zone intact when a game leaves.** If a filler goes out on loan, leave a placeholder card so the zone still reads correctly and you remember where it went. For group libraries, this is part of the [shared shelf social contract](/articles/shared-shelf-social-contract/).

## A sample role-based shelf layout

Here is a layout for a typical single-bookcase collection of around 20 games, which is about where a mature [core library](/articles/core-library-300-to-1000/) lands for one household. Adjust the counts to your shelf, but keep the order.

| Shelf position | Role zone | Target count | Example games | Label on shelf edge |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Top (eye level) | Filler | 3-4 | Sushi Go!, Love Letter, The Crew | FILLER / under 20 min |
| Top (eye level) | Gateway | 2-3 | Ticket to Ride, Carcassonne, Catan | GATEWAY / teach in 5 |
| Second | Party | 2-3 | Codenames, Just One, Wavelength | PARTY / 6+ players |
| Second | 2-player | 2-3 | Patchwork, Hive, Jaipur | TWO / for two |
| Third | Co-op | 2 | Pandemic, Spirit Island | CO-OP / same side |
| Third | Stretch | 2-3 | Wingspan, Terraforming Mars, Root | STRETCH / 90+ min |
| Bottom or closet | Campaign | 1-2 | Gloomhaven, a Cube | CAMPAIGN / in progress |

Notice that the counts are small. Five or six well-chosen games cover most occasions; the rest of a shelf is depth within a role. Notice also that the layout is readable by a stranger. That is the standard: your shelf should work for a guest the same way a library works for someone who has never been in it.

## What changes once your shelf is organized by role

You play more of what you own, because games that had become invisible now sit in a labeled zone that gets scanned every game night. You buy differently, because "which zone does this go in, and is it already full?" usually answers itself, and the [cost per play](/articles/cost-per-play-explained/) math improves on its own. And you talk about the collection differently: "we need a co-op" is a sentence everybody can act on.

The game is the reason. The people are the point. A shelf that explains itself gets out of the way and lets the evening start.

## FAQ

### What is the best way to organize board games?

Organize board games by the role each one plays on game night rather than by box size or alphabet. Common roles are gateway, filler, 2-player, party, co-op, stretch, and campaign. Give each role its own labeled shelf zone, add a short spine label with player count and real play time, and keep the most-played roles at eye level.

### How do I organize a small board game collection?

With fewer than ten games, you still benefit from roles, just with smaller zones. Use a single shelf split into labeled sections with bookends or tape marks. The goal is the same: anyone should be able to answer "what can we play tonight?" by glancing at the shelf, without pulling every box down.

### Should board games be stored vertically or horizontally?

Vertical storage saves space and makes labels easy to read, but loose components can shift and pop lids. Horizontal storage is safer for games with fragile inserts or many small pieces. A practical rule: store games vertically once their components are bagged, and horizontally if they rely on a loose insert.

### How do I know if I have too many games in one category?

Sort your games into role piles. If one pile is three or four times taller than the others, you have been buying the same role repeatedly. Ask of each extra game whether it creates plays that would not otherwise happen. If not, it is a candidate to trade or sell, and the money can fill a role you are missing.

### How should a game group organize a shared collection?

Have each member organize their own shelf by role, then compare zones. Overlaps show where the group has bought duplicates; empty zones show what to buy next. Assign each member a lane so nobody buys into a role someone else already covers, and keep a simple shared list of who owns what.
