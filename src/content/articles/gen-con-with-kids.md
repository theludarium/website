Gen Con with kids is a different convention from Gen Con without them. The badge rules change, the schedule shrinks, and the Exhibit Hall becomes a place you pass through rather than live in. Done well, it is one of the best family weekends in Indianapolis. Done badly, it is a long, loud, expensive meltdown with dice.

We have three daughters and a hometown that hosts the show, so we have run both versions. Here is what we have learned, with the official rules linked.

## What are the badge rules for children?

This part is specific, and it matters at the door.

Gen Con's badge page says [children 11 and older must have a badge to attend](https://www.gencon.com/gen-con-indy/your_badge), at the same prices as adults. Children 10 and under can attend without a badge, but every child must be registered for a **Child Wristband** when you buy your own badge, and they must be with a parent or guardian who holds a valid badge.

The wristband carries the parent's name and lets the child explore the Exhibit Hall with you. It also lets you register them for events in the **Kid Activities** category. If your child wants to play in events outside that category, such as a regular board game demo or an RPG, they need a full badge in addition to the wristband.

Two practical notes:

- Register the wristband when you buy your badge, not later. It is tied to your account.
- Write your phone number on the inside of the child's arm or on a card in their pocket. The wristband has your name; a number is faster.

## How old is old enough?

There is no right answer, but there are patterns.

| Age | What tends to work | What tends not to |
|---|---|---|
| Under 5 | One short visit, stroller, the Block Party outside, leaving before nap time | The Exhibit Hall at peak, any ticketed event |
| 5 to 8 | Kid Activities events, a short Exhibit Hall loop, open gaming with family games | Four-hour days, waiting in lines |
| 9 to 12 | A full-day badge with two events, a budget to spend, demo tables | Late nights, heavy games with strangers |
| Teens | Their own schedule with check-in times, their own events | Being dragged to yours |

The honest rule: plan for half the hours you would do alone, then cut one more.

## What is there for kids to actually do?

More than first-timers expect.

- **Kid Activities events.** The event catalog has a KIDS category with crafts, simple games, and learn-to-play sessions. These are the events a wristband can register for. Book them in advance like any other event; our guide to [Gen Con event registration](/articles/gen-con-event-registration-tips/) explains the wish list.
- **Family-friendly demos in the Exhibit Hall.** Many publishers run demos of games that play in fifteen minutes. Look for the ones with big colorful components and small tables. Games like [Dragomino](https://link.amazon/B09WKyU19) or [My First Castle Panic](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07KMLHPMK?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) show up at publisher tables and play in a sitting.
- **Open gaming.** The open-gaming library has a shelf of family games, and a quiet corner of the hall is often the best hour of a kid's day.
- **Costumes.** Children love the cosplay. Budget time to stop and look, and teach the rule: ask before photographing.
- **The outdoor Block Party.** Gen Con closes [South Street, just south of the convention center](https://gencon.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/16797680652692-Gen-Con-Block-Party), for music, food trucks, and more, running from 9 a.m. to 10 p.m. Thursday through Saturday. For small kids, it is often the highlight.

## How do you plan the day?

A schedule that has worked for us with a seven-year-old and a ten-year-old:

1. **Breakfast at the hotel, early.** Arrive at the convention center when it opens, when the halls are emptiest.
2. **One Kid Activities event in the morning.** Ninety minutes, seated, with a thing to make or play.
3. **A short Exhibit Hall loop.** Thirty to forty-five minutes. Pick one aisle, let each child choose one demo, leave before anyone cries.
4. **Lunch out of the building.** Walk to a restaurant or back to the hotel. The break from noise is the point.
5. **Afternoon: open gaming or the hotel pool.** Not another event.
6. **Done by 4 p.m.** Dinner, Block Party if there is energy, bed.

That is a full day for a child. Adults who want more should take turns: one parent takes the kids back to the hotel while the other plays an evening event, then swap the next day.

## What should you bring?

Everything from the [Gen Con packing list](/articles/gen-con-packing-list/), plus:

- Snacks the child actually eats, more than you think
- A water bottle with their name on it
- Headphones or ear defenders for sensitive kids; the Exhibit Hall is loud
- A small backpack they carry themselves, with one toy or book for lines
- A stroller or carrier for under-fives, even if they usually walk
- A change of clothes for anyone under eight
- A photo of each child on your phone taken that morning, in that day's outfit

Leave the big game purchases for the last day, so nobody carries a box through the hall.

## What goes wrong?

The same things, every year, to every family.

**Overbooking.** Two ticketed events plus the Exhibit Hall plus a late dinner is an adult day. Kids break around hour five.

**The Exhibit Hall at 10 a.m. Saturday.** It is a crush. Go Thursday morning or Sunday afternoon.

**Lines.** Children do not understand why they are standing in a line for a demo. Skip anything with a line longer than ten people.

**Spending.** Give each child a set budget in cash on day one. It ends the negotiation at every booth.

**Losing a child.** It happens, even to careful parents, in a building that size. Agree on a meeting point before you walk in, make sure they know your phone number, and point out the staff shirts.

**Expecting them to love what you love.** A ten-year-old may not care about the new heavy euro. They will care about the dice vendor with a bin of a thousand colors.

## Is it worth it?

Yes, with the right expectations. The children we know who grew up going to Gen Con for one day a year talk about it the way other kids talk about the state fair. They remember the costumes, the dice, the one game they played with a stranger who was kind to them.

That is a reasonable thing to give a child: a weekend where thousands of adults are visibly, unselfconsciously playing. The lesson lands without a word.

If a full Gen Con is too much, Indianapolis has family-friendly places to play games year-round, and we keep a list in [family-friendly places to play games in Indianapolis](/articles/family-friendly-game-cafes-indianapolis/). Regional conventions like Origins in Columbus are also gentler for families; see our Midwest convention guide.

The game is the reason. The people are the point. For a child, the people are you, and a weekend of your full attention beats any event ticket.

## FAQ

### Do kids need a badge for Gen Con?

Children 11 and older need a full badge. Children 10 and under are [admitted free with an attending adult](https://www.gencon.com/attend/policies) and wear a Child Wristband, issued through the registration system or at a Customer Service or Badge Registration kiosk onsite, that carries the parent's contact information.

### Can my child attend regular events with a Child Wristband?

No. The wristband covers the Kid Activities category only. For other events, a child needs a regular badge in addition to the wristband.

### What is the best day to bring kids to Gen Con?

Thursday or Sunday. Both are quieter than Friday and Saturday, and Sunday single-day badges are the least expensive.

### Is there a quiet place at Gen Con for an overwhelmed child?

Open-gaming areas and hotel lobbies are usually calmer than the Exhibit Hall. Plan a break outside the building every two to three hours.
