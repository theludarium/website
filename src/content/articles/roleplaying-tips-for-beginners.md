The most useful roleplaying tips for beginners address a single moment: the GM turns to you, the table goes quiet, and your mind goes blank. You do not know what your character would do. You are not sure you know who your character is. Everyone is waiting.

This happens to every new player, and to plenty of experienced ones. It is not a sign that you are bad at this. It is a sign that nobody told you the small set of moves that always work. Here they are.

## Why does the blank moment happen?

Because "anything" is a bad menu. A board game gives you three or four legal moves, and choosing among them is easy. A tabletop RPG gives you infinite options, and infinity is paralyzing.

There is a second cause. New players think roleplaying means performing. They believe the right answer is a clever line delivered in a voice, and since they do not have one ready, they say nothing. But roleplaying is not acting. It is making decisions as someone else. A decision described in plain words ("he backs away from the door") is complete roleplaying. No voice required.

Once you stop looking for the perfect line and start looking for any decision, the blank moment shrinks to a second or two.

## What are the six moves that always work?

Memorize these. Any one of them is a legitimate, useful turn in any situation.

**1. Ask a question about the scene.** "What does the room smell like?" "Is the guard armed?" "Does anyone look nervous?" Questions are always legal. They give you information, they give the GM a chance to add detail, and they buy you ten seconds to think. Experienced players ask questions constantly.

**2. React to what just happened.** Someone else at the table just did something. Respond to it. If the fighter kicked the door in, your character flinches, or laughs, or steps through behind them. Reacting requires no invention. The scene provides the material.

**3. Do the obvious thing.** Attack the nearest enemy. Walk toward the noise. Open the door. Beginners feel that the obvious move is somehow a failure of imagination. It is not. The obvious move keeps the story moving and the story is what everyone came for.

**4. Help someone.** "I'll cover her while she picks the lock." "I stand next to the wizard in case something comes at him." Most systems reward this mechanically, and it is a move that makes another player happy, which is never wrong.

**5. Say what your character is feeling.** You do not need a plan. "She's terrified and trying not to show it" is a complete turn. It tells the GM and the table something true, and often another player will build on it.

**6. Ask another character a question.** Turn to the player next to you, in character: "Do you trust this guy?" Now two people are roleplaying and neither of you had to invent anything.

If you take nothing else from this article, take the list. Six moves, any one of which gets you through any moment of silence at any table.

## How do you build a character you can actually play?

The blank moment is often a design problem. A character defined by "elf ranger, Dexterity 16" gives you nothing to decide with. A character defined by two or three traits does.

Before your next session, answer these three questions in one sentence each.

- **What does your character want?** Not in the campaign; right now, in general. Money, respect, safety, to prove something, to go home. One thing.
- **What is your character afraid of?** Fire, being alone, authority, being wrong, the dark. One thing.
- **How does your character treat strangers?** Warm, wary, rude, overly polite, curious. One word.

Now, in the blank moment, you have a decision engine. A character who wants respect and is wary of strangers will handle the suspicious innkeeper differently from one who wants money and treats everyone like a friend. You are not inventing; you are consulting. We wrote more on the mechanics of first sessions in [how to play D&D for the first time](/articles/how-to-play-dnd-for-beginners/), but these three questions are the heart of it.

Pre-made characters usually include a personality sketch. Read it before you sit down and underline the one trait you intend to lean on. One is enough. Trying to play five traits at once is how you end up playing none.

## What should you do in combat when you freeze?

Combat has more structure, which helps, but it also has an audience, which does not. Here is a simple priority list for when the GM says "your turn" and you have nothing.

| Situation | Default move |
|---|---|
| An enemy is next to you | Attack it |
| No enemy is next to you | Move toward the nearest one and attack, or move to stand next to a friend |
| A friend is badly hurt | Move to them; help or heal if you can |
| You are badly hurt | Step back behind someone sturdier |
| You are a spellcaster and unsure | Ask "what's my simplest attack spell?" and cast it |

A plain turn is a good turn. Nobody at the table is grading creativity. The player who attacks the goblin every round is contributing. The player who spends four minutes agonizing is the one slowing the night. If you want to try something clever, do it when you have thought of it in advance, on someone else's turn.

## How do you roleplay if you are shy?

Many of the best roleplayers are introverts. Shyness is not a disadvantage; it changes which tools you use. A few that work well for quieter players:

- **Play a quiet character.** The stoic bodyguard, the observant scholar. A character who speaks rarely makes each line land, and gives you permission to listen most of the time.
- **Describe instead of perform.** "He nods and says he'll take first watch" is roleplaying. You never have to speak in first person if you do not want to.
- **Pair up.** Find one other player whose character yours trusts, and build small exchanges with them. Two-person scenes are easier than addressing the whole table.
- **Use the physical stuff.** Move your miniature. Hand over a prop. Write a note to another player. Action can carry a turn as well as words.
- **Tell the GM.** "I'm a bit quiet; feel free to call on me." Good GMs will make space for you without putting you on the spot.

If your table makes shyness feel like a failing, that is a table problem. We have a piece on [hosting for introverts](/articles/game-night-for-introverts/) that applies as much to RPG tables as board game nights.

## What should you not worry about?

A short list of things that occupy new players and do not matter.

Doing a voice. Optional, always. Being consistent with a character you have known for two hours; nobody will notice drift, and if they do, call it growth. Knowing the lore; ask, and the GM will be thrilled to tell you. Making the optimal tactical choice; the GM is adjusting difficulty behind the screen anyway. Being funny; humor at a good table arises from the situation, not from preparation.

What does matter is being present. Listen when it is not your turn. Build on what others do. Say yes to invitations other players extend in the fiction. A player who does those three things and never delivers a memorable line is a player every table wants back. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### Do you have to talk in character in D&D?

No. Describing what your character does in third person is complete roleplaying. First-person dialogue and voices are optional.

### What should I do if I freeze on my turn?

Ask the GM a question about the scene, react to what another player just did, or take the obvious action. Any of these is a good turn.

### How do I get better at roleplaying?

Define your character by one want, one fear, and one way of treating strangers, then consult those when deciding. Listen closely on other players' turns and build on what they do.

### Is it okay to play a character who is quiet?

Yes. Quiet characters are common and effective, and they suit players who would rather listen than perform.
