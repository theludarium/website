The best first purchase for a board game collection is nothing. Play before you pay: spend your first few months at other people's tables, at game cafés, and at the library, and buy a game only after you already know you like it. This is the $0 tier, and it is where every good library starts.

This article opens our series, Building Your Library on Purpose, which walks from $0 to a shelf you will still pull from in 20 years. The whole path is mapped in [The Starter Shelf](/articles/starter-shelf-40-to-300/), but the first step costs no money at all. It costs a little attention.

## Why play before you pay beats buying first

Most people build a collection backward. They see a game they like the look of, buy it, open it, and discover the rules take an hour to teach, or it only sings at four players and they usually have two, or their spouse finds it dull. The box goes on the shelf. That is how the [Shelf of Shame](/articles/shelf-of-shame-infinite-cost/) is born: games with zero plays and an infinite cost per hour.

Playing first flips the order. You find out what you like with real people and a real clock, and only then do you spend. Typical new games run $15 to $60, with most family and strategy titles around $25 to $40. Skip three wrong buys in your first year and you have saved roughly $100, which is most of a [starter shelf](/articles/starter-shelf-40-to-300/).

There is a second reason, and it is the one we care about most. The [One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/) says a play group needs one copy of a game, not one copy per person. If a friend already owns the game, playing at their table is not a stopgap before you buy your own. It might be the whole plan. You may never need to buy that game at all.

## Where to play board games for free

You have more free tables available than you think. Here are the ones that work for almost everyone.

- **Other people's tables.** Somebody you know owns games. Ask what they have and invite yourself over. Game owners love teaching; it is half the hobby.
- **Game cafés.** Most charge a small cover or a minimum on drinks and give you access to hundreds of titles. Two hours for the price of a coffee is the cheapest demo in the world.
- **Public libraries.** Many libraries now lend board games the same way they lend books. Check your branch's catalog. A two-week loan is enough to learn whether a game belongs on your shelf.
- **The Ludarium Game Lounge, when it opens.** We are building a membership game lounge in Indianapolis for exactly this: show up, sit down, and someone will teach you something. You bring nothing but yourself. [Join the Indianapolis waitlist](/lounges/indianapolis/) to hear first.
- **Local game stores.** Many run open play nights or keep a demo library. Ask at the counter.
- **Friends of friends.** When a game owner invites you, say yes, and bring a snack. That is the entry fee.

## How to take notes on what you actually liked

Playing is only half of the $0 tier. The other half is remembering what you learned, because memory is a terrible collection advisor. Six weeks after game night you will recall "that one with the birds" and nothing about why it worked.

After each play, write down four things:

1. **The mechanic.** Not the theme. Were you building a route, bidding, drafting cards, cooperating against the board, bluffing? Themes attract you to a box; mechanics decide whether you play it twice.
2. **The length.** The actual time, including the teach. A game that says 45 minutes on the box and ran 90 at the table is a 90-minute game for you.
3. **The player count.** How many were at the table, and did it feel right? A game that was brilliant with five may drag with two.
4. **Who you played with.** The people matter as much as the cardboard. A game your spouse enjoyed is worth more to you than one your most competitive friend enjoyed.

Add a one-to-five rating and a single sentence of why. That is the whole discipline. It takes 90 seconds.

## A simple taste log you can copy

Keep this in a notes app, a spreadsheet, or a pocket notebook. The format does not matter; the habit does.

| Date | Game | Mechanic | Players | Real length | Played with | Rating | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oct 3 | Ticket to Ride | Route building, set collection | 4 | 70 min | Beth, Sam, Rae | 4 | Easy teach, tense ending, Beth asked to play again |
| Oct 10 | Codenames | Word association, teams | 8 | 25 min | Party at Sam's | 5 | Whole room laughing, works with new players |
| Oct 17 | Terraforming Mars | Engine building, card drafting | 3 | 3 hrs | Sam, Rae | 3 | Loved the engine, too long for a weeknight |
| Oct 24 | Jaipur | Set collection, push your luck | 2 | 20 min | Beth | 4 | Perfect after the kids are down |

Those entries are invented to show the shape; your log will look different. After ten or fifteen rows, patterns appear that you would never have guessed from browsing a store. You might find that every five-star entry was under 30 minutes, or that you only rated games highly when your partner was at the table, or that you love engine building and merely tolerate everything else.

## How the taste log saves you hundreds

Here is the math in illustration form. Say you would otherwise buy one new game a month at about $35 each, and that roughly a third of blind purchases turn out to be misses. That is four misses a year, about $140 of cardboard that gets played once or never.

With a taste log, your misses drop because every purchase is backed by a play you already enjoyed. Even cutting misses in half saves about $70 a year, and most people do better than that. Over the five years it takes to build a real library, that is a few hundred dollars that went into games you love instead of games you own.

The log also tells you which role a game would fill, which matters once you start building a [starter shelf](/articles/starter-shelf-40-to-300/) one role at a time. You will know you need a filler, not another 90-minute strategy game, because the log says you never finish 90-minute games on weeknights.

## When playing first means never buying at all

This is the part that surprises people. If your taste log shows that you played Catan four times at Sam's house and loved it every time, the right move is often not to buy Catan. It is to keep playing at Sam's.

That is the [One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/) in action. Sam's copy already serves the group. A second copy would cost about $40 and create no new plays; you would still play at Sam's, because that is where the group meets. The [second-copy test](/articles/when-a-second-copy-is-worth-it/) asks one question: does the second copy create plays that would not otherwise happen? Usually the answer is no.

So your taste log does double duty. It tells you what you like, and it tells you who already owns it. Mark the owner in the "played with" column. When you reach the point of spending your [first $40](/articles/your-first-40-dollars/), you will spend it on something your group does not have yet, which is the beginning of [claiming a lane](/articles/claim-a-lane/) and building a [Shared Shelf](/articles/one-copy-rule/) across several homes.

## How long should the $0 tier last?

Longer than feels natural. Most people get itchy after three or four plays and want to own something. Hold out for ten to fifteen logged plays across at least three different kinds of games. That is roughly two or three months of regular game nights.

You will know you are ready to spend when three things are true:

- You can name a mechanic you like and one you do not.
- You know your most common player count, the one you actually sit down with most weeks.
- There is a game you have played at least twice, enjoyed both times, and nobody in your group owns.

When all three line up, you are not guessing anymore. You are buying an anchor, and the next article covers exactly how to choose it.

## FAQ

### Can you try board games before buying them?

Yes. Game cafés let you play from a large library for a small cover charge, many public libraries lend board games for free, local game stores often run open play nights, and friends who own games are almost always happy to teach. Playing first is the most reliable way to avoid buying games you will never play.

### Do public libraries lend board games?

Many do. Board game lending has spread through US public library systems over the past decade, often under a "Library of Things" program. Check your branch's online catalog or ask at the desk. Loans usually run one to three weeks, which is enough to play a game two or three times and decide whether it belongs on your own shelf.

### How do I know which board games I will like?

Keep a taste log. After every play, note the core mechanic, the real play time, the player count, who you played with, and a one-to-five rating. After ten to fifteen plays, patterns show up: a preferred length, a favorite mechanic, the people you most enjoy playing with. Those patterns predict what you will enjoy far better than box art or rankings.

### Is it worth buying a board game a friend already owns?

Usually not. A play group needs one copy of a game, not one per person, because the copy's value is already shared by everyone at the table. Buy a second copy only if it creates plays that would not otherwise happen, such as a second group in a different city. Otherwise, spend that money on a game your group does not have yet.
