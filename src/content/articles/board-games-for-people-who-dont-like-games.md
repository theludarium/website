Choosing games for people who don't like games starts with a question most hosts skip: what exactly do they not like? Almost nobody dislikes the whole category. They dislike one specific thing, usually from a bad experience: a two-hour Monopoly grudge match, a rules lecture, a know-it-all brother-in-law, or a game that made them look dumb in front of people. Find the thing, and there is a game that avoids it.

This article names the five most common objections and matches each with games that sidestep it. Then it covers how to make the invitation, because the game is only half the problem.

## What do people who "don't like games" actually dislike?

When someone says "I don't like games," listen for what comes next. It is usually one of these:

1. **"I don't want to learn a bunch of rules."** The rules objection. They remember a 20-minute teach and a confusing turn.
2. **"I always lose."** The competition objection. Games have been humiliating or just unfun because someone else always won.
3. **"I hate being put on the spot."** The performance objection. Charades, drawing, acting, anything where people watch you.
4. **"They take forever."** The time objection. Monopoly, Risk, or a strategy game that ran until 1 a.m.
5. **"I'd rather just talk."** The social objection. They came to see people, not to stare at a board.

Each of these is a real and reasonable complaint about certain games. None of them is a complaint about Wavelength. The table below maps objections to games, and the sections after explain why.

| Objection | Games that sidestep it |
|---|---|
| Rules | Wavelength, Just One, Telestrations, Codenames |
| Losing | Just One, The Crew, Hanabi, Telestrations |
| Being watched | Codenames (as guesser), Azul, Sushi Go!, Cascadia |
| Time | Sushi Go!, Love Letter, Skull, No Thanks! |
| Wanting to talk | Wavelength, Codenames, Just One, Poetry for Neanderthals |

## Which games need almost no rules?

The rules objection is the easiest to beat because the best party games of the last decade have teaches under one minute.

**Wavelength** <!-- AFFILIATE --> takes about thirty seconds to explain. One player sees a hidden target on a dial. The dial has a spectrum, say "overrated" to "underrated." The player gives a one-word clue, like "pumpkin spice latte," and the team argues about where it falls. That is the entire game. It produces more conversation per minute than any other game we know.

**Just One** <!-- AFFILIATE --> is cooperative. One player closes their eyes. Everyone else writes a one-word clue for a secret word. Matching clues are erased. The guesser sees what is left and guesses. Thirty seconds to teach.

**Telestrations** <!-- AFFILIATE --> is the telephone game with drawing. Write a phrase, pass it. Draw the phrase, pass it. Guess the drawing, pass it. At the end everyone reveals their book and laughs at how "giraffe" became "angry ladder."

**Codenames** <!-- AFFILIATE --> takes about two minutes. The guessers need to know almost nothing: listen to a word and a number, point at cards. Only the clue-giver carries any rules load, and the host can take that role.

For reluctant players, teach by starting. Do not explain the game and then play it. Play the first round out loud, narrating as you go. See [how to teach a board game](/articles/how-to-teach-a-board-game/) for the structure.

## Which games remove the losing?

Some people do not dislike games. They dislike losing, or more precisely, they dislike losing in front of people. Cooperative games fix this entirely: everyone wins or everyone loses, and nobody is singled out.

- **Just One** <!-- AFFILIATE --> is scored as a team. Thirteen words, how many did we get? The score is a shared story, not a ranking.
- **The Crew** <!-- AFFILIATE --> is a cooperative trick-taking game. If your family plays Euchre or Spades, it is immediately familiar, and nobody loses alone.
- **Hanabi** <!-- AFFILIATE --> is a quiet cooperative card game where you hold your cards facing out. The team builds a fireworks display together.
- **Telestrations** has a scoring system that nobody uses. Most tables skip it entirely. The game is the reveal, not the points.

There is also a middle option: games where losing does not feel like losing. Sushi Go! and Azul have final scores, but the gap is small, the game is short, and nobody remembers who won ten minutes later. For someone with a competitive scar, that is the right next step. We have more on this in [how to be a good loser](/articles/how-to-be-a-good-loser/), which is really about building tables where losing is cheap.

## Which games avoid the spotlight?

The performance objection is about being watched. Some people will never enjoy charades, and that is fine. These games let them play without ever being the center of attention.

- **Codenames as a guesser.** The guessers point at cards and argue with each other. The clue-giver has the spotlight. Give that role to someone else.
- **Azul** <!-- AFFILIATE -->. Everyone looks at the tiles, not each other. Turns are quick and quiet.
- **Sushi Go!** <!-- AFFILIATE -->. Everyone picks a card at the same time. There is no "your turn" to be watched during.
- **Cascadia** <!-- AFFILIATE -->. A calm tile-placement puzzle where each player builds their own map. Interaction is minimal.

Simultaneous-turn games are the secret here. If everyone acts at once, no one is on stage. Sushi Go!, 7 Wonders, and Wavelength (where the whole team argues together) all share this property.

## Which games respect the clock?

The time objection comes from games that ran too long. The answer is games that end before anyone wants them to.

- **Sushi Go!**: 15 minutes.
- **Love Letter** <!-- AFFILIATE -->: 10 minutes per round, 16 cards.
- **Skull** <!-- AFFILIATE -->: 15 minutes of bluffing with coasters.
- **No Thanks!** <!-- AFFILIATE -->: 15 minutes, one rule (take the card or pay a chip to pass).

Tell the reluctant player how long the game takes before it starts, and be accurate. "This is fifteen minutes" is a promise. Keep it, and the next invitation is easier. For a reference on which games run long, see [board game play time accuracy](/articles/board-game-play-time-accuracy/).

## Which games are really just conversation?

The last objection is the most honest one: "I came to see you, not to play a game." These games are a conversation with a light frame around it.

- **Wavelength** is the best answer. The game is literally people arguing about whether a hot dog is a sandwich.
- **Poetry for Neanderthals** <!-- AFFILIATE --> has you describe words using only one-syllable words, while a teammate hits you with an inflatable club if you slip. It is silly and it gets people talking in a way they do not expect.
- **Codenames** at a table of friends is about forty percent game and sixty percent talking about why someone thought "bark" was a good clue for "tree" and "dog" and "seal."

For this person, do not say "let's play a game." Say "I want to show you something," put Wavelength on the table, and start a round. Ten minutes later they will be arguing about whether cereal is soup.

## How do you make the invitation?

The game matters less than how it is offered. A few rules:

1. **Do not call it game night.** "Come over Thursday" and the game appears after dinner. Framing a whole evening around games raises the stakes for someone who thinks they dislike them.
2. **Start without asking.** Put the game on the table and begin a round with the willing players. Reluctant people join a game that is already fun. They rarely join one that is being proposed.
3. **Give them the easy seat.** Guesser, not clue-giver. Team member, not solo player.
4. **Stop after one.** If they liked it, they will ask for another. If you push a second game, you spend the goodwill the first one earned.
5. **Never say "you'll love it."** It is a promise you cannot keep, and it puts pressure on them to perform enjoyment.

## FAQ

### What is the best game for someone who hates board games?

Wavelength. It takes thirty seconds to learn, has no losing to speak of, runs entirely on conversation, and does not feel like a board game.

### Are there board games with no reading or strategy?

Yes. Telestrations, Just One, and Wavelength require no strategy and almost no reading. Azul and Sushi Go! have simple choices but nothing that feels like studying.

### How do you get a reluctant person to play a game?

Start the game with willing players and let them watch. Give them the lowest-pressure role when they join. Keep it to one short game and stop while it is fun.

### What games work for people who don't like competition?

Cooperative games: Just One, The Crew, Hanabi, and Telestrations (which nobody scores). Everyone wins or loses together.
