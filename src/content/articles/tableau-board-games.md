A tableau in a board game is the set of cards or tiles a player has placed face up in front of them, which stays there and keeps affecting the game. If a card in your hand is a possibility and a card in your discard pile is history, a card in your tableau is a permanent part of who you are in this game. The term "tableau board game" or "tableau builder" describes games where building that personal display is the main activity.

This article explains what a tableau is, how it differs from the other places a card can be, why so many designers use it, and which games show it off best.

## Where does the word come from?

"Tableau" is French for picture or scene, and English borrowed it long ago for a staged, frozen arrangement of people or objects. Card players picked it up for solitaire, where the [tableau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_patience_terms) is the main layout of columns you build on. Modern board gaming took it from there.

The sense carries over well. A player's tableau is a picture of their position: everything they have built, laid out for the table to see.

## What makes something a tableau?

Three features, and all three matter.

**It is face up.** Everyone can see it. There is no hidden information in a tableau, which is why tableau games tend to be readable: you can look across the table and know what your opponent has.

**It is persistent.** Cards in a tableau stay there. They are not discarded after use. A card you placed on turn two is still working on turn twenty.

**It is personal.** Each player has their own. A shared display in the middle of the table is a market or a board, not a tableau.

Put those together and you get a growing, visible, personal record of your decisions. That is a tableau.

## Tableau, hand, deck, and discard: what is the difference?

Players new to the hobby sometimes hear "tableau" and think it means "cards on the table." It is more specific than that. A card can be in several places, and each place means something different.

| Zone | Visible? | Persistent? | What it represents |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hand | Only to you | Until played | Options you have not used yet |
| Deck | To nobody | Until drawn | The future, in random order |
| Discard | Usually yes | Until reshuffled | Things you have used |
| Market or display | To everyone | Until taken | Things anyone could acquire |
| Tableau | To everyone | For the rest of the game | Things you have built |

A deck builder like Dominion has a hand, a deck, and a discard, but almost no tableau; cards cycle through and nothing stays out. <!-- AFFILIATE --> A tableau builder like Race for the Galaxy has a hand and a tableau, and cards move from one to the other and stay. The two feel completely different even though both are "card games where you acquire cards."

Many games mix zones. Wingspan has a hand of bird cards and a tableau of played birds. <!-- AFFILIATE --> Terraforming Mars has a hand of projects, a tableau of played cards, and a shared board. The tableau is one place among several, and the game's character depends on how much happens there.

## What is a tableau builder?

A tableau builder is a game where adding cards to your tableau is the main thing you do and where the tableau's contents determine most of your scoring and most of your abilities.

Race for the Galaxy is the game most hobbyists name first. <!-- AFFILIATE --> You play planets and developments into a tableau; each one gives a power or a scoring condition, and the game ends when someone has twelve cards out. The whole game is in the tableau. There is no board at all.

Other well-known tableau builders:

- **Wingspan**, where the tableau is three rows of birds, one per habitat, and each row's powers trigger together.
- **7 Wonders**, where every drafted card goes into a tableau grouped by color, and scoring reads straight off it.
- **Everdell**, where the tableau is your city, limited to fifteen cards, which makes every placement a choice about what not to build. <!-- AFFILIATE -->
- **Terraforming Mars**, where the tableau holds your played project cards and their ongoing effects.
- **San Juan**, the card-only cousin of Puerto Rico, where buildings go into a tableau and cards double as currency.
- **Imperial Settlers** and **Res Arcana**, both tighter tableau games with strong card interaction.

What these share is that the interesting choice is not "which card do I play" but "which card do I add to what I already have." The tableau gives every new card context.

## Why do designers like tableaus?

A few reasons, most of them about information.

**They make the game legible.** Because every player's position is face up, you can see who is ahead, what they are building toward, and what you need to stop. Hidden hands supply the uncertainty; the tableau supplies the clarity.

**They reward planning.** Since cards persist, a card that is weak now and strong later is a real option. In a game where cards are spent and gone, you would never play it. In a tableau game, you play it on turn three and enjoy it on turn fifteen.

**They create combos.** Two cards that each do something become more than that when both sit in the same tableau. Designers can write a card that says "whenever you do X" and know it will stay out long enough to matter.

**They show progress.** A tableau grows. Players like watching their picture fill in, and newcomers find it easier to feel engaged with something in front of them than with an abstract score.

**They scale.** Most tableau games have little direct interaction, so adding a fifth or sixth player adds little time. 7 Wonders handles seven players in thirty minutes largely because every player's tableau is their own business.

That last point is also the standard complaint. A table of tableau builders can feel like six people doing a puzzle in parallel. Designers who care about interaction add shared markets, competitive scoring, or neighbor effects, as 7 Wonders does with military. ## How do you read an opponent's tableau?

Since it is all face up, you should. Three things to check on every turn.

**What are they scoring?** Most tableau cards show their point value or their scoring condition. Add them up. If someone's tableau is quietly worth forty points, you need to know.

**What do they produce?** Look for cards with repeating effects. A tableau with three income cards will pull ahead in a few turns even if it is behind now.

**What are they missing?** A player collecting a set needs the last piece. A player with a strong engine needs cards to spend it on. If the thing they need is in the shared market, you now know what to take.

The habit of reading the table rather than only your own cards is what separates intermediate tableau players from beginners.

## FAQ

### What does tableau mean in board games?

The face-up, permanent set of cards or tiles a player has in front of them, which keeps affecting the game until it ends.

### Is a tableau the same as a player board?

Not quite. A player board is a printed mat that comes in the box. A tableau is built from cards or tiles during play. Many games have both, with the tableau sitting on or next to the board.

### What is the best tableau building game for beginners?

Wingspan is the most approachable because the theme carries the rules. 7 Wonders is the fastest to teach for a large group.

### Is Dominion a tableau game?

No. Dominion's cards cycle through your deck and are never permanently placed, which is the opposite of a tableau.

## The picture you build

A tableau is a picture of a game played, visible to everyone who sat down for it. It is one of the few things in the hobby you can point to at the end of the night and say, "that is what I did." The game is the reason. The people are the point.
