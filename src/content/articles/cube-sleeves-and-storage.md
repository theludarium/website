Cube sleeves take more abuse than any sleeves in Magic. A constructed deck gets shuffled by one careful owner. A cube gets shuffled by eight different people, dealt into packs, riffled in deckbuilding, shuffled again in three rounds, then torn down and shuffled back into the box. Every week. For years. The sleeve that is fine for a Commander deck will split, cloud, or warp under that load within months.

This article is about what holds up: which sleeves, whether to double sleeve, how to box and sort the cube so setup is fast, and how to handle the basic lands that every drafter needs. It is the unglamorous half of [running a cube draft](/articles/how-to-run-a-cube-draft/), and getting it right saves you money and an hour a month.

## Which sleeves survive weekly cube play?

Three things matter: shuffle feel, durability under repeated riffling, and consistency between sleeves so no card is marked. Opacity matters too, since most cubes include a few cards worth protecting from view and from fingerprints.

What we have found, and what the cube community broadly agrees on:

**Matte-backed, textured sleeves from the major brands.** Dragon Shield Matte, Ultimate Guard Katana, KMC Hyper Matte, Ultra Pro Eclipse Matte. All four are used in long-running cubes and hold up for a year or more of weekly play. <!-- AFFILIATE --> Katanas shuffle the smoothest when new and show wear sooner; Dragon Shield Mattes are thicker and more resistant to splitting; KMC Hyper Mattes are the thinnest and the cheapest to replace. Any of them is a fine choice. Pick one and buy enough from the same production batch that you have 10 percent spares.

**Avoid glossy sleeves.** They stick together when humid, show scratches, and the clear fronts cloud. Fine for a deck in a box; bad for a cube.

**Avoid cheap bulk sleeves.** Fifty-cent-a-pack sleeves split along the sides after a few dozen shuffles. Replacing them one at a time means the cube ends up with mixed sleeves, which is a marking problem.

**Match the batch.** Sleeves from the same brand and color can differ slightly in shade or texture between production runs. If you buy 400 sleeves over three orders, check them against each other before sleeving. A cube where the five oldest cards have slightly darker sleeves is a cube where someone knows when they are about to draw a bomb.

A sleeved 360-card cube needs about 400 sleeves including spares and basics, which at typical prices for the brands above runs a few dozen dollars. That is the single most important recurring cost of owning a cube, and it is small.

## Should you double sleeve a cube?

Double sleeving means a tight-fitting inner sleeve, usually KMC Perfect Fit or Dragon Shield Perfect Fit, inside the outer sleeve. <!-- AFFILIATE --> It protects the cards from moisture and edge wear far better than a single sleeve.

Arguments for double sleeving a cube:

- **Valuable cards.** If your cube contains real dual lands, fetches, or anything over 50 dollars, the inner sleeve is cheap insurance.
- **Humidity.** In a humid climate or a basement, single-sleeved cards curl. Inner sleeves slow this.
- **Longevity.** The inner sleeve means the outer sleeve can be replaced without the card ever being exposed to handling.

Arguments against:

- **Shuffle feel.** Double-sleeved cards are thicker and stiffer. A 45-card pile of double-sleeved cards riffle-shuffles badly, and new players shuffle it worse. Most double-sleeved cubes are mash-shuffled, which takes longer.
- **Time.** Double sleeving 400 cards takes an afternoon. Resleeving the outer layer takes another.
- **Pack size.** A fifteen-card double-sleeved pack is a chunk. Not a real problem, but you notice it.

Our own cube is single sleeved with inner sleeves only on the handful of cards that would hurt to replace. <!-- EDITOR: add detail --> That compromise is common. If your cube is pauper or peasant, skip inner sleeves entirely.

Whatever you decide, do it for the whole cube or for a set of cards that cannot be identified from the back. A cube where only the expensive cards are double sleeved is a cube where thickness tells you what is coming.

## How should a cube be boxed?

A cube needs a box that holds the full list plus basics, closes securely for transport, and opens to a layout that lets eight people grab cards without chaos.

**Box types that work:**

| Box | Holds (sleeved) | Good for | Downside |
|---|---|---|---|
| Two-row cardboard 800-count box | 540 cube plus basics | Cheapest, fits a backpack | Wears out in a year, no latch |
| Plastic or acrylic cube box | 360 to 540 | Durable, see-through, latches | Heavy, can crack if dropped |
| Wooden deck box with dividers | 360 to 540 | Looks great on a table | Expensive, heavy |
| Binder with 9-pocket pages | Any | Browsing the list, rotisserie | Not for shuffling or packs |
| Two 360-card deck boxes | 360 plus basics | Split cube and basics | Two things to carry |

Whichever box you choose, leave room. A packed box damages sleeves at the edges every time you pull cards out, and you will add cards. Aim for a box rated 20 to 30 percent above your cube's sleeved count.

**Transport.** A cube goes to a store, a brewery, or a friend's kitchen every week. A box that latches, inside a bag with some padding, is enough. A box held shut with a rubber band is how a cube ends up on a parking lot.

## How should the cards be sorted?

The sort is a trade-off between fast setup, where you just need to shuffle and deal, and fast teardown and auditing, where you need to put cards back and notice what is missing.

The common answer: **sort by color, then alphabetically, for storage. Shuffle everything at the start of the night.**

Sorted storage lets you check the list against the box in ten minutes, find a specific card for a swap, and spot a missing card when teardown ends and the white section is one short. Then on game night, you pull the whole cube, shuffle it in chunks, combine, and deal packs. Teardown is each player sorting their deck and sideboard back into colors and the owner merging the stacks.

Some owners store the cube pre-shuffled to skip the setup shuffle. It saves five minutes and costs the ability to audit, and it means a missing card goes unnoticed for weeks. We do not recommend it.

Use dividers. Colored dividers between the seven sections (five colors, gold, colorless, and lands) make teardown a task eight people can do in parallel without instructions.

## How do you handle basic lands?

Every drafter needs access to basics during deckbuilding, usually 16 or 17 lands in a 40-card deck, which means the cube owner needs a supply. Two approaches:

**Full basic land station.** Around 20 of each basic type per seat, sleeved in the same sleeves as the cube, carried in a separate box. For eight players, that is 800 sleeved basics, which is more sleeves than the cube itself. The upside is a clean draft where every deck is fully sleeved and uniform.

**Shared pool, returned after.** Around 30 to 40 of each basic type, sleeved, in a single box. Drafters take what they need and return them at teardown. This is what most weekly groups do. It works because not every deck needs 10 of one land type.

Whichever you choose, the basics must be sleeved in the same sleeves as the cube. An unsleeved or differently sleeved basic in a deck is a marked card.

Decide on art. Full-art basics, or a single matching set, make a cube table look finished. Mixed basics from twenty years of sets look like a kitchen table. Neither affects the game.

## Maintenance that keeps a cube playable for years

A short checklist, roughly monthly:

1. **Inspect for splits.** Ride a thumb along the edges of each stack. Replace any sleeve that is split, badly scuffed, or bent at a corner. Keep spares from the original batch.
2. **Audit the count.** Sort and count each section against the list. Cards go home in decks by accident and nobody notices until the Simic drafter wonders where the ramp went.
3. **Clean the box.** Dust and grit in a box scratch sleeves. Wipe it out.
4. **Full resleeve every one to two years.** When more than a handful of sleeves look worn, resleeve the whole cube in a fresh batch rather than replacing them piecemeal, so nothing is marked.

If you are [updating the list](/articles/how-to-update-a-cube/), do it at the same time as the resleeve. You are already handling every card.

## FAQ

### What are the best sleeves for a cube?

Matte, textured sleeves from an established brand: Dragon Shield Matte, Ultimate Guard Katana, KMC Hyper Matte, or Ultra Pro Eclipse Matte. Buy enough from one batch to include spares.

### Should I double sleeve my cube?

Double sleeve if the cube holds expensive cards or lives in a humid place. Otherwise single sleeving is fine and shuffles better. Do not double sleeve only some cards; thickness marks them.

### How should I store a cube between drafts?

Sorted by color and alphabetically, in a box rated for more cards than you have, with dividers. Shuffle at the start of the night, not before storing.

### How many basic lands does a cube need?

For a shared pool returned after each night, 30 to 40 of each type, sleeved to match the cube. For a full station, around 20 of each type per seat.

## The box is the easy part

Sleeves and boxes are the kind of thing you set up once and then forget until something fails. Set them up well, check them monthly, and the cube is ready every week for years. What you do with the box is the hard part, and the good part. The game is the reason. The people are the point.
