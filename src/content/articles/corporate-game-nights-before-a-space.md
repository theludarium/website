The Ludarium is a corporate game night business before it is a lounge, and that order is on purpose. We bring tabletop sessions to companies in Indianapolis, run them as team and leadership training, and use what we earn and learn to build toward the room. This article explains why we chose to start with events instead of a lease, what the service actually is, and what it teaches us about running the space we eventually want to open.

## Why not just open the lounge?

Because the two things that kill a lounge are rent before members and inexperience before customers, and events solve both.

We laid out the arithmetic in [the membership math](/articles/game-lounge-membership-model/). A room needs a few hundred members to carry its fixed costs. Those members do not exist on opening day unless you built them beforehand. Open too early and the first members pay for an empty room, which is the fastest way to lose them.

Events have no rent. A corporate session happens in the client's conference room or an offsite venue they already booked. We bring the games, the hosts, and the plan. We earn revenue from day one, with no lease, no build-out, and no fixed cost waiting for Friday night.

Events also teach us to host. Running a room is a craft. Teaching Codenames to twelve skeptical account managers at two in the afternoon is harder than teaching it to four friends at nine at night, and doing it fifty times before we open means we will be good at it when a member walks in. <!-- AFFILIATE -->

## What is the corporate tabletop events service?

It is a facilitated session, usually two to three hours, where a team plays carefully chosen games with a host who connects what happens at the table to how the team works.

That last part is what separates it from "we brought some games to the office." A good session does three things:

1. **Picks the right games for the goal.** Communication problems get cooperative games like The Crew or Pandemic, where the quarterback problem shows up fast and visibly. Trust and candor get social deduction. Negotiation gets Catan or a trading game. <!-- AFFILIATE -->
2. **Hosts, not just explains.** The facilitator teaches fast, keeps the energy up, and watches. Who takes over. Who goes quiet. Who recovers well from a bad turn.
3. **Debriefs.** Fifteen minutes at the end where the host names what happened and the team talks about it. This is where the training is. Without it, it was a fun afternoon. With it, people learn something about each other they could not have gotten from a slide deck.

We have written about the underlying ideas in pieces like [board games for team building](/articles/board-games-team-building/). The service is those ideas, delivered.

## Why is this a good first revenue line for a game lounge business?

Several reasons, and they compound.

**It is daytime revenue.** A lounge's busiest hours are evenings and weekends. Corporate sessions happen on weekday afternoons. When we have a room, events fill the hours the membership does not, which is close to free money for the building.

**The margins are better than anything else we will do.** No food cost, no rent, modest equipment. The cost is the host's time and the games, which we reuse.

**It introduces us to people who would never walk into a game cafe.** The HR director who books a session is not a hobbyist. Neither are most of the people in the room. Some of them discover they like this, and they are exactly the members we want, the ones who thought games were not for them.

**It builds a reputation before we have an address.** When we do open, we want companies to already know us as the people who ran that session that actually worked. That is a different starting position than "new cafe opens, hopes for the best."

## What does a corporate game night teach us about the lounge?

More than we expected. Here is a working list of lessons so far, which we will keep updating.

| What we learned in corporate rooms | What it means for the space |
|---|---|
| The first five minutes decide the session | The first game a walk-in plays decides whether they return |
| Teaching speed matters more than game quality | Hire hosts for teaching, stock games they can teach fast |
| Noise kills table conversation | Two acoustic zones in the first space, no exceptions |
| Quiet people need a game that gives them a role | Program for introverts, not just the loud table |
| A debrief turns play into meaning | Members who talk after the game come back; build in time for it |
| Nobody wants to feel tested | Frame everything as play, even when it is training |

The noise lesson alone changed our space checklist. We had quiet zones as a nice-to-have. After a session in an open-plan office where two tables could not hear each other, it moved to must-have.

## How do we keep the events service honest?

By not overselling it.

Games are a good way for a team to see itself. They are not therapy, not a personality assessment, and not a substitute for a manager doing the hard work of leading. We say that in the first conversation with a client. A team that plays Pandemic together will understand its communication habits better. It will not fix them in an afternoon. <!-- AFFILIATE -->

We also decline sessions that are really just entertainment, or we price them as entertainment and skip the training framing. A holiday party wants a host and a stack of party games, not a debrief. Both are fine. Pretending one is the other is not.

And we keep the mission in the room. Togethering is the point, whether the people at the table are old friends or coworkers who have only ever met on video calls. If a session ends with people knowing each other better, it worked. Fun is nice. Knowing each other is the product.

## What happens to the events service when the lounge opens?

It keeps going, and gets easier.

Once there is a room, we can host sessions on site in a space designed for it, with the library and the kitchen already there. Companies that do not want to host in their own office get a venue. We get daytime revenue in a room that would otherwise be quiet.

We also expect the events service to feed the membership directly. A team that had a good afternoon comes back on a Thursday. A company that books quarterly sessions asks about a corporate membership. The events service is the top of the funnel for the building, and it was the funding for the building before that. That is why it comes first.
