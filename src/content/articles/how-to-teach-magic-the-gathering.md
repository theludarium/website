If you are trying to work out how to teach Magic the Gathering to a friend, the answer that works most often is not a starter deck or a rules walkthrough. It is a cube draft, run at a slow pace, with a patient table. Cube teaches the three things a new player most needs, which are what cards do, how decks fit together, and why the choices matter, and it does them in one evening instead of a month.

I have taught Magic the wrong way many times. Two starter decks across a kitchen table, me explaining the stack while their eyes glazed. Cube fixed it, and this is why.

## Why do most attempts to teach Magic fail?

Magic has a reputation as a hard game to learn. The rules are part of that, but they are not the main problem. The main problem is that the usual teaching methods separate the parts of the game that make it fun.

**Starter decks skip deckbuilding.** The new player is handed a deck someone else built. They play it without understanding why the cards are there. When they lose, they do not know whether it was the deck or them.

**Rules-first teaching front-loads the boring part.** Nobody falls in love with Magic because of the rules for the combat damage step. They fall in love with casting a dragon. If the first hour is rules, many people never reach the dragon.

**Playing against a collection is demoralizing.** The teacher has been playing for years and owns a thousand cards. The student has a precon. The student loses every game and concludes the game is about money.

Cube solves all three at once, which is why it has become the teaching format of choice for many groups.

## What does a cube draft teach that a starter deck cannot?

A cube is a fixed set of cards, usually 360, that players draft from and then play with. If that is new to you, [what a Magic cube is](/articles/what-is-a-magic-cube/) covers the basics. For teaching, four properties matter.

**The student builds their own deck.** From pick one, they are choosing. The picks are small, concrete decisions: this creature or that spell. By the end they have forty cards they chose, and they understand each one because they chose it.

**The student sees every card type in context.** A draft shows a new player creatures, instants, sorceries, enchantments, artifacts and lands, not as a vocabulary list but as options in a pack. They learn by comparing.

**The playing field is level.** Everyone drafts from the same pool. The student's deck is as strong as the choices they made. When they win a game, and they will, it is theirs.

**The teacher sits next to them.** During a draft the experienced players can look over the new player's shoulder, ask "why that one?", and explain without stopping the game. Nobody is waiting. The explaining is the activity.

## How do you run a cube draft for beginners?

The format needs a few adjustments when half the table is new.

### Choose the right cube

A pauper or peasant cube is far better for teaching than a powered one. Commons and uncommons do simple things. A card that says "destroy target creature" teaches the game. A card with four paragraphs of text and a delayed trigger does not. The comparison of [pauper cube versus vintage cube](/articles/pauper-cube-vs-vintage-cube/) covers why the cheap cube is often the better one.

### Slow the draft down

Standard draft etiquette is to pick in under thirty seconds. Throw that out. Let new players read every card. Let them ask what a word means. A teaching draft takes twice as long and that is fine.

### Pair experienced players with new ones

Seat a veteran to the right of each beginner, since the right-hand neighbor passes to them in two of three packs. The veteran can nudge a color their way. Not every table does this, but it helps the first time.

### Explain archetypes before the draft

Spend five minutes on the two-color pairs the cube supports. "Red-white is fast creatures. Blue-black is removal and card draw. Green is big creatures." Give each new player a one-sentence plan to aim at. It turns forty-five picks from overwhelming to directed.

### Build decks together

After the draft, do deck construction out loud. "Sort by mana cost. Count your creatures. You want about sixteen. You want seventeen lands." The veteran players build at the same time, so nobody is being watched.

### Play slow, open-handed games first

For round one, let new players play with hands revealed, or let them ask the table questions mid-game. The point is reps. Competitive integrity can wait for round two.

## What rules should you teach first, and what can wait?

The full rules of Magic run to hundreds of pages. A new player needs about ten concepts for a first game. Here is a rough order.

| Teach in the first ten minutes | Teach when it comes up | Skip entirely at first |
|---|---|---|
| Lands make mana; one per turn | The stack and responding | Layers and timestamps |
| Creatures attack and block | Instants during combat | Priority passing in detail |
| Life totals; 20 to 0 | Summoning sickness (first time it matters) | Replacement effects |
| The five colors and what they like | Trample, flying, first strike | Mulligans beyond "shuffle and draw six" |
| Mana cost and the curve | Planeswalkers | State-based actions |
| Draw, play land, cast, attack, pass | Graveyard interaction | Anything with "layers" in the rulebook |

The principle: teach rules at the moment they matter. A new player who learns "trample" because they just got hit by it remembers trample. A new player who learns twelve keywords from a sheet remembers none.

## What mistakes do teachers make?

A few patterns that undo an otherwise good session.

- **Correcting every misplay.** Let them make the wrong attack. Mention it after the game if it mattered. In-game corrections make people feel watched and stop them experimenting.
- **Playing to win against the beginner.** Play well, but not cruelly. You do not need to counter their one big spell in their first game.
- **Over-explaining the stack.** The stack is important and the stack is boring. Introduce it when an instant is cast in response to something, and keep the explanation to one sentence.
- **Making them keep score of the rules.** You handle rules questions. They handle decisions. Split the cognitive load that way for the first few sessions.
- **Ending on a loss.** If the new player is 0-2, find a way to play a casual game three they can win. It is not charity. It is pedagogy.

## Why does teaching this way build a group?

Teaching Magic with a cube is not just efficient. It changes who the game is for.

Someone who learns from starter decks learns alone and needs to buy cards to keep playing. Someone who learns from a cube learns at a table, in front of seven people who now know their name, and needs nothing to come back next week except an invitation. The cube is a shared object. Learning on it makes you part of the group that shares it.

That is why the groups that teach well tend to grow and the groups that teach badly tend to shrink to the same six people. If you are building a regular table, the guide on [how to run a cube night](/articles/how-to-run-a-cube-draft/) covers the hosting side of keeping newcomers around.

## FAQ

### What is the easiest way to learn Magic the Gathering?

Draft a low-power cube with a patient group. You learn the cards by choosing them and the rules by playing them, with experienced players beside you to answer questions.

### How long does it take to learn Magic?

Most people can play a complete game after about thirty minutes of explanation, and feel comfortable after three or four sessions. Mastery takes years, which is the point.

### Should beginners start with Commander or 1v1?

One-on-one games are easier to learn from because there is less happening at once. Commander is a fine second format once the basics are in place.

### Do you need to buy cards to learn Magic?

No. If someone you know owns a cube, you can learn and keep playing without buying a single card.
