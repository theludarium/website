The best games to play with grandparents who "don't play games" are the ones that look like something they already do. [Rummikub](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00000IZJB?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) looks like gin rummy with tiles. [Qwirkle](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1933054395?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) looks like dominoes. Codenames looks like a word puzzle. [Ticket to Ride](https://link.amazon/B0jh8RB7f) looks like a map. Almost every grandparent who claims not to play games played cards, dominoes, or Scrabble for decades. They do not dislike games. They dislike learning new rules in front of grandchildren.

Once you see it that way, the job changes. You are not converting someone. You are finding the modern game that sits closest to the one they already know.

## Why do grandparents say they don't play games?

Ask a few and the real reasons come out. Almost none of them are about games.

- **They don't want to be taught by a nine-year-old.** Being the slowest learner at the table is uncomfortable at any age, and more so when the teacher is your grandchild.
- **The last game they tried was the wrong one.** Someone put Catan in front of them at Christmas with a twenty-minute rules explanation. That is a hard first game at seventy.
- **The pieces are small and the text is smaller.** A lot of modern games have tiny icons and gray-on-beige cards. That is a design failure, not a grandparent failure.
- **They think games are for kids.** Monopoly and Candy Land shaped that impression. They have not seen what a good adult game looks like.
- **They don't want to hold things up.** Slow turns feel like a burden on the family. A game with short, simple turns removes that worry.

Every one of these has a fix, and most of the fixes are about choosing the game, not persuading the person.

## What makes a game work for a grandparent?

Five things, roughly in order of importance.

1. **A familiar hook.** Cards, tiles, words, or a map. Something they can look at and say "oh, it's like..."
2. **Big pieces and readable text.** Rummikub tiles, Qwirkle blocks, and [Azul](https://link.amazon/B0bGRYHex) tiles are all large and high contrast. Avoid games with small cards full of icons.
3. **Short turns, no clock.** Nothing timed, nothing that punishes thinking. Codenames has a timer in the box. Leave it there.
4. **Talking allowed.** The best grandparent games have gaps for conversation. Rummikub and Qwirkle run fine with a story going on across the table.
5. **No one gets eliminated.** Sitting out is lonely. Everyone plays to the end.

Physical comfort matters too. A chair with a back, a table they can reach across, and good light. A surprising number of "I don't like games" objections are about a hard dining chair for two hours.

Rummikub deserves its spot at the top. It was designed decades ago, it is in a lot of retirement community game rooms already, and it has a quality the others lack: a grandparent might know it better than you do. Being the expert at the table for once changes everything.

## How do you get a reluctant grandparent to the table?

Do not ask them to play a game. Start playing one near them.

Set up Qwirkle at the kitchen table while they are having coffee. Play a few turns with a kid. Grandparents who would decline an invitation will lean over and ask what the blue square does, and a minute later they have a hand of tiles. This works because watching first removes the fear of being taught in front of everyone.

A few other approaches that work:

- **Ask them to teach you their game.** Euchre, pinochle, cribbage, canasta, dominoes. Learn it properly. Then, a visit or two later, suggest Skull King "because it's a bit like euchre."
- **Put them on a team.** In Codenames, pair the grandparent with a grandchild. Two heads, shared blame, and a built-in reason to talk.
- **Give them a job.** Banker, scorekeeper, tile-drawer. A role at the table without the pressure of playing, which usually turns into playing.
- **Keep the first game short.** Twenty minutes, then stop, even if it is going well. Especially then.

## Why does it matter?

Because the table is one of the few places where a seven-year-old and a seventy-five-year-old are equals. Outside a game, their relationship runs through adults. In a game of Qwirkle, they are two players with the same tiles and the same goal, and the grandchild sometimes wins.

There is a health case as well. The [Harvard Study of Adult Development](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2017/04/over-nearly-80-years-harvard-study-has-been-showing-how-to-live-a-healthy-and-happy-life/), which has followed participants since 1938, has consistently found that close relationships are among the strongest predictors of health and happiness late in life; satisfaction with relationships at fifty predicted health at eighty better than cholesterol did. Regular, in-person, low-pressure time with family is one of the most direct ways to keep those relationships alive. A standing game of Rummikub on Sunday afternoon is that, in a box.

We have written about the broader version of this, [four generations at one table](/articles/multigenerational-board-games/), and about why [games may be good for aging brains](/articles/board-games-and-dementia/). But the short version is simple. People who play together stay in each other's lives.

## What should you avoid?

- **Games with lots of small icons or text.** Many modern strategy games. Save them for later or skip them.
- **Timers.** Anything with a sand timer or app clock adds stress for no benefit.
- **Trivia.** It tests knowledge instead of creating time together, and generational knowledge gaps make it lopsided in both directions.
- **Long teaches.** If the explanation runs past five minutes, pick a different game for the first visit. See our guide to [teaching a game in five minutes](/articles/how-to-teach-a-board-game/) for making the explanation shorter.
- **Direct attack games.** Games where you take things from other players are a bad first impression. Grandparents often will not attack a grandchild, and then they lose, and then they are done.

## FAQ

### What is the best board game to play with grandparents?

Rummikub. It is familiar, the tiles are large, turns are simple, and many grandparents already know it. Qwirkle is the best choice if they have never played anything.

### What games can grandparents play with young grandchildren?

Qwirkle, Sequence, [Outfoxed!](https://link.amazon/B0hYNduxd), and [Zingo!](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01DY818JG?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) all work with children as young as five or six and are easy on an adult of any age. Our list of [board games for 5-year-olds](/articles/board-games-for-5-year-olds/) has more.

### How do you get older parents interested in board games?

Pick a game that resembles one they already know, let them watch a round first, keep the first session under thirty minutes, and never put them in a position to be taught by a child.

### Are board games good for seniors?

Regular game play is linked in several studies with better mood and thinking skills in older adults. A [20-year French cohort study](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/3/8/e002998) found board game players had a lower risk of depression and slower cognitive decline, and an [Edinburgh study of people in their seventies](https://www.ed.ac.uk/research-innovation/latest-research-news/games-can-protect-thinking-skills-in-older-age) found that those who played more non-digital games held onto memory and processing speed better. Both are observational, so nobody can say how much is the game and how much is the company. Either way, it is time well spent.
