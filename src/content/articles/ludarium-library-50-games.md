A board game lounge library is not a collection. A collection is what you love. A library is what works for strangers who walked in an hour ago. The two overlap, but not as much as most new owners expect. This is the list of fifty games we would put on the shelves of The Ludarium in Indianapolis on day one, and the reasoning behind each slot.

Fifty is deliberate. It is small enough that staff can teach every game and large enough to cover every group that walks in. A bigger library mostly adds dust. We wrote about that trade-off in [the library problem](/articles/board-game-cafe-library/).

## How do you choose games for a lounge library?

Every game on this list had to do a job, and we sorted the shelves by job rather than genre, because that is how people ask. Nobody says "I want a mid-weight Euro." They say "there are six of us and we have an hour."

The criteria for every slot:

- Teachable by staff in under ten minutes, most under five.
- Durable components that survive hundreds of plays and the occasional drink.
- Replaceable. If a piece goes missing, we can get another copy.
- A clear player count and time, so the host can match it to the table.
- Earns a second play. Guests should leave wanting to come back for it.

We also kept a balance between party games, light strategy, cooperative, two-player, and heavier picks. A library that is all one kind of game serves one kind of guest.

## What did we leave out, and why?

Some notable absences, with reasons.

Gloomhaven and other campaign games. A campaign needs the same people at the same table week after week, and a public library cannot hold a box's state for one group. We may run campaigns as scheduled programs instead.

Monopoly, Risk, and other long classics. A lounge library should offer what a living room does not.

Cards Against Humanity. Monikers, Skull, and Coup serve that crowd and stay funny longer. We made the case in [our card game list](/articles/adult-card-games/).

Most Kickstarter exclusives. They cannot be replaced when a piece goes missing, and in a library, pieces go missing.

## How would this library change over time?

Slowly. We would swap about five games a year: pull the ones nobody has requested in six months, add new releases that passed our tests. Faster change confuses regulars and overloads staff.

We would also keep extra copies of the heaviest hitters. On a Friday night, Codenames and Ticket to Ride both get asked for by more than one table.

Each game would get a printed card with the one-sentence teach, the real play time, and the right player count. The game on the shelf is half the product. Getting it to the table fast is the other half.

## FAQ

### How many games should a board game lounge have?

Fifty to a hundred well-chosen games cover almost every group. Larger libraries look impressive but are harder to teach and maintain.

### What are the essential board games for a game cafe?

Codenames, Ticket to Ride, Azul, Pandemic, and a two-player game like Patchwork. Those five handle most tables; the rest of this list fills in the edges.

### Should a lounge library include heavy games?

A few. Wingspan, Terraforming Mars, and Scythe draw dedicated players and make the shelf look serious, but they should be a small share of the total.

### How do you keep library games in good condition?

Sleeve the card games, keep food away from the table, and store boxes flat. Replace the most-played games every year or two rather than nursing worn copies.
