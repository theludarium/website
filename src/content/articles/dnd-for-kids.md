D&D for kids works, and it works earlier than most parents expect, as long as the adult running it changes three things: shorten everything, simplify the numbers, and let the kids drive. A seven-year-old cannot sit through a four-hour dungeon crawl. A seven-year-old can absolutely spend ninety minutes negotiating with a dragon who has lost its glasses.

This is a guide for the parent, aunt, teacher, or older sibling who has been asked, or has decided, to run a game for children. It covers what ages work, what to change, which systems fit, and the handful of table rules that keep the evening from falling apart.

## What age can kids start playing D&D?

There is no hard floor, but a rough map helps.

**Ages 5 to 7:** Full D&D is too much, but the core activity (pretend, with a grown-up describing the world and a die to decide close calls) works beautifully. Use one die, one or two numbers, and sessions under an hour. Several games are designed for exactly this range.

**Ages 8 to 10:** Simplified D&D works. Kids this age can track a short character sheet, understand "roll high," and follow a two-hour story if it moves. They will want to fight things and they will also want to adopt every animal they meet.

**Ages 11 and up:** Most kids can play the standard rules with a patient GM. The current starter set, [Heroes of the Borderlands](https://shop.asmodee.ca/en/wtcd4705), is rated for ages 12 and up, and many school and library programs run D&D for middle schoolers. Reading level matters more than age; a child who reads comfortably can handle a character sheet.

The best test is not age but attention. If a child can sit through a chapter book being read aloud and ask questions about it, they can play an RPG pitched at their level.

## What should you change about the rules?

Simplify in four places.

**Fewer numbers.** A standard D&D character sheet has dozens of entries. For younger kids, reduce it to three or four: how tough they are, how good at fighting, how good at sneaking, how good at talking. Use the d20 and tell them "high is good." That is enough rules for a complete game.

**One action per turn.** Standard combat lets a character move, act, and sometimes do a bonus thing. Kids under ten do better with "on your turn, you do one thing." It keeps turns short, which keeps the kids who are not acting from drifting.

**No character death without consent.** When a kid's character drops to zero, they are knocked out, captured, or rescued, not dead. A child who loses a character they built can be done with the hobby for a year. Danger should feel real; consequences should be recoverable.

**Shorter everything.** Sessions of sixty to ninety minutes. Fights of three rounds. Dungeons of three rooms. Kids experience time differently, and a "short" adult session is an eternity to an eight-year-old. End while they are still asking for more; that is how you get a second session.

## Which systems work best for kids?

You do not have to use D&D. Several games were built for children and do a better job at younger ages.

| System | Best ages | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| No Thank You, Evil! | 5–9 | Built for kids; simple dice, bright components, a creature called a "snarf" |
| Hero Kids | 4–10 | Very simple, short adventures, one page of rules for players |
| Amazing Tales | 4–8 | A single die per skill and a story built with the child; parent-and-kid format |
| Kids on Bikes | 10+ | Small-town mystery; collaborative town-building that kids love |
| Simplified D&D | 8+ | The real thing, trimmed; good when kids already know the brand |
| Full D&D (starter set) | 11+ | Standard rules with a patient GM |

For a mixed-age family table, simplified D&D is often the best compromise. The older kids get the brand they asked for, the younger kids get a sheet with four numbers, and the GM runs both at once. ## What table rules keep a kids' game fun?

The rules of the game matter less than the rules of the table. These six are the ones we would not run a kids' session without.

**1. One voice at a time.** State it at the start, and enforce it gently. A talking stick, a toy, or "the dice are the microphone" all work. Kids will interrupt; the fix is a reminder, not a lecture.

**2. Everyone gets a turn in every scene.** Not just in combat. If the kids are talking to the mayor, go around the table and ask each one what their character says or does. The quiet kid will never claim the spotlight unless you hand it over.

**3. Yes, and then a roll.** Kids will try things no adult would think of. The gnome wants to befriend the giant spider. The fighter wants to use the dragon as a slide. Let them try. Set a target number and roll. "No" is the fastest way to lose a kid's interest.

**4. Villains can be scary, not cruel.** Kids like being frightened a little. They do not like being made to feel small. The monster roars and chases; it does not mock. And the villain always has a weakness the kids can find.

**5. Snacks after the fight, not during.** Dice, miniatures, and sticky fingers do not mix. Build a snack break into the session as a reward for finishing a scene. It also serves as a natural reset for attention.

**6. Stop on time.** Whatever you said the length was, keep it. Kids will beg for more. That is the goal. "Next time" is the best cliffhanger there is.

## How do you handle the common problems?

A few situations come up at nearly every kids' table.

**The kid who wants to attack everything.** Give them something that needs hitting early, so the urge is satisfied. Then introduce a problem that cannot be solved by hitting: a locked door, a crying creature, a puzzle. Most kids pivot fast once they see talking works too.

**The kid who will not decide.** Offer two options. "Do you want to sneak past or walk up and say hello?" A choice between two is easy; a choice among infinity is a wall. This is also the fix for adults, as we cover in [roleplaying tips for beginners](/articles/roleplaying-tips-for-beginners/).

**The kid who is upset by a bad roll.** Acknowledge it, make the failure interesting instead of punishing, and move on quickly. "You slip, but you grab the ledge, and now you can see something under the bridge nobody else noticed." Failure that reveals something is failure kids will accept.

**Siblings fighting in character.** Separate the fiction from the feeling. If the conflict is fun for both, fine. If one is upset, pause, say "out of game for a second," and reset. The game is never worth a real fight.

**The adult who is tempted to take over.** You will see the clever solution before they do. Do not say it. Ask a question that points toward it, and let them find it. The point is their story, not yours.

## Why is this worth doing?

Because an RPG asks children to do several hard things at once, and makes them fun. Take turns. Listen to what someone else just said and build on it. Imagine how another person feels. Make a decision with incomplete information and live with the result. Lose a roll and keep playing.

Those are not game skills. They are the skills we spend years trying to teach kids in other ways, and at the table they practice them for ninety minutes without noticing. A family that plays together also gets something rarer: a shared story that belongs only to them, with inside jokes that will surface at dinner for years. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### What age is appropriate for D&D?

Simplified versions work from around age 5 to 7 with an adult running it. Standard D&D rules suit most kids from about 11, depending on reading level and attention span.

### How long should a D&D session for kids be?

Sixty to ninety minutes for children under ten; up to two hours for older kids. Stop while they still want more.

### Is D&D too violent for kids?

It does not have to be. The GM controls the tone, can keep fights cartoonish, and can make talking and puzzles as rewarding as combat. No character needs to die.

### What is the best RPG for young children?

Games designed for kids, such as No Thank You, Evil!, Hero Kids, or Amazing Tales, work better than full D&D under about age 8.
