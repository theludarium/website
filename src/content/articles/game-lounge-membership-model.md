The game lounge membership model is simple to describe and hard to get right: enough people pay a monthly fee that the room covers its own costs before anyone buys a coffee. This article works through the math as we are using it to plan The Ludarium in Indianapolis. The numbers below are illustrative, not quotes, because we have not signed a lease or set a price. But the shape of the arithmetic is the shape of the business, and we would rather show it than hide it.

## What does a game lounge have to pay for?

Every venue's costs are different, but the categories are the same. Here is ours, in rough order of size.

1. **Rent and occupancy.** The lease, utilities, insurance, property taxes passed through. For a lounge, this is usually the largest fixed cost and the one that kills venues that get it wrong.
2. **People.** At least one host on every shift whose job is to teach games and know names. This is not optional. A library without a teacher is a shelf.
3. **The library and furniture.** A one-time cost to open, then a steady replacement budget. We cover the opening list in [how we're building the library](/articles/building-a-game-lounge-library/).
4. **Food and drink cost of goods.** Only if we serve, which we plan to.
5. **Everything else.** Software, cleaning, payment processing, marketing, the inevitable broken chair.

The important thing is which of these are fixed and which move with attendance. Rent and core staff are fixed. They are due whether the room is full or empty. That is the number membership has to cover.

## How many members does it take to cover rent?

Here is the arithmetic, with made-up but plausible figures so you can see it work.

Suppose fixed monthly costs (rent, utilities, insurance, one full-time host, software) come to a round number, call it X. Suppose a full membership is priced at M per month. Then break-even on fixed costs is simply X divided by M.

| Monthly fixed costs | Membership price | Members to cover fixed costs |
|---|---|---|
| $12,000 | $40 | 300 |
| $12,000 | $60 | 200 |
| $18,000 | $40 | 450 |
| $18,000 | $60 | 300 |

Those dollar figures are illustrative. Real rent in a real Indianapolis neighborhood will land where it lands, and we will share it when we know it. <!-- EDITOR: add detail once a space is identified --> But notice the range: somewhere between a couple hundred and a few hundred full-price members, before food, drink, events, or guest covers contribute anything.

That is a lot of members for a room that does not exist yet. It is also roughly the size of a healthy mid-sized gym's active base, or a coworking space's desk count, which is why we keep pointing at those models. It is a known size of community. People have built it before.

## Why is churn the number that matters most?

Price gets all the attention. Churn decides whether the business lives.

Say we reach 300 members. If 5 percent leave each month, we lose 15 people a month and have to recruit 15 just to stand still. If 10 percent leave, it is 30. Over a year, a 10 percent monthly churn means we have to replace the entire membership base to end where we started.

Membership businesses that work, gyms, clubs, coworking, all fight the same fight, and they win it the same way: by making the place part of someone's week. A member who has a standing Tuesday table with the same four people does not cancel. A member who came twice in January and meant to come back does.

So the real work of the membership model is not sales. It is programming. Leagues, campaign tables, learn-to-play nights, the host who notices you have not been in. Every one of those is a churn tool before it is anything else. We will write about how we program a week in its own article.

## How do food, drink, and events change the picture?

They turn break-even into a business.

Once fixed costs are covered by membership, every coffee, every beer, every corporate session, and every guest cover is margin. This is the opposite of a cover-charge cafe, where food and drink have to carry rent and the room gets designed to push volume on Friday night.

We think about the stack like this:

- **Membership** covers the building and the people. Predictable, recurring, the base.
- **Food and drink** add per-visit revenue without a per-visit decision. A member who has already paid to be there buys a coffee without thinking about it.
- **Corporate events** are high-margin, daytime, and bring in people who have never heard of us. They also run before the room exists, which is why we are starting there. See [why corporate game nights come before the space](/articles/corporate-game-nights-before-a-space/).
- **Guest covers and day passes** are the top of the funnel. They should be cheap, because their job is to produce members, not revenue.

The trap, and we have watched other venues fall into it, is letting the supporting lines quietly become the plan. If food margin starts covering rent, the kitchen starts designing the room. We want membership to carry fixed costs on its own so everything else stays optional.

## What about tiers?

In [how a game lounge membership works](/articles/game-lounge-membership/) we described the common three-tier structure: a cheaper off-peak community tier, a full tier, and a founding or patron tier. The math changes a little with tiers, and mostly for the better.

Off-peak members use the room when it would otherwise be empty, so they are close to pure margin on the building. Patron members pay more than the room costs them because they want the place to exist. Full-tier members are the core. A realistic mix might be a third off-peak, half full, and a small group of patrons, which produces an average revenue per member somewhat below the full price, but a room that is used across the whole day instead of just the evening.

We will not price tiers until we have a space and a real cost base. But we will test willingness to pay before that, with a founding tier offered to people who have followed the build. The founding tier is not about the money. It is about finding out whether 300 people in Indianapolis will actually pay, before we sign anything that assumes they will.

## What could break this?

Three things keep us up.

**Rent too high for the member count.** The coworking collapse was a lease problem, not an idea problem. Long expensive leases against short flexible memberships. We plan to grow into space, not ahead of it, even if the first room is smaller than we would like.

**Opening before the community exists.** An empty lounge churns its first members fast, because the thing they paid for, the people, is not there yet. Content, events, and the newsletter list are how we build the community before the door.

**Underestimating hospitality.** This is a hospitality business with a membership layer. Clean bathrooms, good coffee, and a host who remembers your name matter more than the game library. We have no illusions about that, and we will hire for it.

## FAQ

### How many members does a game lounge need to be profitable?

It depends entirely on rent and staffing. As an illustrative range, a few hundred full-price members covers fixed costs in a modest space, with food, drink, and events providing the profit on top.

### What is a reasonable price for a game lounge membership?

Most venues using the model land near a gym membership in the same city. We have not set ours and will publish it when we do.

### Why not just charge a cover like a board game cafe?

A cover charge ties revenue to Friday and Saturday volume and makes every visit a purchase decision. Membership produces predictable revenue and makes showing up free at the margin, which is what a weekly habit needs.

### Is a game lounge membership model risky?

The main risks are signing a lease the membership cannot carry and opening before there is a community to fill the room. Both are avoidable with patience, which is why we are building the community first.
