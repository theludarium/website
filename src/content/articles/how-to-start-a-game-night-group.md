If you want to know how to start a board game group, the answer is less about games and more about calendars. Pick a night. Make it the same night every time. Play whatever shows up. Groups that last are not built on a great first session. They are built on the twentieth ordinary one.

That sounds too simple to be the whole answer. It nearly is. Most game nights die for one reason: nobody knows when the next one is. The host waits until everyone is free, which is never, and six weeks later the group is a memory. The fix is a standing night, and the rest of this article is about how to run one.

## Why do most game nights fade out after three sessions?

A first game night usually goes well. The host picks a crowd-pleaser like Codenames or Ticket to Ride, people laugh, someone says "we should do this more often." Then the second one takes three weeks of texting to schedule. The third one gets cancelled when two people drop out. There is no fourth.

The problem is not interest. The problem is that every session requires a fresh decision from every person. Each decision is a chance to say no. Multiply that by six people and a dozen sessions, and the odds run out.

A standing night removes the decision. Thursday is game night. You either come or you do not, but nobody has to plan anything. The event exists whether or not any individual shows up. That one change does more for longevity than any game you could buy.

## How often should a board game group meet?

More often than feels comfortable, at a lower intensity than you think.

Weekly is the sweet spot for most groups. Every two weeks works. Monthly is the floor, and monthly groups tend to drift because a single missed session means two months between tables. A friend you see every two months is an acquaintance.

The reason weekly works is not that people have more time. It is that short gaps make the habit cheap to keep. Nobody needs to catch up on three months of life before the first card is dealt. Conversation picks up where it left off. The table becomes a place rather than an event.

Trade length for frequency. A two-hour Thursday that happens fifty times a year beats a six-hour Saturday that happens four times. If you are choosing between a weekly night of short games and a monthly marathon of Gloomhaven, choose the weekly night. You can add the marathon later.

## The five rules of a standing game night

These are the mechanics. None of them are clever. All of them matter.

1. **Fixed night, fixed time, fixed place.** Thursday at 7, your dining room, every week. Do not poll. Do not rotate. If the night does not work for someone, they come when they can. The group is not built around any one schedule.
2. **It happens with two.** This is the rule that saves groups. If only two people show, you play a two-player game. Patchwork, Jaipur, 7 Wonders Duel, and Lost Cities exist for exactly this night. The session never gets cancelled for low turnout, because cancelling teaches people that attendance is optional in both directions.
3. **No RSVP required.** Asking people to commit in advance makes attendance feel like a favor. A standing invitation means people show up when they are free and feel no guilt when they are not. Attendance goes up when the pressure goes down.
4. **The host does not cook.** If the host has to make dinner, the host burns out by month three. People eat before they come or bring something. Keep snacks simple. The table is the point, not the menu.
5. **Hard stop.** Name an end time and keep it. People with early mornings and young kids will only keep coming if they trust they will be home by 10. A game night that reliably ends on time is one people can say yes to every week.

## What games keep a recurring group coming back?

The games for a standing night are different from the games for a one-off. You want titles that teach fast, play in under an hour, and get better with repetition. You also want a range, because you will not know who is coming until they arrive.

A small shelf covers most nights. Something like this:

| Player count | Reliable picks | Teach time |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Patchwork, Jaipur, 7 Wonders Duel | 5 min |
| 3–4 | Azul, Wingspan, The Crew, Cascadia | 5–15 min |
| 5–6 | Codenames, Wavelength, Just One, Ticket to Ride | 5 min |
| 7+ | Codenames, Wavelength, Decrypto, Skull | 5 min |

Notice what is missing. No four-hour games. Nothing with a forty-minute rules teach. Those have a place, but not on the recurring night. Save the big boxes for a scheduled Saturday once the group has proven it exists.

Repetition is a feature. A group that has played The Crew twenty times is playing a different and richer game than a group playing it for the first time. The inside jokes, the known tendencies, the running tallies all come from playing the same games again. Novelty is overrated. Depth comes from the fourth play, not the fourth game.

## How do you grow a game group without breaking it?

Groups of three or four are fragile. One person moves away and the group is in trouble. Six to ten regulars, with four to six showing on any given night, is the stable range.

Growth should be slow and by invitation. Each regular can bring someone, with one rule: the new person is treated as a guest of the table, not of the person who brought them. Everyone learns their name. Someone else teaches them the game. If they come three times, they are a regular.

Avoid the public meetup trap early on. Open-invite events on social platforms bring strangers who do not return, and the churn is exhausting. Build the core first. Once eight people reliably show, the group can absorb newcomers without losing its character. If you are new to a city and need to find a core in the first place, start with [the approach that works for people who just moved](/articles/make-friends-in-a-new-city-board-games/) and then stabilize it with a standing night.

Be honest about fit. Someone who sulks after a loss or lectures the table through every turn will cost you two other regulars. Address it kindly and early. Most of the time it is a habit, not a character flaw, and [learning to lose well is a skill the table teaches](/articles/how-to-be-a-good-loser/) if someone gives it a nudge.

## Why does the standing night matter beyond the games?

Here is the part that is easy to miss. A standing game night is one of the few structures in adult life that produces repeated, unplanned, low-stakes contact with the same people. Childhood is full of that. School, practice, the bus. Adulthood strips it away. Most adult friendships thin out not because anyone chose it but because nothing on the calendar forces the contact.

The research on this is consistent. The [Harvard Study of Adult Development](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2017/04/over-nearly-80-years-harvard-study-has-been-showing-how-to-live-a-healthy-and-happy-life/), which has followed participants since 1938, found that how satisfied people were with their relationships at 50 predicted their physical health at 80 better than their cholesterol levels did. The [U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf) on loneliness put the mortality risk of lacking social connection on par with smoking up to 15 cigarettes a day. Those are large claims, and worth checking, but the direction is not in dispute.

A game night does not fix loneliness by itself. What it does is manufacture the thing friendship needs most, which is time together with no agenda. The game provides the agenda so the people do not have to. Two hours of Wingspan is two hours of being in a room with people who know you, every week, for years. Few things on an adult calendar can say that.

This is what we mean by togethering. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### How many people do you need to start a board game group?

Two. Start with whoever will show up, play two-player games on thin nights, and let the group grow by invitation to a stable core of six to ten regulars.

### What is the best night of the week for game night?

Whichever night the host can hold every week without exception. Weeknights tend to work better than weekends because they compete with less and end on time naturally.

### What if people keep cancelling?

Stop asking them to RSVP and keep hosting anyway. A night that happens with two people and ends on time builds the trust that brings the cancellers back.

### How long should a weekly game night last?

Two to three hours with a hard stop. Short enough to say yes to every week, long enough for two or three games.

## Start your own standing night

Pick the night this week. Text four people one sentence: "Thursdays at 7 are game night at my place, starting this week, come whenever you can." Then hold the night whether two show or six.
