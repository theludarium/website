Every stage of building a collection has been about this year's table. A lifetime board game library is about the table in 20 years: your kids grown, your friends older, a Thanksgiving where someone pulls a worn box off the shelf and says "we have to play this one." Most games you buy will not be in that scene. A few will. The lifetime library is the practice of noticing which, and keeping them.

## What a lifetime board game library is for

Think about the games that are already there for you. The deck of cards at your grandparents' house. The chess set someone taught you on. The copy of whatever your family played at the lake, box corners taped, a missing piece replaced with a button. Those were lifetime games, and nobody chose them on purpose. They just lasted.

The lifetime library is the choice to do that deliberately. Out of the 30 or 50 games a curator keeps, maybe eight or ten will still be played in two decades. Those are the ones that deserve the best shelf, the extra care, and the replacement copy when the first one wears out. Everything else can rotate.

This is also where the [One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/) reaches its final form. A lifetime game is the one copy that serves not just a group but a family across generations. One box, played by three generations, is the best cost per play that exists.

## What makes a board game last 20 years?

Not every great game is a lifetime game. Some of the best games on your shelf are brilliant for a particular group at a particular moment and nothing else. The ones that last tend to share four qualities.

### Durable

Physically, first. A lifetime game survives spills, moves, toddlers, and a hundred teaches. Wooden pieces outlast plastic; thick tiles outlast thin cards; a game with few components has fewer to lose. Hive is close to indestructible. [Carcassonne](https://link.amazon/B012Rr5ed)'s tiles will still be fine in 2046.

Durable also means durable in design. The game should not depend on a rules edition that gets revised every few years, a companion app, or components that go out of print. Simple rules that fit on one card are the most durable thing a game can have.

### Teachable across ages

A lifetime game has to be taught by a 10-year-old to a grandparent and the other way around. [Ticket to Ride](https://link.amazon/B0jh8RB7f) can be. [Catan](https://link.amazon/B0h8BDGR2) can be. [Terraforming Mars](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01GSYA4K2?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) cannot, however much you love it. The teach has to survive the teacher forgetting half of it and still produce a good game.

This matters because the people at a lifetime table change. The group of four who learned a game in their twenties becomes a group of twelve with kids and in-laws. If the game cannot stretch to meet them, it stays in the box.

### Scales in player count

Twenty years is a long time for a table to stay the same size. A lifetime game works at two on a quiet night and at six when everyone is home. [Codenames](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B014Q1XX9S?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) does this; it is as good with four as with a dozen. Dominion plays at two and at four without feeling like a different game. A game that is only good at exactly four will have long stretches of no plays as the household shifts around it.

### Evergreen

Some games are tied to their moment. A clever twist that was fresh in its year, a theme from a show that aired once, a mechanic that every later game did better. Evergreen games are the ones whose appeal does not depend on novelty. Chess is the extreme case. Among modern games, Carcassonne, Catan, Ticket to Ride, Codenames, and [Pandemic](https://link.amazon/B0eHP5GCg) have all passed the first test, which is being as good on play 50 as on play 5. A game that is best the first time is not a lifetime game.

## How to think in decades

The practical shift is in the time horizon of the question you ask before buying or keeping. The curator asks "will this get played this year?" The lifetime librarian asks "will this get played in a decade, by people who are not at the table yet?"

That question changes a few things.

**It makes you keep outgrown games.** A game your kids have aged out of at twelve will be exactly right for their own kids at six. Hold it. Shelf space is cheaper than finding a copy in 15 years.

**It makes you value the second edition less.** The version your family learned is the version that has the memories in it. Upgrades are for games you play for the mechanics. Lifetime games are played for the people, and the people remember the old board.

**It makes you buy a replacement copy early.** When a lifetime game starts to show wear, buy the next copy while the game is still in print. This is the one case where a second copy clearly passes the [second-copy test](/articles/when-a-second-copy-is-worth-it/): it creates every play that would otherwise end when the first copy falls apart.

**It makes you write things down.** Who taught you. The house rule your family uses. The year the piece went missing. A lifetime game accumulates a history, and the history is half of why anyone wants to play it. A note inside the lid takes a minute and lasts as long as the box.

## Which games on your shelf are candidates?

Go back to your play log and your annual reviews. The lifetime candidates are usually obvious once you look for them.

- Games that have survived three or more annual reviews without ever being a candidate to leave.
- Games that have been requested by name by more than one generation at your table.
- Games you have taught more than ten times and never once dreaded teaching.
- Games where the play count is high and still rising, year after year, rather than peaking and fading.
- Games your group calls by a nickname. That is a sign a game has become a place, not a product.

Most lifetime libraries, when people actually list them, are eight to twelve games and lean heavily toward the gateway, filler, and party roles from the [starter shelf](/articles/starter-shelf-40-to-300/). Heavy stretches are rare on the list, not because they are worse but because they need a particular group, and groups change. The games that last are the ones that are good with whoever shows up.

## Passing games to the next generation

A lifetime library is not just kept; it is handed on. This is where the [Shared Shelf](/articles/one-copy-rule/) stops being a group of friends and becomes a family.

Handing a game on works best when it is done early and on purpose. Teach your kids to teach it, not just to play it. Let them run the setup. Let them explain it to their friends while you stay out of it. A game that a child can host is a game that will survive the move to their first apartment, and that is the moment most family games get lost.

Give the game itself at a meaningful point, with the history attached. The note inside the lid, the house rules, the story of who bought it and when. A copy of Carcassonne with twenty years of notes is a better inheritance than a new one, and the new one can always be bought.

And play with them as adults. The games you taught your children at eight are the games they will bring to your table at thirty, with their own kids learning on your floor. That is the whole point of a library built across decades. Togethering for a lifetime is not a slogan about any single game night. It is this: the same box, the same people, and the years between.

## FAQ

### What board games will still be played in 20 years?

The ones that are physically durable, teachable across ages, playable at two and at six, and as good on the fiftieth play as the fifth. Modern games that have already shown these qualities include Carcassonne, Catan, Ticket to Ride, Codenames, and Pandemic. Heavy strategy and campaign games rarely last because they need a specific group, and groups change over two decades.

### How do you build a board game collection that lasts?

Track your plays for years and notice which games survive every annual review, get requested by more than one generation, and are never dreaded as a teach. Keep those on the best shelf, buy a replacement copy while they are in print, and hold games your kids have outgrown for the next generation. Most lifetime libraries settle at eight to twelve games.

### Should you keep board games your kids have outgrown?

Yes, if they were played often and are in good shape. A game that was right for a six-year-old will be right for their own children or younger cousins, and finding a copy in 15 years may be hard or impossible. Shelf space is cheaper than a reprint. Add a note inside the lid about who played it and when; the history is part of what makes it worth keeping.

### What are the best family board games to pass down?

Games with simple rules, few components, and strong replay value. Carcassonne, Ticket to Ride, Catan, and Codenames are common examples among modern titles, alongside classics like chess and a standard deck of cards. The best game to pass down is the one your family already plays by name, with house rules and stories attached. Teach your kids to teach it, not just to play it.
