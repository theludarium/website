Cube vs Commander is the choice that decides what a Magic: The Gathering group becomes. They are the two most popular ways to play Magic with the same people every week without spending money on new cards each time. One asks a group to build decks on the spot from a shared pool. The other asks each player to bring a deck they built at home. The cards are the same. The evenings are not.

We have a stake in this one. The Ludarium's founder started Cube Commonwealth, a Cube community that met weekly for about three years and is relaunching as part of The Ludarium. So we will say up front: we love Cube. We will also be fair to Commander, because it is the format most Magic players actually play, and for good reasons.

## What is Commander?

**Commander** <!-- AFFILIATE --> is a multiplayer format, usually four players, where each player brings a 100-card deck built around a single legendary creature, the commander. Every other card in the deck must be a different card (singleton) and must match the commander's colors. Players start at 40 life instead of 20. The commander sits in a special zone, can be cast from there, and can return there when it dies or is exiled, at a rising cost. Games are free-for-all: last player standing wins, with the social politics that implies.

It is Magic's flagship casual format, with a formal ban list [managed by Wizards of the Coast since September 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander_(Magic:_The_Gathering)). Preconstructed Commander decks are sold in stores, so a new player can buy a deck and play that night.

## What is Cube?

**Cube** is a draft format where one person builds a curated collection, often [360 or 540 cards](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/540-vs-360-vs-720-whats-the-perfect-size-for-a-cube/), with exactly one copy of each. On game night, the cards are shuffled into fifteen-card packs, and players draft them the way you would draft a booster box: open a pack, take a card, pass the rest, repeat. Each player then builds a 40-card deck from what they drafted and plays one-on-one matches, usually in a small tournament. When the night is over, every card goes back into the Cube.

Nobody buys anything. The Cube owner curated it once; the group plays it forever. Cubes range from budget collections of commons to "vintage" Cubes holding the most powerful cards ever printed. The format has no official rules body; every Cube is its own design.

## Cube vs Commander: the side-by-side

| | Cube | Commander |
|---|---|---|
| Players | 4–8 drafting (8 ideal), then pairs | 3–5, usually 4 at a table |
| Time | 3–4 hours for a draft and three rounds | 1–2 hours per game, often two games a night |
| Weight (complexity) | Heavy: drafting plus deckbuilding plus play | Heavy: large decks and long board states |
| Teach time | Needs players who already know Magic; drafting itself takes 10 minutes to explain | Needs players who already know Magic; format rules take 5 minutes |
| What you bring | Nothing; the Cube owner brings everything | Your own deck |
| Ongoing cost | One person builds the Cube once | Each player buys and upgrades their own decks |
| Game type | One-on-one, best of three | Free-for-all multiplayer |
| Who decides the power level | The Cube owner, for everyone | Each player, which leads to mismatches |
| Elimination | Nobody sits out; losers play losers | Eliminated players wait for the game to end |
| What it's best at | Fairness, variety, and the skill of building under pressure | Self-expression, big plays, and drop-in play at any store |

Playtimes and player counts reflect common practice; neither format is defined by a publisher's box, though Wizards' [Commander page](https://magic.wizards.com/en/formats/commander) lists three to five players.

## Which one is easier to start?

Commander. You can buy a preconstructed deck, walk into almost any game store on Commander night, and sit down with three strangers. The format is everywhere. Every store runs it, every Magic content creator covers it, and the preconstructed decks are good enough to have fun with immediately. For someone who has just learned Magic and wants to play with people, there is no faster path.

Cube requires one person to have built a Cube, which is a real project: choosing hundreds of cards, sleeving them, and bringing them every week. Once a Cube exists, starting a night is simple, but somebody has to be that person.

## Which one is fairer?

Cube, and this is its strongest argument.

Every player at a Cube draft starts with the same thing: a seat and a pack. Nobody has an advantage from having spent more money or more hours at home. The person who drafts and builds best wins. The Cube owner sets one power level for the whole table, so there are no awkward games where one deck is worth two hundred dollars and another is worth twenty.

Commander's chronic problem is the power mismatch. Four people sit down with decks built to different budgets and different intentions, and someone's deck is either far too strong or far too weak for the table. The community has built a lot of etiquette around it, a conversation before the game about what everyone is playing, a [five-level bracket system](https://magic.wizards.com/en/formats/commander) from the publisher, and it still goes wrong regularly. A single over-tuned deck can make an evening miserable for three people.

## Which one has better social dynamics?

This is where Commander makes its case.

A Commander game is a conversation. Four players, open board, shifting alliances, and a lot of talking: "if you attack me, I'll have to deal with you, and then he wins." Threat assessment, deals, and betrayals are part of the design. Big splashy plays get reactions from the whole table. For a group that likes to talk and laugh and does not mind politics, Commander is a social game in a way one-on-one Magic is not.

The cost is the eliminated player. In a two-hour game, the first person knocked out might wait forty minutes for the rest. That is a lot of sitting.

Cube's social moment is the draft, where eight people sit around one table passing packs in silence punctuated by groans. After that, it is pairs playing matches, with the rest of the room talking between games. Nobody is ever eliminated; you play all three rounds whether you win or lose. The [kingmaking problem](/articles/kingmaking-board-games/) that haunts Commander does not exist, because every game has two people in it.

## Which one costs less over time?

Cube, by far, for everyone except the Cube owner.

A Commander player typically owns several decks and upgrades them as new cards release. It is a hobby with an ongoing bill, and it is easy to spend more on Commander in a year than a modest Cube costs to build.

A Cube is a single expense, and it can be as small as the owner wants. A Cube of commons and uncommons costs little and plays beautifully. After it is built, every draft night is free. Cube Commonwealth met weekly for about three years, and nobody paid for packs.

The catch is that the cost is concentrated on one person, who also carries the box, maintains the card list, and keeps the sleeves clean. In a healthy group the others chip in, bring food, or build a second Cube.

## Which one works for a drop-in night?

Commander. The format's great strength is that any four Magic players with decks can sit down anywhere, anytime. A [game lounge](/articles/where-to-play-magic-indianapolis/) can run Commander every night of the week with no organizer, no Cube, and no schedule beyond "show up."

Cube wants eight people at the same time and a three-hour block. It can run with six, or four with adjustments, but it is at its best as a planned event with a reliable group. That makes it ideal for a standing weekly night and poor for a spontaneous one.

## Verdict: which format should your group play?

**Play Commander if:**

- Your group wants to drop in and out week to week without planning.
- Players enjoy building and personalizing their own decks at home.
- The table likes politics, big plays, and long conversations.
- Nobody wants to be the person who builds and carries a Cube.

**Play Cube if:**

- You have four to eight people who can commit to the same night.
- Your group values fairness and wants skill to decide games.
- One person is willing to build and maintain the Cube, and the rest will help.
- You want a format that costs nothing after the first investment and never gets stale.

**Play both if** you can. Many groups do: Cube on the scheduled night, Commander when fewer people show up or when someone brings a friend. They solve different problems. Commander is the format of the game store. Cube is the format of the standing group. The Ludarium plans to host both in Indianapolis, starting with Cube Commonwealth's relaunch.

## FAQ

### Can beginners play Cube?

Yes, if they already know how to play Magic. Drafting and building a 40-card deck under time pressure is hard at first, so pair a new drafter with a veteran for their first night. A Cube built from simpler cards helps too.

### How many players do you need for Cube?

Eight is ideal for a full draft with three rounds of matches. Six works. Four works with a smaller pod and more packs per player. Two players can use Winston draft or grid draft.

### Is Commander good for new Magic players?

It is the most accessible way to find a game, because preconstructed decks are sold ready to play. The games themselves are long and complex, so a new player will have a lot to track.

### How much does it cost to build a Cube?

Anything from [under $100 for a pauper Cube](https://draftsim.com/pauper-cube-mtg/) of commons to tens of thousands of dollars or more for a [vintage Cube](https://mtg.wiki/page/Vintage_Cube) with the Power Nine, which is why most paper vintage Cubes use proxies. The format works at every budget because everyone drafts from the same pool.
