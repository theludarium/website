Once six games cover six roles, the question changes. You no longer ask "what am I missing?" You ask "where do we actually play?" To grow your board game collection from $300 to $1,000 well, you go deeper in the places your group already lives and resist the pull to go wider everywhere else. This is the core library stage, and it is where most collections either sharpen or sprawl.

This article is the fourth in Building Your Library on Purpose. It assumes a finished [starter shelf](/articles/starter-shelf-40-to-300/): gateway, filler, 2-player, party, co-op, stretch. If you are still filling those roles, finish that first. Everything here depends on having data from real plays.

## Grow your board game collection where your group plays

The starter shelf was built on coverage. The core library is built on evidence. By now you have months of plays logged, and the log tells a story: maybe your filler has been played 40 times and your stretch twice, or your two-player game carries every weeknight while the party game comes out at holidays.

That story is your purchase plan. The role with the most plays is where a new game will get played most. The role with the fewest plays is where a new game will sit. It is the opposite of how most people buy, which is toward the gap they feel rather than the table they have.

A simple rule for this stage: 70 percent of your spending goes to the two roles your log says you play most; the rest goes to everything else. The shelf is for the table, and the table has told you what it wants.

## When to buy your first expansion

Expansions are the natural first purchase in the core library, because they deepen a role you already know you use. But they are also the easiest way to spend $30 on something that changes a game you already love into a game you have to re-learn.

The test is plays. If a game has 15 or more logged plays and your group has started saying "we should try something different in this one," the expansion is probably worth it. If a game has four plays, the expansion will not rescue it; it will just raise its cost per play. Our full guide to [when expansions are worth it](/articles/expansions-worth-it/) covers the categories, but the short version is this: expand the game your group reaches for on its own, not the game you wish they reached for.

A second use of expansions worth knowing: they make the best gifts. If a friend owns the base game, an expansion is a present that lands on the [Shared Shelf](/articles/one-copy-rule/) and gets played by everyone, including you. That is covered in [Gifting as Library Strategy](/articles/gifting-as-library-strategy/), and it is the cheapest way to grow the group's library without buying a single new box yourself.

## Start tracking cost per play

If you kept a taste log through the $0 tier, you are already most of the way to tracking cost per play. Now add the price. The formula is simple: price divided by plays divided by players divided by hours. The [full explanation](/articles/cost-per-play-explained/) is in our Math of Game Night series, but at this stage you only need the first two steps.

As an illustration: a $40 game played 20 times is $2 per play. Divide by four players and it is 50 cents per person per play. A $60 game played twice is $30 per play, $7.50 per person. The second game is not worse; it is just far from paying for itself, and a new purchase in the same role will slow it down further.

Once you track this for a few months, something shifts. You stop buying games that look interesting and start buying games that will get played, because the log shows you which kinds those are. [Tracking your plays changes what you buy](/articles/tracking-plays-changes-what-you-buy/) is the single most reliable habit for a collection that stays useful. The Ludarium app, in development, is being built around this kind of play tracking; it is coming soon. A spreadsheet column labeled "plays" works today.

## Buying games for specific people

The core library is where you start buying with a name attached. Not a role, a person.

**Your spouse or partner.** If the two-player role is your busiest, this is where a second and third two-player game belongs. But go by their taste log, not yours. If they lit up at Azul and tolerated Patchwork, buy the next game that feels like Azul.

**Your kids.** Children age through a library faster than anything else. A game that was perfect at six is beneath them at nine. Buy the next step, not the current one, and keep the outgrown games; they come back for younger cousins and for your kids' own kids, which is the [lifetime library](/articles/lifetime-library/) thinking that this series ends on.

**One friend.** Every group has a person who loves one kind of game nobody else owns. The friend who wants a deep deck builder, or a heavy euro, or a bluffing game. Buying that game is a gift to the group's shared shelf and to that one friend's attendance. It is a purchase with a person's name on it, and it almost always gets played, because that person will make sure of it.

Buying for a person is the opposite of buying for a fantasy group. Our article on [matching games to your actual group](/articles/match-games-to-your-group/) covers this; the core library is where the principle becomes a budget line.

## The second game in a role

At the starter stage, the rule was absolute: no two games fill the same role. At the core library stage, it becomes a question. When does a second game in a role earn its place?

Ask the [second-copy test](/articles/when-a-second-copy-is-worth-it/), adapted for roles instead of copies: does the second game create plays that would not otherwise happen? A second filler probably does, because fillers get played constantly and variety keeps them fresh. A second party game does if your crowds differ, one for word people and one for the group that would rather act things out. A second stretch game, for a group that plays the first stretch twice a year, does not. It splits two plays into one and one.

A rough guide for the core library:

| Role | Second game earns its place when |
|---|---|
| Filler | The first has 25+ plays and the group asks for something new |
| 2-player | You play at two most weeks; a second and third are reasonable |
| Party | Your crowds differ in size or temperament |
| Co-op | The first is played out or too long for your usual night |
| Gateway | Rarely; a second gateway is usually a stretch in disguise |
| Stretch | Only once the first stretch has 10+ plays |

The plays column in your log answers most of these before you have to think about them.

## Building toward your lane in the group

Here is where the core library connects to the group. If your friends each built a starter shelf and coordinated, you already share a library across several homes. The core library is where you each go deep in one direction, which is what [claiming a lane](/articles/claim-a-lane/) means.

Your lane is the role or genre your log says you play most and your friends play least at their own houses. If you host the big nights, your lane is party and co-op. If you and your partner play every weeknight, your lane is two-player. If you are the one who reads rulebooks for fun, your lane is the stretch and beyond.

Once you know your lane, the group's [One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/) becomes a practical filter on every purchase: is this in my lane, or is this someone else's job? If a friend has claimed co-ops, you do not buy Spirit Island; you text them and ask whether they have played it. If nobody has claimed two-player and that is your busiest role, you take the lane and go deep.

A group of four, each going deep in one lane from $300 to $1,000, builds a shared library that no single collector could afford. Our [case study of two groups](/articles/two-groups-two-years/) shows what happens when one group does this and another does not.

## What the core library stage is not

A few traps that open up once the starter shelf is done and the budget loosens.

- **It is not the time to chase rankings.** A game's reputation says nothing about your table. Your log does.
- **It is not the time for campaign games, unless your group has met monthly for a year.** A campaign needs a stable group. If you have one, Gloomhaven and its kind become reasonable. If you do not, wait.
- **It is not the time to buy for the group you wish you had.** Twilight Imperium is a glorious game for six people with eight hours. If that night does not exist on your calendar, the box is a wish, not a purchase.
- **It is not the time to buy on a crowdfunding timeline.** A game that arrives in 14 months cannot be judged by a log you keep today. The [Kickstarter question](/articles/kickstarter-question/) belongs to the curator stage, when a shelf cap tells you what a new box displaces.

The core library should get heavier in exactly the places you play and no heavier anywhere else. If every role is growing at the same rate, you are collecting, not building.

## FAQ

### When should you buy a board game expansion?

Buy an expansion once the base game has roughly 15 or more plays with your group and people have started asking for variety. At that point the expansion deepens a game that has already proven itself. An expansion for a game with only a few plays raises its cost per play without creating new plays. Expansions also make strong gifts for a friend who owns the base game.

### How do you calculate cost per play for a board game?

Divide the price by the number of plays, then by the number of players, then by the hours per play. A $40 game played 20 times is $2 per play; with four players that is 50 cents per person per play. Tracking plays in a simple log is the only input you need. Over a few months the numbers show which kinds of games your group actually reaches for.

### Is it worth owning two board games of the same type?

Sometimes. Ask whether the second game creates plays that would not otherwise happen. A second filler usually does, since fillers get played often and variety keeps them fresh. A second heavy strategy game usually does not if the first one is played only a few times a year. Use your play log: a role with many plays can support a second game; a role with few cannot.

### How many board games should a collection have?

At the core library stage, roughly 10 to 20 games is typical, growing from a starter shelf of six. The number matters less than the distribution. A healthy collection grows in the roles your group plays most and stays thin everywhere else. If every role is growing at the same rate regardless of plays, the collection is drifting toward a shelf of unplayed games.
