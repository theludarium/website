A community cube is a single box of cards that one person owns and everyone has opinions about. The second half is the point. Cube Commonwealth drafted the same cube weekly in Indianapolis for about three years, and by the end the list belonged to the group in every way that mattered except legally. Members lobbied for cards, argued about cuts, and treated the change log like the week's news. That did more to hold the group together than anything we did on purpose.

This is how we handled member input without letting the cube become a committee, and what we would do differently now that we are relaunching.

## Why let members shape the cube at all?

Because a cube that nobody but the owner cares about is just the owner's deck collection with extra steps.

When a player lobbies for a card and it goes in, three things happen. They come next week to see it drafted. They watch who takes it. They defend it when it underperforms and claim it when it wins. A card becomes a small stake in the night. Multiply that by twenty members over three years and the cube stops being a product the host provides and becomes a thing the group maintains.

The founding idea is in [Cube Commonwealth: a community around one cube](/articles/cube-commonwealth/). The mechanics of taking input are below.

## How did we take input without taking orders?

The short version: everyone could propose, the room could argue, and one person decided. All three parts were explicit from the beginning.

**Everyone could propose.** A channel in the Discord, #the-cube, existed for exactly this. "Cut the three-mana walker, it's never good." "Add the new green two-drop." No forms, no voting, no process. A sentence and a reason.

**The room could argue.** And did. The best arguments happened at the table in the five-minute debrief at the end of the night. What did you draft, what felt bad, what should go. Half the changes we ever made started in that conversation.

**One person decided.** The owner. Not because the owner was always right, but because a cube needs a single point of view to stay coherent. A committee cube drifts toward whatever the loudest three members want this month. An owner with a stated philosophy can say "that card is strong, and it doesn't fit, and here's why" and have it stick.

The deal was never hidden. "This is my cube, I want your input, I make the final call." Nobody ever complained about the arrangement, because the alternative was no input at all.

## What is a change log and why does it matter so much?

The change log is the single most popular ritual we ever had, and it cost nothing.

Every time the cube changed, the owner posted what came in and what went out, with one line of reasoning per swap. Then, before pack one on the first night with the new list, the owner read it aloud at the table and took heckling.

We started doing this because of a mistake. Early on, cards were swapped quietly between weeks and players felt the cube shifting under them. One regular drafted around a card that was no longer in the box. The log fixed that, and then it became something else: a weekly argument people looked forward to.

The format, roughly:

> **Changes, [date]**
> OUT: [card]: never drafted above pick eight in six months
> IN: [card]: [member] has been asking for this since spring; let's see
> OUT: [card]: too strong for the table; three 3-0s in a row
> IN: [card]: fills the red two-drop hole from the last update

Short. Reasoned. Credited. The "credited" part is the one people cared about. Seeing your name next to a card you lobbied for is a small thing that keeps someone coming for months. The craft side of updating a list is in [how to update a cube without breaking what works](/articles/how-to-update-a-cube/); this article is about the people side.

## What kinds of member input were useful?

Not all input is equal, and over time we learned which kinds moved the list.

| Type of input | Example | How useful |
|---|---|---|
| Table data | "Blue-red has gone 0-3 four weeks running" | Very. This is what the cube is for. |
| Play-pattern complaints | "That card makes every game against it the same" | Very. Fun is the goal, and this is a fun report. |
| Hole-spotting | "There's no playable white four-drop" | Useful. Owners miss gaps. |
| Power lobbying | "This new mythic is insane, add it" | Rarely. Power is not the problem a cube has. |
| Personal favorites | "I just love this card" | Sometimes. One slot per person per year, as a treat. |
| Nostalgia cuts | "That card was in my first deck, don't cut it" | Honored more than we should admit. |

The pattern is that input about how the games feel is gold and input about which cards are strong is noise. A cube owner can read a card. A cube owner cannot sit at every table, and the table is where the cube actually happens.

## What went wrong?

A few things, each of them instructive.

**The power creep season.** For a few months the owner said yes to too much power lobbying. The cube got more exciting on paper and worse at the table, because the gap between strong and new players widened. New players bounced. We stepped it back and said so in the change log, which was its own useful moment: the owner admitting the cube had drifted built more trust than pretending it had not.

**The pet card problem.** When a member's card underperformed for months, cutting it felt like cutting the member. We left a few duds in far too long. The fix was a norm: lobbied cards get a season, then they are evaluated like anything else, and the person who lobbied gets to argue the case one more time before the cut.

**Deciding by argument volume.** The loudest three voices in #the-cube were not the most representative. The debrief at the table, where quiet players spoke up, corrected for that. If we only had the Discord, the cube would have been built for the people who type the most.

**Not writing the philosophy down.** For two years the owner's design goals lived in the owner's head. Every argument had to re-derive them. A one-paragraph pinned post ("This cube wants aggro to be viable, games to end by turn eight, and every two-color pair to have a plan") would have settled half the debates before they started.

## What does the relaunch change?

Cube Commonwealth paused when the night depended too heavily on one person's calendar, and that person had a season where the calendar won. We are relaunching as a branch of The Ludarium, and the community cube question is one we are thinking about differently.

A few changes we are making:

- **The philosophy is written down and pinned.** Day one.
- **Two or three people can propose changes to the list, not one.** The owner still decides, but a second cube curator means the list keeps moving when the owner is busy.
- **A quarterly "state of the cube" post** replaces ad hoc updates: what the archetype data says, what is coming in, what the group has asked for. More ceremony, more ownership.
- **Member slots.** One card per regular per year, no questions asked, clearly marked in the log. A small formalization of the "personal favorites" row above. <!-- EDITOR: confirm -->

Whether a cube should be owned by a group outright, with shared purchases and shared decisions, is a different question. We think it mostly fails, for the same reason shared bank accounts between friends fail. One owner, many voices, and a log everyone can read is the arrangement that lasted. ## FAQ

### What is a community cube?

A cube owned by one person and shaped by a group's input, with changes proposed by members, argued at the table, and decided by the owner.

### Should a cube group vote on card changes?

Not formally. Open proposals and a single decision-maker keep the list coherent. Votes drift toward power and toward whoever shows up to vote.

### What is a cube change log?

A short post every time the cube changes, listing each card in and out with one line of reasoning and credit to whoever suggested it.

### How do you handle a member's favorite card that is not performing?

Give it a set period, then evaluate it like any other card, and let the member make their case once more before you cut it.
