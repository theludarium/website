Deck building games start every player with the same small, weak deck of cards. On each turn you draw a hand, use it to buy better cards, and shuffle the new purchases into your deck so they show up later. The deck is both your resources and your engine, and it gets stronger as the game goes on. Dominion introduced the idea in 2008, and it has spread into card games, board games, video games, and hybrids of all three.

This article explains how deck building works, why Dominion's version was so clean, what later designers changed, and what to think about if you want to design one yourself.

## How does a deck building game work?

The loop in almost every deck builder is the same five steps:

1. Draw a hand from your deck, usually five cards.
2. Play cards for money, actions, or attacks.
3. Buy or acquire new cards from a shared supply.
4. Discard everything, including what you bought.
5. When your deck runs out, shuffle the discard pile and keep going.

Step five is where the magic lives. Because every purchase goes into the discard pile and comes back after a shuffle, the deck you have at minute forty is the sum of every decision you made in the first thirty-nine. Nothing is lost. Nothing is permanent either, since each card only shows up when the shuffle says so.

Compare that to a game like Ticket to Ride, where a card you draw is spent once and gone. In a deck builder, a card you buy is a promise to yourself about the future.

## Why was Dominion so important?

Donald X. Vaccarino's Dominion was not the first game with a changing deck, but it was the first to make deck growth the whole game. Everything else was stripped away. There is no board. There is no map. There is a supply of ten Kingdom card piles chosen from a much larger pool, plus Treasure and Victory cards, and that is the game.

Three design choices made it work.

**Victory cards are dead weight.** The cards that win you the game do nothing when drawn. Buying them early clogs your deck. Buying them late might be too late. That single tension gives the whole game its shape.

**The supply is different every time.** With ten Kingdom piles drawn from dozens, no two setups play the same. The game had enormous replay value with a single box.

**Turns are short.** You draw five, do what you can, and pass. Most of the thinking happens while others are taking their turns, so downtime stays low even with four players.

Dominion won the Spiel des Jahres in 2009 and the format exploded. Within a few years there were deck builders about space, superheroes, zombies, Cthulhu, trains, and the Roman Empire.

## What did later deck builders change?

Dominion's purity was also its limit. It can feel abstract and solitary, with players running their own engines in parallel. Designers who followed kept the core loop and attacked the weak points.

| Game | What it added | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|
| Ascension (2010) | A shared center row that refills as cards are taken | Players compete for the same cards instead of a fixed supply |
| Thunderstone (2009) | A dungeon to fight with the cards you build | Gave the engine a target beyond points |
| Star Realms (2014) | Direct attacks on an opponent's life total | Made it a duel, not a race |
| Clank! (2016) | A board to move on and a dragon that punishes noise | Tied the deck to a physical space |
| Aeon's End (2016) | Cooperative play and no shuffling at all | Players control deck order by how they discard |
| Dune: Imperium (2020) | Worker placement powered by cards | Merged two of the most popular mechanisms into one turn |

The table shows a pattern. Each step took something a different genre did well and asked what happened when a deck fed it. Aeon's End is the most radical, because removing the shuffle turned a game of probability into a game of sequencing. You know your next hand because you chose it.

Deck builders also borrowed from Dominion's cousins. The idea of thinning a deck, buying cards that remove weaker cards, became a standard skill. So did the idea of cards that trigger when another card is played, which rewards building toward a plan rather than buying whatever looks strong.

## What makes a deck builder feel good to play?

Players describe good deck builders with the same word: momentum. The first few turns are slow. Then one shuffle lands, your engine turns over, and you draw seven cards instead of five and buy the best thing on the table. That moment of acceleration is the reward the whole format is built around.

A few design elements protect it.

**A visible power curve.** Players should be able to feel their deck getting better every two or three turns. If improvement is too gradual, the game is a grind. If it is too fast, the game ends before anyone enjoys the engine they built.

**Meaningful trash.** Removing starting cards from the deck is often the strongest early move. Giving players a way to do it, and making them choose between thinning and buying, creates a real strategic fork.

**A clock players can see.** Dominion ends when the Province pile or three supply piles empty out. Everyone can count. Games that hide the end condition leave players unsure whether to keep building or start scoring, which is frustrating rather than tense.

**Interaction that does not stall the game.** Attack cards in Dominion are quick and bounded. A Militia makes you discard to three. That hurts, but it does not take five minutes to resolve. Designers who add long, chaining attack sequences usually find the fun leaks out through the downtime.

## How do you design a deck builder that is not just Dominion again?

If you are prototyping your own, here is the hard truth: the base loop is solved. Nobody needs another game where you buy cards to buy more cards. The question is what the deck is *for*.

Ask what the cards do when they are not buying things. In Clank! they move you through a dungeon. In Dune: Imperium they place workers. In Aeon's End they cast spells against a boss. The deck is a resource engine, and the engine needs something to power.

Then think about the supply. A fixed supply like Dominion's rewards planning. A rolling market like Ascension's rewards opportunism and adds luck. Each has fans. Pick the one that fits the feeling you want and accept the tradeoff.

Play count matters more than usual here. A deck builder with four players and long turns has a downtime problem, because the one thing you cannot do while waiting is improve your deck. Many of the best modern designs cap at four and shine at two or three. Our piece on [designing for the player count people have](/articles/player-count-design/) covers this in more depth.

And watch the starting deck. Ten cards, seven money and three dead points, is Dominion's answer and it still works. But a starting deck with one unique card per player, or one small weakness to fix, gives each player a reason to care from turn one.

## What are the common mistakes?

New designers of deck builders tend to make the same handful of errors.

- Too many cards do slightly different things, so no purchase feels decisive.
- Starting decks are so weak that the first three turns are boring for everyone.
- The best strategy is to buy only money, which means the interesting cards are a trap.
- Victory points are easy to buy, so the game becomes a race rather than a build.
- Attacks are strong enough that the group targets the leader and the game drags.

Most of these show up in the first few plays if you watch for them. A [structured playtest](/articles/how-to-playtest-a-board-game/) with a notebook and a willingness to cut your favorite card will catch the rest.

## Why do deck builders bring people back to the table?

Deck builders have a quality that few genres share: they are easy to teach and hard to exhaust. Draw five, buy cards, shuffle. A new player understands it in two minutes. A veteran is still finding combinations after a hundred plays. That gap between entry and ceiling is why Dominion is still in print and still on cafe shelves nearly two decades on.

They also play well across experience levels. A new player who buys sensible cards will have a good deck and a close game. That matters at a lounge table, where the goal is not to crown a champion but to give five people a reason to sit together for an hour. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### What was the first deck building game?

Dominion, designed by Donald X. Vaccarino and published in 2008, is widely credited as the first game built entirely around deck building. Earlier games had evolving decks, but Dominion made it the entire design.

### Is Magic: The Gathering a deck building game?

Not in this sense. In Magic you build a deck before the game starts. In a deck builder you build it during play, from a shared supply, and everyone starts with the same cards.

### What is the best deck building game for beginners?

Dominion remains the clearest teacher of the format. Star Realms is a cheaper, faster option for two players, and Clank! adds a board for groups who want more to look at.

### How long does a deck building game take?

Most play in 30 to 60 minutes. Games with boards or campaign elements, like Clank! or Aeon's End, run closer to 60 to 90.
