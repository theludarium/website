D&D vs Pathfinder is the question almost every new tabletop roleplayer asks within a month of getting interested. The two games look alike. Both are fantasy, both use a twenty-sided die, both have classes, levels, spells, and a Game Master who runs the world. They grew from the same root. But they have drifted into two different philosophies, and a beginner will feel the difference in the first session.

The short version: Dungeons & Dragons is easier to start, easier to find a group for, and has far more beginner material. Pathfinder Second Edition is more detailed, more consistent, and more generous with its rules, and it rewards players who like building characters as much as playing them. Most people should start with D&D. Some people should not.

## What is the actual difference between D&D and Pathfinder?

**Dungeons & Dragons** <!-- AFFILIATE --> is published by Wizards of the Coast. The current rules are the 2024 revision of fifth edition, sometimes called 5.5e by players, though the publisher just calls it D&D. Its design goal since 2014 has been accessibility: fewer numbers to track, a bounded range of bonuses, and a system called advantage and disadvantage (roll two dice, take the better or worse) that replaces most situational math. The core free rules are published online by the publisher ([D&D Beyond Basic Rules](https://www.dndbeyond.com/sources/dnd/free-rules)).

**Pathfinder** <!-- AFFILIATE --> is published by Paizo. The current version is Second Edition, revised in 2023 with the "Remaster" books Player Core and GM Core. Pathfinder began in 2009 as a continuation of D&D's third edition after Wizards moved on, and it kept that edition's love of options. Second Edition rebuilt everything around a three-action economy: on your turn you get three actions, and nearly everything, moving, attacking, casting, raising a shield, costs one or more. Its rules are free online at [Archives of Nethys](https://2e.aonprd.com/), an [official rules resource](https://www.wargamer.com/pathfinder/archives-of-nethys-new-agreement), under Paizo's open [ORC license](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathfinder_Roleplaying_Game).

Both games can tell the same stories. The difference is in how much the rules say.

## Which one is easier to learn?

D&D, for the player. The 2024 rules are built so that a new player with a pregenerated sheet can understand their turn in a few minutes: move, take one action, maybe a bonus action. Advantage and disadvantage mean you rarely add up modifiers. The number of decisions a first-level character makes in combat is small by design.

Pathfinder's three-action system is elegant once it clicks, and many players prefer it, but it asks more at the start. Attacking three times in a row gets progressively harder (the multiple attack penalty), so a new player has to learn that two attacks and a Raise Shield usually beats three swings. Conditions are more numerous and more precisely defined. Spellcasting has more moving parts. None of this is hard for someone who likes systems. All of it is friction for someone who does not.

For the Game Master the gap is smaller and arguably reversed. D&D's rules leave more to the GM's judgment, which is freeing for an experienced GM and stressful for a new one. Pathfinder's rules cover more situations explicitly, so a new GM can look things up instead of improvising. Its encounter-building math is also more reliable, which means fewer accidental party wipes.

## Which starter set is better for a first game?

Both publishers sell a boxed introduction, and both are good.

The **D&D Starter Set: Heroes of the Borderlands** was released in September 2025 at a list price around $50 ([EN World](https://www.enworld.org/posts/9706779/)). It uses the 2024 rules and takes a board-game approach: class boards, cards for species, backgrounds, and equipment, map tiles, and three adventure booklets built around a classic location, the Keep on the Borderlands. It is designed to reduce page-flipping and to let a group start playing with very little reading. <!-- EDITOR: confirm current price -->

The **Pathfinder Beginner Box** is a long-running product, revised for the Remaster, with a Heroes' Handbook, a Game Master's Guide, a full dice set, pregenerated characters, a flip-mat, and over a hundred cardboard pawns ([Paizo](https://paizo.com/pathfinder/beginnerbox)). Its first adventure is a solo tutorial you can play alone before you run anything for friends, which is a thoughtful touch for the nervous first-time GM. Paizo's store listed it at [$36 to $45](https://store.paizo.com/pathfinder-beginner-box) at the time of writing; a revised box, Secrets of the Unlit Star, lists at $49.99.

If the first session is your main concern, the D&D box gets a table going faster. If you are the person who will be running the game and you want to feel prepared, the Pathfinder box teaches more carefully.

## Which is easier to find a group for?

D&D, by a wide margin, and this matters more than most beginners expect.

D&D is the default. Most game stores run D&D Adventurers League nights. Most online looking-for-group boards are mostly D&D. Most of your friends who "used to play" played D&D. If you learn D&D you will never struggle to find a table.

Pathfinder has a loyal and organized community through Pathfinder Society, Paizo's organized play program, and in a city the size of Indianapolis you will find tables. But there are fewer of them, and in a smaller town there may be none. [Finding a D&D group in Carmel, Fishers, or Westfield](/articles/dnd-groups-carmel-fishers/) is a solved problem. Finding a Pathfinder group can take a few weeks.

The flip side: Pathfinder players tend to be experienced and welcoming to newcomers, because they know their game needs ambassadors.

## Which costs less?

Pathfinder, if you are willing to read rules on a screen. The complete rules are free and legal online, and many groups play for years without buying a book. The physical core books are priced in the same range as D&D's.

D&D's free Basic Rules cover enough to play, but the full set of classes, subclasses, and options lives in the paid Player's Handbook, and digital content on D&D Beyond is a second purchase. A D&D group typically ends up spending more on books over time, though a single Player's Handbook shared at the table is enough for most.

Either way, the real costs are dice, snacks, and a few evenings a month. Both games are cheap hobbies per hour.

## Which one holds up at higher levels?

Pathfinder. This is the complaint experienced D&D players make most often: around level 10 and beyond, D&D's combat math gets loose, some classes outpace others badly, and the GM has to work hard to build a challenging fight. Pathfinder's tighter math keeps fights meaningful through level 20, and its character progression keeps handing you interesting choices the whole way.

A beginner will not feel this for a year or more. But if you know you are the kind of player who sticks with a campaign for the long haul, it is a point in Pathfinder's favor.

## Verdict: which should a beginner start with?

**Start with D&D if:**

- You want the fastest route from "curious" to "playing".
- You need to find a group rather than bring one.
- You or your friends are coming from video games, podcasts, or a show like Stranger Things, and want the thing you have heard about.
- The person running the game is also new and wants the most tutorials, videos, and ready-made adventures available.

**Start with Pathfinder if:**

- You like building characters and want a meaningful choice at every level.
- You enjoy tactical combat where positioning and action choice matter.
- Your GM is comfortable reading rules and wants the rules to back them up.
- You want to play for free from day one without feeling like you are missing half the game.

**It does not matter much if** your group is already set and enthusiastic. The group is what makes an RPG work. A great table playing either game beats a mediocre table playing the "right" one. If you have four people who want to play on Thursdays, pick the one the most experienced person knows and start. Switching later is easier than you think, and most long-time players have done it. ## FAQ

### Is Pathfinder harder than D&D?

Yes, moderately. Pathfinder Second Edition has more rules, more conditions, and more character options. It is not harder to enjoy, but it takes longer to learn and asks more of players in combat.

### Can you use D&D adventures with Pathfinder, or the reverse?

Yes, with conversion work. The stories transfer easily; the monster stats and treasure do not. Experienced GMs do it regularly, but a beginner should stick to adventures written for their system.

### Is Pathfinder free to play?

The complete rules are legally free online through Archives of Nethys. You still need dice and someone willing to run the game. Printed books are optional.

### Which is better for a solo or two-person game?

Both have community resources for small tables. The Pathfinder Beginner Box includes a solo tutorial adventure, which makes it the easier way to try the hobby alone before finding a group.
