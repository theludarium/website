Picture a four-person group that has been meeting every other Thursday for about a year. They play at whoever's house has the cleanest table. Somewhere in that year, each of them bought [Catan](https://link.amazon/B0h8BDGR2). Four boxes. One of them gets played. The other three have been sitting in closets, shrink-wrapped, for months.

At about $40 a copy, that is roughly $120 of games that nobody opened. It could have been three more games on the table. Nobody did anything wrong. Each person saw a game they loved, had the money, and bought it. Nobody told them there was another way.

This article lays out the One Copy Rule, the first idea in our series called The Shared Shelf. You will get the rule itself, the handful of exceptions that matter, and a plan you can start this week with the group you already have.

## Why does every play group end up with duplicate games?

Buying a game is a solo act. You are standing in a store or scrolling a listing, and the question in your head is "do I want this?" Playing a game is a group act. The question at the table is "what do we want to play tonight?" Those two moments almost never talk to each other.

Most hobby advice makes it worse. Collection guides and top-ten lists are written for one person building one shelf. For most of us that is not true. We play with the same four to six people most of the time, and those people are buying games too.

Then there is the enthusiasm problem. You play [Wingspan](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07YQ641NQ?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) at a friend's place, have a great night, and the next morning you order a copy. That is the most natural purchase in the hobby, and it is also the one most likely to be a duplicate. The game you just loved is, by definition, already in your group.

So the four Catan boxes are not a story about bad judgment. They are what happens when solo buying meets group playing, with nobody coordinating.

## What does cost per play tell us about duplicate copies?

The clearest way to see the waste is cost per play. The full formula is:

**price ÷ plays ÷ players ÷ hours**

Most people stop at price ÷ plays. The "÷ players" step is the one that matters for this rule, because it shows that a single copy's value is already shared by everyone at the table.

Here is an illustration. Say that $40 copy of Catan gets played 20 times by the same four people.

- $40 ÷ 20 plays = $2 per play
- $2 ÷ 4 players = about 50 cents per player per play

Every person at that table is getting fifty-cent game nights out of one box. Now add three more copies of the same game to the same group. The group's spend goes from $40 to $160. The number of plays does not move. The four copies cannot be played at once by the same four people. Cost per player per play quadruples to about $2, and the group has gained exactly zero additional game time.

Four copies equals four times the cost for zero added plays. That sentence is the whole argument. The One Copy Rule is just what you do once you believe it.

## What is the One Copy Rule?

> **The One Copy Rule:** A play group needs one copy of a game, not one copy per person.

That is the habit. It is not a vow of poverty and it is not a rule against buying games. It is a rule about which games to buy: the ones your group does not already have.

The rule has a corollary that turns the habit into something bigger:

> **The Shared Shelf:** A play group's collection is a single library spread across several homes.

Once you accept the One Copy Rule, your group's games stop being four small collections and start being one shared library. Your copy of [Codenames](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B014Q1XX9S?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) is on the shelf. Your friend's copy of [Pandemic](https://link.amazon/B0eHP5GCg) is on the shelf. The shelf happens to be split across four apartments, but it is one shelf, and everyone in the group can play from it.

## What does the One Copy Rule actually get you?

People hear "one copy" and assume the point is saving money. It is not. The point is range.

Take the same four-person group with the same budget. Suppose each person buys five games over a couple of years, so twenty purchases total. If they buy without coordinating, overlap is almost guaranteed. Everyone owns the same handful of hits, and the group ends up with maybe eight distinct games and a dozen duplicates. If they follow the One Copy Rule, those twenty purchases are twenty distinct games.

| | Uncoordinated group | One Copy Rule group |
|---|---|---|
| Total purchases | 20 | 20 |
| Distinct games on the Shared Shelf | about 8 | 20 |
| Duplicate copies | about 12 | 0 |
| Player counts covered | Patchy | Two players through party size |
| Dollars spent on repeat games (at $40 each, illustration) | about $480 | $0 |

Same money, more than twice the library. Twenty distinct games means a two-player option for the night only two people show up, a party game when someone brings their roommate, a co-op for the night nobody wants to fight, and a heavy strategy game for the long Saturday. Eight games with twelve duplicates means the group plays Catan again.

There is a second layer to this. When your group knows the shelf is shared, each person can collect on purpose. One person takes the strategy games, one takes party games, one takes co-ops and two-player titles. We call that Claim a Lane, and it gets its own article in this series: [Claim a Lane: How Four Friends Build a 20-Game Library](/articles/claim-a-lane/). The short version is that four people buying in four different directions cover far more ground than four people buying the same thing.

## When is a second copy of a game worth it?

The rule is "one copy per play group," not "one copy per planet." There are real cases where a second copy earns its keep. The way to tell is the second-copy test:

**Does the second copy create plays that would not otherwise happen?**

If the answer is yes, buy it with a clear conscience. If the answer is no, the money belongs in a different game. Here are the cases where the answer is usually yes.

### You play in two separate groups

If you have a Thursday group in town and a family group two hours away, those are two Shared Shelves. A copy of [Ticket to Ride](https://link.amazon/B0jh8RB7f) in each place creates plays that one copy, living in one place, could not. The test passes.

### You need a home or family copy

Your group's copy of [Azul](https://link.amazon/B0bGRYHex) lives at Dana's apartment. Your kids want to play Azul on a Tuesday. A home copy produces plays the group copy never would. That is not a duplicate; that is a second library with its own players.

### The game is better with two sets

A few games are designed to grow when you combine sets. Two copies of [Dominion](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01LYLIS2U?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) let one group build a much larger card pool and run different kingdoms at the same time. A Magic Cube seats eight drafters, so a group of sixteen regulars needs a second Cube to run two pods on the same night. In these cases the second copy adds plays directly.

### The anchor that is always needed

Some groups have a game that gets pulled out every single session, often as the opener or the closer. If the owner of that game misses a night, the night is noticeably worse. A second copy of a cheap, always-played anchor can be worth it purely for availability. Run the test with a straight face, though. "We would miss it" and "we would not play" are different answers.

### Cheap fillers

A $12 copy of [Love Letter](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00AGJ4HC2?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) or [Sushi Go!](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00J57VU44?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) is cheap enough that owning your own, so it is in your bag at all times, is defensible. The dollars at stake are small and the game shows up in more places. Just do not let "fillers are cheap" become a loophole for $40 games.

## How do you start a Shared Shelf this week?

You need one conversation and a few habits. Here is the order that works.

1. **Pool your shelves.** Everyone sends a list, or a photo, of the games they own. Put it in the group chat. This takes ten minutes and it is often the first time anyone has seen the whole library.
2. **Find the duplicates and count the money.** Mark every game that appears more than once. Multiply the extra copies by a rough price. Say it out loud: "We own three spare copies of Catan, which is about $120." Nobody will argue with the number.
3. **Agree on the rule out loud.** One sentence: "From now on, we buy one copy of a game for the group, not one each." Saying it is what makes it real.
4. **Check the shelf before you buy.** Before any purchase, glance at the pooled list or ask the chat. This is the whole habit in one step.
5. **Decide who buys next.** When someone wants the group to have a new game, agree on who is buying it. If you are using Claim a Lane, the lane owner buys.
6. **Route expansions to the base owner.** If the group loves [Carcassonne](https://link.amazon/B012Rr5ed) and wants an expansion, the person who owns the base game gets it. Expansions make the best gifts for exactly this reason, which is the subject of [Gifting as Library Strategy](/articles/gifting-as-library-strategy/).

If you want a bit more structure, we built a short Group Shelf Charter template, with a pooling table and a place to record who owns what. It lives in [How to Audit Your Group's Game Shelf](/articles/audit-your-group-shelf/).

## What are the common objections to the One Copy Rule?

Two objections come up every time we explain this. Both are fair, and both have answers.

### "What if I want to play it when they are not around?"

Then you want a home copy, and that is one of the exceptions above. Run the second-copy test. If you will actually play Wingspan with your partner on weeknights, a second copy creates plays and passes. If you want a copy because owning it feels nice, that is a different purchase and you should call it what it is.

In practice this comes up less than people expect. Most of us play most of our games with the same group. And a Shared Shelf makes borrowing normal: ask the owner to bring it, or ask to keep it for a week.

### "What if the owner moves away?"

Then the game moves away with them, and the group replaces it if the group still wants it. This is not a flaw in the rule; it is the rule working. Over the life of a play group, people join and leave. Sometimes a game leaves too. Buying one replacement copy when that happens is cheaper than buying four copies up front against the possibility.

Storage, breakage, and departures all deserve a plan, and we lay one out in [The Shared Shelf Social Contract](/articles/shared-shelf-social-contract/).

## What does a Magic Cube prove about shared copies?

If you want the purest version of the One Copy Rule, look at a Magic: The Gathering Cube.

A Cube is a curated pool of cards, usually a few hundred, that one person owns and maintains. Eight players sit down, draft from that one pool, build decks, and play. At the end of the night every card goes back in the box. Nobody buys anything. One person's collection serves eight people, week after week, for years.

That is the One Copy Rule at its most extreme. One copy, eight players, unlimited plays. The owner is not giving anything up; the Cube is more fun with eight people than with one.

[Cube Commonwealth](/articles/cube-draft-indianapolis/), our Magic Cube community in Indianapolis, drafted weekly for about three years and is relaunching as part of The Ludarium. It is living evidence that one carefully curated copy can be the whole library for a whole community. We wrote a full piece on what any play group can borrow from it: [Why a Magic Cube Is the Perfect Shared Library](/articles/cube-perfect-shared-library/).

## Why the One Copy Rule is really about people

Strip the math away and the One Copy Rule is a statement about what a play group is: one group with one library, bigger than anyone's shelf because everyone contributes a different part of it.

The three unopened Catan boxes in that closet are not a tragedy. They are an invitation. Pool the shelves, count the duplicates, say the rule out loud, and the next $120 your group spends buys three games you have never played instead of a game you already own.

The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### What is the One Copy Rule for board games?

The One Copy Rule says a play group needs one copy of a game, not one copy per person. Because a game's value is already shared by everyone at the table, extra copies add cost without adding plays. Groups that follow the rule spend the same money and end up with a much larger, more varied shared library.

### Is it rude to play a friend's board game without buying your own?

No. Playing from a friend's copy is how the hobby works, and most owners would rather their games get played than sit on a shelf. The fair trade is to contribute something different to the group's library, host sometimes, and treat borrowed games with care. The Shared Shelf model makes this an explicit agreement instead of an awkward favor.

### How much money do duplicate board games waste?

It depends on the group, but it adds up fast. Catan runs about $40, so three extra copies in a four-person group is roughly $120 to $150 that bought zero additional plays. Pool your group's shelves, count every repeat copy, and multiply by a rough price. Most groups are surprised by the total.

### When should a play group own two copies of the same game?

Buy a second copy when it creates plays that would not otherwise happen: you play in two separate groups, you need a home copy for family, the game improves with two sets (like Dominion or a second Magic Cube), or the game is a cheap filler you want in your bag. Otherwise the money is better spent on a game the group does not own.
