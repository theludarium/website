MTG proxies are stand-in cards that represent a real Magic card you do not own. A proxy can be a basic land with "Black Lotus" written on it in marker, a color printout in a sleeve, or a professionally printed card that looks nearly like the original. They are not legal in sanctioned tournaments. In casual play, which is most Magic, whether they belong at the table is a question of ethics and etiquette, and this article tries to answer it plainly.

The short version: proxies for personal play are fine, proxies sold as real cards are fraud, and the gray area between is smaller than the internet makes it sound.

## What is a proxy, and how is it different from a counterfeit?

The distinction is intent and honesty.

A **proxy** is a card that both players know is not the real thing. It represents a card for the purpose of playing a game. Nobody is deceived. It might be a scribbled basic land or a high-quality print, but everyone at the table knows what it is.

A **counterfeit** is a fake card made to pass as genuine. Its purpose is to deceive someone, usually a buyer. Counterfeiting is theft from whoever ends up holding the card thinking it is real, and it damages the trust the whole secondary market depends on.

The physical card can be identical. A high-quality printed Black Lotus in a cube, announced as a proxy, is a proxy. The same card sold on an auction site as real is a counterfeit. The object does not change. The honesty does.

| | Proxy | Counterfeit |
|---|---|---|
| Everyone at the table knows | Yes | No |
| Intended to be sold as real | No | Yes |
| Harm to anyone | None, in casual play | Buyer, seller reputation, market trust |
| Legal in sanctioned events | No | No |
| Ethical in casual play | Generally yes | Never |

## Why do people use proxies?

Three reasons, and they are all reasonable.

**Cost.** The most powerful cards in Magic are decades old, printed in small quantities, and will never be reprinted because of the publisher's [reserved list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic:_The_Gathering) policy. At the time of writing (October 2026), a single [Revised Underground Sea](https://www.mtggoldfish.com/price/Revised+Edition/Underground+Sea) listed above $1,000, an [Unlimited Mox Sapphire](https://www.mtggoldfish.com/price/Unlimited+Edition/Mox+Sapphire) above $6,000, and an [Unlimited Black Lotus](https://www.mtggoldfish.com/price/Unlimited+Edition/Black+Lotus) above $20,000. A powered cube with real cards is a serious asset. A powered cube with proxies costs a weekend of printing.

**Testing.** Players who compete in constructed formats proxy cards to test a deck before buying. Nobody buys a playset of a $60 card to find out it does not fit.

**Preservation.** Some players own the real cards and proxy them anyway, keeping the originals safe while the proxies get shuffled four hundred times at cube night.

For cube specifically, proxies are what make the format's most famous version possible for ordinary people. The comparison of [pauper cube and vintage cube](/articles/pauper-cube-vs-vintage-cube/) covers why that matters.

## Are proxies ethical?

Here is where people argue, so let me lay out the strongest version of each side.

**The case against.** The publisher designs and owns these cards. Printing your own copy of their artwork and card frame, even for personal use, uses their work without paying. Players who proxy are also opting out of the market that supports game stores, which are the physical heart of the hobby. And a room full of proxies makes it easier for a counterfeit to slip through.

**The case for.** The cards in question are mostly ones the publisher has publicly said it will never reprint. There is no lost sale, because there is no sale to lose. Proxying a card that only exists on the secondary market takes money from nobody except speculators. The publisher itself [said in 2016](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/announcements/proxies-policy-and-communication-2016-01-14) that it has no desire to police playtest cards made for personal, non-commercial use, and the custom artwork and alternate-frame proxy scene is a creative community in its own right. As for game stores, cube players tend to be the regulars who buy sleeves, snacks, and the occasional booster box at the same store every week.

My own view is that the second case is stronger for reserved-list cards and casual play, and the first case is stronger when someone proxies a current-set card to avoid buying it from the store they play at. Proxying a Mox hurts nobody. Proxying the new mythic the store has on its shelf is a different thing, even if the rules treat them the same.

## Where are proxies welcome, and where are they not?

Etiquette varies by setting. Read the room, and when in doubt, ask.

- **Your own cube at your own table.** Entirely up to you. Many of the best-known cubes are fully proxied.
- **Someone else's cube.** The owner's rules. If they ask you not to add proxies, do not.
- **Casual Commander at a game store.** Store policy. Many allow proxies in casual play, some do not. Ask the counter.
- **Commander with strangers.** Announce proxies before the game. Most people are fine. Some will not play against a proxied deck that is far more powerful than theirs, and that is a fair objection about power level, not proxies.
- **Sanctioned tournaments.** Never. This is not a gray area.
- **Any sale or trade.** Never, unless explicitly described as a proxy and priced accordingly.

The common thread: proxies are fine wherever everyone knows and nobody minds. The moment someone is deceived or feels ambushed, you have crossed a line, and it is the same line whether the card is a real Lotus or a printed one.

## How do you make proxies that play well?

If you are going to proxy a cube, do it in a way that respects the table.

1. **Make them legible.** A proxy should have the full card text. "You know what this does" is not a card.
2. **Use the same sleeves as the rest of the cube.** A proxy should be indistinguishable from the back. If it is thinner or thicker, back it with a basic land.
3. **Mark them.** A small symbol in the corner or a different border means no one ever mistakes them for the real thing, which protects you from any accusation and keeps them out of the secondary market by accident.
4. **Match the power.** Do not proxy only the broken cards into an otherwise budget deck without warning. The complaint will not be about proxies. It will be about the mismatch.
5. **Do not sell them as cards.** Print services exist; use them for yourself and your group.

Many cube owners commission custom proxy art for their most-loved cards, which turns the stand-in into something the group treasures. A cube with twenty hand-drawn proxies has more character than most binders of real rares.

## Does proxying make the game worse?

Some players worry that if everyone proxies, the cards stop meaning anything. I understand the feeling. There is something about owning a real card from 1994 that a printout does not replicate.

But the thing that makes a cube night good is not the cardboard. It is eight people, a long table, and an argument about a pick from pack two. If you have never had a cube night, [what a Magic cube is](/articles/what-is-a-magic-cube/) explains why the format works. A proxied cube delivers all of that for the cost of printing. The group that could never afford a powered cube can have one, and the night is the same night.

Collecting and playing are different hobbies that happen to share a card. Proxies let people who only want to play do that without pretending to be collectors.

## FAQ

### Are proxies legal in Magic: The Gathering?

Proxies are not legal in sanctioned tournament play. In casual play, including most Commander and cube games, they are allowed wherever the group or store permits them.

### What is the difference between a proxy and a counterfeit?

A proxy is openly a stand-in that everyone at the table knows is not real. A counterfeit is a fake made to deceive someone, usually a buyer, into thinking it is genuine.

### Is it okay to proxy cards in a cube?

Yes, if the cube owner allows it. Many of the most popular vintage cubes are built entirely or partly from proxies.

### Should you tell opponents you are using proxies?

Yes, always, before the game starts. Honesty is the entire difference between a proxy and a counterfeit.
