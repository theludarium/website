Aggro cube decks lose for the same reasons in almost every cube: there are not enough one-drops to curve out, the midrange and control decks have too many cheap blockers and lifegain, and the burn is too slow to close. The designer did not decide to kill aggro. It starved while nobody was counting.

That matters because a cube without working aggro is a cube where every game is decided on turn six or later, every deck is a pile of the best cards, and the drafter who takes the risk of beatdown gets punished for it. This article is about what goes wrong and the specific counts, cuts, and tests that fix it. For the lanes in general, see [cube archetypes explained](/articles/cube-archetypes-explained/).

## Why does aggro fail in cube when it works in retail sets?

A retail draft set is balanced for aggro by professionals. Commons include plenty of two-power two-drops, the removal is costed so a 2/1 can get in twice before it dies, and the bombs are rare. In a cube, every card is a "rare" in the sense that someone picked it for being above the curve. The whole environment is bombs.

That changes the math for the attacker in three ways:

**The blockers are better.** In a retail set, a turn-two 2/2 attacks into an empty board or a 1/1. In a cube, it attacks into a Wall of Omens, a Kitchen Finks, or a Tarmogoyf. Two power stops mattering fast.

**The answers are cheaper.** Cube removal is Swords to Plowshares, Lightning Bolt, Fatal Push. A one-drop that costs the attacker a card and the defender one mana is a losing trade at scale.

**The late game is unfair.** If the control deck survives to turn five, it plays a planeswalker or a Titan and the game is over. In retail limited, surviving to turn five just means you are even.

So aggro in cube cannot be "play some cheap creatures." It has to be a deliberately supported strategy with enough redundancy that a drafter can reliably assemble a curve of 16 or more creatures costing two or less, plus reach.

## How many one-drops does a cube need?

More than you think. In a 360-card cube with eight drafters, two of them might end up aggressive, and both need a curve. The numbers below are a floor, not a goal.

| Slot | White | Red | Black | Green |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| One-drop creatures | 8 to 10 | 7 to 9 | 4 to 6 | 3 to 5 |
| Two-drop creatures | 10 to 12 | 8 to 10 | 6 to 8 | 6 to 8 |
| Three-drops that attack | 6 to 8 | 5 to 7 | 4 to 6 | 4 to 6 |
| Burn or reach | 0 | 8 to 10 | 2 to 4 | 0 |

White and red carry aggro. Black supports it as a partner (Rakdos, Orzhov) and green can support a stompy version, but if you only fix two colors, fix those two.

Quality within the slot matters as much as the count. The best cube one-drops do something beyond attacking for two:

- **Grow or scale:** Figure of Destiny, Kytheon, Hero of Akros, Student of Warfare, Monastery Swiftspear with prowess.
- **Replace themselves or generate value:** Thraben Inspector, Esper Sentinel, Champion of the Parish in a humans-heavy list.
- **Punish blocking:** Goblin Guide and Zurgo Bellstriker with haste, Bomat Courier, Mother of Runes to blank a blocker.
- **Carry equipment:** any evasive or first-striking one-drop paired with Bonesplitter or a sword becomes a real threat.

A one-drop that is a vanilla 2/1 is fine in pauper and nearly unplayable in a powered cube. Match the quality to your band.

## What should you cut to give aggro room?

Supporting aggro is half adding and half removing. The cards that quietly kill beatdown are often good cards that nobody thinks of as anti-aggro.

**Incidental lifegain on value creatures.** Kitchen Finks, Thragtusk, Scavenging Ooze, Uro. Each one is a great card. Together they mean the midrange deck gains 10 to 15 life in a normal game, which is the whole margin an aggro deck plays for. Keep a few. Count them.

**Cheap sweepers.** Pyroclasm and Anger of the Gods cost two or three mana and kill four creatures. One in a cube is a reasonable tax on going wide. Four means a Boros drafter is playing into a coin flip every game.

**Walls and high-toughness two-drops.** Wall of Omens, Wall of Blossoms, Courser of Kruphix. Again, good cards. Just know that each one is a one-drop's worst day.

**Slow burn.** If your red section's reach is Fireball and Rolling Thunder, aggro has no way to close through a stabilized board. Trade some of that for Lightning Bolt, Chain Lightning, Skewer the Critics, Fireblast, and creature-based reach like Hellrider or Goblin Rabblemaster.

The principle: for every answer you add that is best against aggro, ask whether you have added a threat that is best for it.

## Which aggro archetypes should a cube actually support?

Pick two or three and support them fully rather than five halfway.

**White weenie (mono-white or Boros).** The classic. Cheap creatures, anthems like Glorious Anthem and Intangible Virtue, a few pieces of equipment, and Armageddon if your group allows it. Needs the highest one-drop count of any deck. Rewards drafters who take creatures over removal early, which is a real skill to teach.

**Red deck wins (mono-red or Rakdos).** Haste creatures and burn. Sulfuric Vortex and Eidolon of the Great Revel punish the slow deck's lifegain and cantrips. The easiest aggro deck to draft because burn is also removal, so the picks are never wasted.

**Rakdos sacrifice.** Half aggro, half engine. Carrion Feeder, Bloodghast, Gravecrawler attack; Viscera Seer and Goblin Bombardment turn chump attacks into damage; Mayhem Devil and Zulaport Cutthroat convert the engine into reach. Harder to support but the games are among the best in cube.

**Green stompy (Gruul).** Mana dorks into Rancor and 4-power three-drops. More midrange than aggro, but it pressures control on the same clock.

A cube that supports white weenie and red deck wins properly, with Rakdos sacrifice as a bridge between them, has enough aggro for eight players. Two aggro drafters a night is a healthy table.

## How do you test whether aggro is winning its share?

Track it. After every draft, note the archetype of each deck and its record. Over ten drafts, aggressive decks should win somewhere around 40 to 50 percent of their matches. If they are below 35 percent, something above is broken. If they are above 60 percent, you have overcorrected and the slow decks need better blockers back.

Two quicker checks:

1. **The last-pick test.** Look at the last five cards in each pack at the end of a draft. If they are consistently one-drops, drafters have learned that aggro does not work and are ignoring it. Fixing the deck will take several weeks after fixing the list, because trust comes back slowly.
2. **The goldfish test.** Build the best possible white weenie deck from your list on paper. Play it against no opponent. If it cannot reliably deal 20 by turn five, it cannot beat a real deck that interacts.

## FAQ

### How many one-drops should an aggro cube deck have?

A working white weenie or red aggro deck in cube wants 6 to 8 one-drops and 16 or more creatures at two mana or less, in a 40-card deck with 16 lands.

### Why is aggro considered the hardest archetype to support in cube?

Because it needs more redundancy than any other deck. A control deck can swap one removal spell for another. An aggro deck with four one-drops instead of eight is a different and worse deck.

### Should a cube include Armageddon?

It is a group decision. Armageddon makes white weenie a real deck against planeswalker control, but some players find losing to it miserable. Many cubes run it; many cut it. Decide on purpose.

### Is lifegain bad for cube?

No. Too much lifegain concentrated on value creatures is. Count how much incidental life your midrange decks gain and keep it in check.

## Keep the pressure on

A cube where aggro works is a cube where every drafter has to respect the clock, where removal has a purpose, and where the player who takes a risk on turn one can be rewarded. It takes counting and it takes trimming cards you like. Do it anyway. The game is the reason. The people are the point.
