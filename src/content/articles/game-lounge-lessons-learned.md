Most lists of starting-a-board-game-cafe mistakes are written after the cafe closes. We would rather write ours while there is still time to be wrong about the fixes too. The Ludarium has not signed a lease or opened a door in Indianapolis, and we have already changed our minds about seven things that felt obvious a year ago. Here they are, with the reasoning we had at the time, because the reasoning is where the lesson is.

## Mistake 1: We thought the room came first

**What we believed.** The lounge is the business. Find the space, build it out, open, and the community forms inside it.

**Why it was wrong.** A room with no community is an expensive empty room, and the first members who join an empty room leave fast, because the thing they paid for, other people, is not there. We worked through how many members a room needs in our membership math article, and the number does not appear on opening day by magic.

**What we changed.** Content, newsletter, public events, and a corporate tabletop service come first. The room comes when there are enough people asking for it that we are sizing a space to a list, not hoping a list shows up. We wrote the whole argument in [why corporate game nights come before the space](/articles/corporate-game-nights-before-a-space/).

## Mistake 2: We planned a small retail shelf

**What we believed.** Members will want to buy the game they just loved. A curated shelf of twenty titles is a service and a little margin.

**Why it was wrong.** A retail shelf is a retail business. It needs inventory, a distributor account, pricing decisions, and square footage, and it quietly starts making choices about the room. Worse, it puts us in competition with the local game stores we want sending people our way.

**What we changed.** No retail. We recommend games, teach them, and point members to Indianapolis stores to buy them. Affiliate links online, a list of local shops on the wall, and nothing in a shrink wrap in the building. The full case is in [why we won't sell games](/articles/why-game-lounges-dont-sell-games/).

## Mistake 3: We treated quiet space as a luxury

**What we believed.** One big open room with a good vibe. Zones are something to add later if the budget allows.

**Why it was wrong.** We ran sessions in rooms with no acoustic separation and watched two tables fail to hear themselves. Long games and real conversation do not survive a party game at the next table. The campaign group, the people who show up every week for two years, is the group that needs quiet most, and that group is the business.

**What we changed.** Two acoustic zones moved from nice-to-have to must-have on the space checklist. A space that cannot be divided is a space we will not take.

## Mistake 4: We were designing for hobbyists

**What we believed.** Our first members will be the people who already love games, so the library should be deep and the programming should assume experience.

**Why it was wrong.** Hobbyists already have places to play and shelves at home. The much larger group is adults who played in college, stopped, and miss the table without knowing where to find one. They walk in nervous, and a wall of 500 heavy games with no host tells them they are in the wrong place.

**What we changed.** The opening library is about 100 games, a third of them teachable in under five minutes, and every shift has a host whose job is to know names and recommend the first game. Hobbyists are welcome and will find plenty on the shelf. But the newcomer is the design target. The reasoning is in [how we're building the library](/articles/building-a-game-lounge-library/).

## Mistake 5: We underrated hospitality

**What we believed.** This is a games business. Get the games and the tables right and people will forgive a lot.

**Why it was wrong.** Nobody forgives a dirty bathroom, bad coffee, or a host who does not look up. A game lounge is a hospitality business with a membership layer. The gym and coworking comparisons we lean on are useful, but gyms close for the same reasons restaurants do: the room stopped feeling cared for.

**What we changed.** We now plan to hire for hospitality first and game knowledge second, and teach the games to the people who are already good with people. It is easier to teach Azul to a great host than to teach warmth to a rules expert. <!-- AFFILIATE -->

## Mistake 6: We wanted the beautiful big space

**What we believed.** Go big enough on day one that we never have to move. Exposed brick, high ceilings, 6,000 square feet, room for everything.

**Why it was wrong.** A half-empty room is a bad experience and a full rent bill. The lesson of the coworking collapse was leases, not the idea: long, expensive commitments against short, flexible memberships. We wrote a whole article about what coworking got right and what it got wrong, and then nearly ignored it ourselves.

**What we changed.** Size the first room to the membership we can realistically have in a year, with an option to expand. Smaller and full beats larger and echoing. We wrote the full checklist in our article on choosing a first space.

## Mistake 7: We used "community" when we meant something smaller

**What we believed.** The mission is building a community of players in Indianapolis.

**Why it was wrong.** Community is the right idea at the wrong scale. It describes the hundred people and tells you nothing about what to do on a Tuesday night. Every venue says it builds community. The word had stopped meaning anything to us.

**What we changed.** We named the actual thing: togethering, people coming together in person to share life, with the game as the medium. It is specific enough to test a decision against. Does this event produce togethering or just attendance? That question has already killed two ideas we were fond of.

## What have we not fixed yet?

Plenty. We still do not have a price, a space, or a date. We have not tested whether a few hundred people in Indianapolis will pay for this, which is the only question that matters, and we will not know until we offer a founding tier and count. <!-- EDITOR: add detail if founding tier timing is set -->

## FAQ

### What is the most common mistake when starting a board game cafe?

Signing a lease before there is a community to fill the room. Rent is due from day one, and members take months to build. Starting with events and content lets the community form before the fixed costs start.

### Should a board game cafe sell games?

We think not, if the business is membership. Retail changes the room's priorities and competes with the local stores that should be allies. Recommend games and send people to buy them locally.

### How big should a first game cafe be?

Small enough to feel full within a year. A half-empty room costs rent and tells members they chose wrong. Expand when you outgrow it.

### What matters more in a game lounge, the games or the hosts?

The hosts. A modest library with a warm, fast teacher beats a huge library with nobody to teach it, every night of the week.
