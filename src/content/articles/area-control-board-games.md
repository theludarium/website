Area control board games are about presence on a map. You place pieces in regions, and whoever has the most in a region when it scores gets the reward. The genre includes [Risk](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01HEQHKNM?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), where presence means armies and the loser is removed from the board, and [El Grande](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00005UN1N?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), where presence means wooden cubes and nobody is ever eliminated. Both are area control. Only one of them ends friendships.

This article looks at why conflict on a map so often turns sour, how the best area control designs keep it enjoyable, and what to carry into your own prototype.

## What is the difference between area control and area majority?

Players use the terms loosely, but designers draw a line.

**Area control** usually means you own a region outright. Risk is the classic case. One army occupies a territory, and you take it by removing the previous owner.

**Area majority** means several players can share a region, and the one with the most pieces scores the most, with second place often getting something too. El Grande, [Blood Rage](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B013TK5DFO?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), and [Inis](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B089Q26FL2?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) all work this way.

The distinction matters because majority systems soften conflict by design. Losing a region in Risk means losing everything there. Losing a majority in El Grande means getting second-place points instead of first. One feels like a defeat. The other feels like a negotiation that did not go your way.

## Why does Risk feel bad to lose?

Risk is a good teacher because its flaws are so visible. Three features make it painful.

**Elimination.** A player knocked out early sits and watches for an hour. In a social setting that is the worst possible outcome.

**Snowballing.** More territories mean more armies, which mean more territories. The leader pulls away and the trailing players can rarely catch up without a coalition.

**Grudge targeting.** Because attacks are direct and visible, the decision about whom to attack becomes personal. The player who got attacked last turn retaliates, and the game becomes about history instead of position.

None of this is unique to Risk. Any area control game that allows elimination, rewards the leader, and makes attacks personal will produce the same evenings. Our article on [catch-up mechanics](/articles/catch-up-mechanics-board-games/) covers the snowball problem in more detail.

## How does El Grande make conflict pleasant?

El Grande, from 1995, is still the design that most area majority games are measured against, and its solutions are worth listing.

Players place caballeros, small cubes, into the regions of medieval Spain. At three scoring rounds, each region pays out points for first, second, and third place. Nobody is removed. Cubes are only moved, never destroyed, and only by specific action cards.

That gives the game four properties.

1. Every player stays in the game until the end.
2. Being second is worth something, so you can share a region profitably.
3. Attacks are indirect. You outnumber someone or move their cubes; you do not roll dice at them.
4. The map changes. The King piece blocks a region from being altered, and the Castillo tower hides cubes until scoring.

The result is a game with constant tension and almost no bitterness. You can be beaten soundly and still enjoy the evening, because the game never asked anyone to destroy you.

## What tools do designers use to keep area control friendly?

Modern designs have built a toolkit. Not every game uses every tool, but most use several.

| Tool | Example | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Majority scoring with second place | El Grande, Blood Rage | Makes sharing a region viable |
| No elimination | Almost every modern design | Keeps everyone at the table |
| Indirect conflict | [Small World](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0024H7OF6?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Kemet](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B096L3HRFQ?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) | Removes dice-rolling grudges |
| Deterministic combat | Kemet, Inis | Fights resolve by card or number, no luck |
| Rewards for losing | Blood Rage | Dying in battle can score points |
| Regional reset | Small World's decline | Lets a losing position become a fresh start |
| Multiple paths to points | [Scythe](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01IPUGYK6?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Root](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07F454YF3?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) | Lets a player avoid conflict entirely |

Blood Rage deserves a closer look because it is a Viking combat game that still avoids bitterness. When your warriors die, they go to Valhalla and can earn you glory. Losing a battle on purpose is a strategy. A fight becomes a transaction with two winners rather than a humiliation.

Small World pulls a different trick. When your race is overstretched, you put it into decline and pick a new one. A losing position does not get worse forever. It gets replaced.

## How do you handle kingmaking?

Kingmaking is the moment a player who cannot win decides who does. It is the bane of any game with direct conflict among three or more players, and area control is its natural habitat.

You cannot eliminate kingmaking entirely in a multiplayer conflict game. You can make it rarer.

- Hide scores or part of them, so players are less certain who is ahead.
- Give the trailing player something to pursue that benefits them, such as end-game bonuses they can still reach.
- Make the final round's actions limited enough that a single player cannot swing it alone.
- Score in several rounds, so a late grudge move affects one third of the points rather than all of them.

El Grande's three scoring rounds do much of this work. Scythe does it by ending the game suddenly when one player reaches six achievements, which rewards the player who was ahead rather than whoever was most recently attacked.

## What about asymmetry?

Root changed the conversation about area control by giving each faction a different rulebook. The Marquise builds, the Eyrie follows a rigid programme, the Woodland Alliance spreads sympathy, the Vagabond wanders alone. Each faction scores points differently, which means conflict is not symmetrical either. Attacking the Alliance can make them stronger. Attacking the Vagabond is barely worth it.

Asymmetry makes conflict interesting because it is no longer obvious whom to hit. But it also multiplies the balance work. Every faction has to be fun to play and fair to play against, and the designers of Root spent years tuning it. For a first prototype, keep the factions identical and earn asymmetry later.

## How should a designer approach their first area control game?

Start with the map. Count the regions, decide how many players can usefully share one, and decide how regions score. A map with too few regions forces everyone into the same fights. Too many and players spread out and never meet.

Then decide the conflict rule. Options, roughly from gentle to harsh:

1. Pure majority, no removal. Pieces are only ever added. (Simplest, and often enough.)
2. Majority with displacement. Pieces can be moved by the opponent.
3. Deterministic combat. Pieces are removed by a known comparison, no luck.
4. Dice combat. Pieces are removed by rolling.

Each step down adds drama and subtracts goodwill. Most modern designs sit at level one or two. Go further only if the drama is the point and you have built in rewards for losing.

Finally, test it with the group you actually want to play it. A map that sings with four might collapse with three, since two players can gang up on the third with no counterweight. ## Why does any of this matter beyond the design?

Area control games are, at bottom, games about negotiating space with other people. That is a skill most adults practice rarely and poorly. A well-designed one lets a group argue, bluff, compromise, and betray within rules that keep it safe, and then shake hands at the end.

A badly designed one teaches people that games are a place to get hurt, and they stop coming to game night. The design decisions above are not just about balance. They are about whether the evening works. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### What is the best area control board game for beginners?

Small World is forgiving and funny, with no elimination and a built-in reset. El Grande is a step up in depth and still easy to teach.

### Is Risk an area control game?

Yes, and it is the one most people learn first. Its elimination and dice combat make it harsher than most modern designs in the genre.

### What is kingmaking in board games?

Kingmaking happens when a player who cannot win decides the outcome between others through their final moves. Hidden scores and multiple scoring rounds reduce it.

### How is area majority different from area control?

In area majority, several players share a region and score by rank. In strict area control, one player owns the region. Majority systems tend to produce less bitter games.
