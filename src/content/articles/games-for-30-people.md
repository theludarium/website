The best games for large groups of 30, 50, or 100 people are not bigger board games. They are formats: one host at the front, the crowd split into teams of six to eight, and a round structure that gives every table something to argue about at once. Team trivia, team betting on number guesses, a projected word grid, and pass-the-paper drawing games all work this way. Pick one or two, give them a host and a clock, and a room of strangers turns into a room of teams.

**The quick answer:** for 30 to 50 adults, run team trivia or a Wits & Wagers style number round, then a projected Codenames match, then Fishbowl at the tables. For 100, stick to formats the host can run from a microphone: trivia, Human Bingo as people arrive, and a scavenger hunt. Plan tables of six to eight, one answer sheet per table, and about 15 minutes per round.

This guide is for office parties, family reunions, church and community events, wedding receptions, and adult classrooms. If your crowd is 8 to 20 people around a living room, our guide to [party games for large groups](/articles/party-games-for-large-groups/) is the better read. This one is about the room where you need a microphone.

## What changes when a group gets past 30 people?

At twelve people, a party game can run itself. At thirty, three things break.

**Nobody can hear the clue-giver.** A room of thirty talking at once is loud. Any game where one person speaks and everyone else listens needs a microphone or a projector.

**Turns stop working.** If each player takes a turn, a thirty-person game is mostly waiting. Every format below has whole teams acting at the same time.

**Someone has to be in charge.** Large groups do not self-organize. They need one host who explains the rules once, keeps time, reads answers, and announces scores. Without that person, the energy leaks to the snack table in ten minutes.

So the recipe for games for big groups of adults is always the same: teams at tables, a host at the front, and short rounds with a scoreboard.

### Team trivia

The most reliable game for 30 people, 50 people, or a few hundred. Teams of four to eight sit at tables, the host reads questions into a microphone, and each table writes one answer per question on a shared sheet. Collect the sheets after each round, score them while the next round runs, and read the standings out loud.

Trivia scales because one voice serves the whole room and every team plays every question. Mix categories so no single expert carries a table, and include at least one round about the event itself: the company, the couple, the family, the church. That round is where a reunion or a work party stops being generic. Our guide on [how to host trivia night](/articles/how-to-host-trivia-night/) covers question writing, difficulty, and scoring in detail.

### A Wits & Wagers style number round

[Wits & Wagers](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B083LH3779?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) is a party game by Dominic Crapuchettes and Nate Heasley, first published by North Star Games in 2005. Every question has a number for an answer ("How many keys are on a standard piano?"). Everyone writes a guess, the guesses go up in order, and then players bet on which guess is closest without going over. The box plays 3 to 7 players or teams, so a bigger group can play in small teams. The later Wits & Wagers Party edition is listed for 4 to 18 players.

For 30 to 100 people, you do not need the box. Borrow the idea. Each table writes one number guess on a card or small whiteboard. The host collects or reads them, lines them up from low to high on a screen, and then each table bets on the guess it thinks is closest. Nobody needs to know the answer: a table that guessed badly can still win by betting on the smartest table in the room.

### Codenames on a projector

[Codenames](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B014Q1XX9S?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), designed by Vlaada Chvátil and published by Czech Games Edition in 2015, won the Spiel des Jahres in 2016. Two teams race to find their secret agents in a five-by-five grid of words, guided by one-word clues from a spymaster who can see which words belong to which team.

The box says 2 to 8 or more players, and that "or more" is the point. Put the grid on a projector and the whole room can play. Split into two big teams (or run two grids side by side for four teams), seat each spymaster where they can see a private key, and give each team a captain who announces the final guess into the mic. Czech Games Edition also runs a free official browser version at codenames.game, which makes projecting the grid easy. Our [Codenames team building](/articles/codenames-team-building/) piece explains why it works so well with coworkers, and the [Codenames course](/academy/codenames/) has a lesson on [how to teach Codenames](/academy/codenames/how-to-teach-codenames/) in about two minutes.

### Human Bingo

Hand everyone a bingo card where each square is a fact about a person: "has been to three continents," "plays an instrument," "was born in another state." Guests mingle, find someone who matches a square, and get that person's name written in it. First to finish a row shouts bingo.

This is the best opener for any group that does not know each other, because it forces conversations without putting anyone on stage. Write the squares for your crowd. At a wedding, use squares like "knew the couple before they met each other." At a reunion, "can name all of Grandma's siblings." Keep squares positive and easy to answer, and skip anything about health, money, or family status.

### Two Truths and a Lie, team version

The classic version, where one person stands and shares three facts, is slow at thirty people and uncomfortable for some. The team version fixes both. Each table writes three statements about its members, two true and one false. The host reads each table's set aloud, and every other table writes down which one it thinks is the lie. Points for catching a lie, points for fooling the room.

At work, give the round a safer frame: statements about hobbies, travel, or first jobs, never anything personal. Our [team building icebreakers](/articles/team-building-icebreakers/) guide has a table-sized version and several other quick warmups.

### Werewolf in rounds

Werewolf is a hidden-role game: a few players are secretly werewolves, and the village tries to vote them out before it is too late. Classic Werewolf eliminates players, which is a problem at an event. The fix is short rounds at several tables. Seat 10 to 15 people per table, give each table its own moderator, and play quick games of about 15 minutes. Eliminated players become the table's audience, then everyone is back in for the next round.

If you have a box of [One Night Ultimate Werewolf](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00HS7GG5G?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), it plays 3 to 10 in about ten minutes, with a free app as narrator and no elimination, so a few boxes can run several tables at once.

### Scavenger hunt

The best format for 100 people or more, and for groups that want to move. Teams of four to eight get the same list of items to find or photos to take, with points for each. A good list mixes easy wins ("a photo of your whole team in one frame") with harder ones ("find someone outside your team born in the same month as your captain").

Set a firm return time, have teams text photos to one judge so scoring is quick, and name the boundaries clearly.

### Wavelength in teams

[Wavelength](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07T446163?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), designed by Alex Hague, Justin Vickers, and Wolfgang Warsch, is listed for 2 to 12 players. A clue-giver sees a hidden target on a spectrum, like "cold to hot" or "underrated to overrated," and gives a clue that sits at that point. The team argues over where to set the dial.

For a big room, put the spectrum on a projector. One volunteer gives a clue into the mic, and every table marks where it thinks the target is on a printed line from 1 to 10. Closest tables score. The arguing happens at every table at once, which is exactly what you want.

### Telestrations Party Pack

[Telestrations](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0F699VTB2?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) is telephone with drawings: write a word, pass the sketchbook, the next person draws it, the next guesses the drawing, and so on. The 12 Player Party Pack plays 4 to 12 and includes twelve sketchbooks and markers.

One box will not cover thirty people, but three will, one per table of ten to twelve. Run all tables at once and have each table nominate its funniest sketchbook for the host to read out. If you only have one box, make it a table people rotate through during a free-play block instead of a whole-room game.

### Just One, team version

[Just One](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07GJ7NDZQ?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) is a cooperative word game for 3 to 7 players, designed by Ludovic Roudy and Bruno Sautter and published by Repos Production. It won the Spiel des Jahres in 2019. One player guesses a mystery word while the others each write a one-word clue. Any matching clues are cancelled before the guesser sees them.

For a crowd, run it table by table with five to seven people each, using paper or small whiteboards. Or flip it for a whole-room version: the host projects a mystery word for everyone except one guesser per table, and tables race to get their guesser to the word.

### Fishbowl (Salad Bowl)

Everyone writes three names, things, or phrases on slips of paper, and the slips go in a bowl. Two teams take turns with a timer. Round one, describe the slip with any words. Round two, one word only. Round three, act it out. The same slips come back every round, so jokes build through the game.

Fishbowl needs only paper and a bowl, which makes it the cheapest game for 30 people or more. Run one bowl per table of 10 to 16, split each table into two teams, and let the host call "start" and "stop" for the whole room so every table plays in sync.

## How do you run games for 50 people or more?

Picking games is the easy part. Running them for 50 people takes a plan.

### Make teams before people sit down

Teams of six to eight are the sweet spot: big enough that nobody is on the spot, small enough that everyone talks. Assign tables with numbered cards at the door, or let people sit where they like and then move a few to balance the room. At a work party, mix departments on purpose. At a reunion, mix branches of the family.

### Appoint one host and one helper per 25 people

The host runs the front: rules, questions, timing, scores. Helpers walk the room, hand out sheets, collect answers, and score. For 100 people, that is one host and four helpers. Brief them on the run of show before guests arrive.

### Teach once, then demonstrate

Explain the rules in under two minutes, then play one practice question or round that does not count. Large groups learn by watching, not listening.

### Keep score in public

A scoreboard on a screen or a big sheet of paper keeps every table invested. Read the standings after each round, and save a little drama for the final reveal. ## What equipment do you need for large group games?

Most of these formats need little more than paper. The short list:

- **A microphone and speaker.** The single most important item past 30 people. A cheap wireless mic beats shouting every time.
- **A projector or large TV.** For Codenames, Wavelength, the trivia scoreboard, and Wits & Wagers style guesses.
- **Answer sheets.** One per table per round, numbered and pre-printed with the table number.
- **Pens, lots of them.** Bring twice as many as you think. They vanish.
- **Small whiteboards or cardstock.** For number guesses and holding up answers.
- **A timer the room can see or hear.** A phone on the mic works.
- **Prizes, optional.** Small and silly beats expensive. Bragging rights do most of the work.

## How long should games last at a big event?

Plan each game segment for 15 to 20 minutes, and keep the whole program under two hours, break included. Big groups lose focus faster than small ones, and the transitions (handing out sheets, moving tables) take longer than you expect, so count them inside each segment's time.

### A sample run of show for 30 to 50 people

| Time | Segment | Format |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | Arrival and mingling | Human Bingo |
| 0:15 | Welcome, teams find tables | Host explains the night |
| 0:20 | Round 1 | Team trivia, 8 questions |
| 0:35 | Round 2 | Wits & Wagers style number round |
| 0:50 | Break | Food, drinks, scores posted |
| 1:05 | Round 3 | Codenames on the projector, two big teams |
| 1:25 | Round 4 | Fishbowl at tables, or the event-themed trivia round |
| 1:45 | Final scores and prizes | Host |

For 100 people, cut it to three segments: Human Bingo at arrival, two rounds of trivia, and a final round about the event itself. For a wedding reception, one 20-minute trivia round about the couple between dinner and dancing is usually plenty.

## Which formats fit which event?

**Office party games for large groups.** Team trivia, Codenames on the projector, and Wits & Wagers style rounds. Keep teams mixed across departments, keep content work-safe, and make it opt-in. Our [office holiday party games](/articles/office-holiday-party-games/) guide covers what to avoid at a work party.

**Family reunions.** Human Bingo with family facts, a trivia round about family history, and a scavenger hunt that mixes cousins of every age.

**Church and community events.** Trivia, Fishbowl, and Just One teams. All are clean by default and easy to run in a fellowship hall. Our list of [board games for church groups](/articles/board-games-for-church-groups/) has more for smaller groups after the main event.

**Weddings.** One short trivia round about the couple, plus Human Bingo on the tables during dinner. Keep it light and short; the dance floor is the main event.

**Adult classrooms and workshops.** Two Truths and a Lie in teams on day one, then Codenames on a projector with course vocabulary as the words. It is a review session that feels like a game.

## Planning a game event for work?

If the event is for your team or company and the goal is more than fun, games can do real work: they show how people communicate, decide, and handle pressure. ## FAQ

### What are good games for 30 people?

Team trivia, a Wits & Wagers style number round, and Codenames on a projector are the safest picks. All three split 30 people into teams of six to eight and let every team play at once with one host at a microphone.

### What group games work for 50 people?

Use formats a single host can run for the whole room: team trivia, Human Bingo, Wavelength on a projector, and Fishbowl with one bowl per table. Add one helper for every 25 guests to hand out and collect answer sheets.

### What are the best party games for 20 people?

At 20, you can still play boxed games in two or three teams. Codenames, Wavelength, and Telestrations with the 12 Player Party Pack all work, and Fishbowl needs no box at all. Our guide to [party games for large groups](/articles/party-games-for-large-groups/) covers this size in depth.

### What are good office party games for large groups?

Team trivia with a round about the company, Codenames on a projector, and number-guessing rounds. Keep teams mixed, content work-safe, and participation optional. Skip drinking games and anything that puts one person on stage alone.

### Do you need a host for large group games?

Yes. Past about 20 people, someone has to explain the rules once, keep time, and run the scoreboard. A host with a microphone is the difference between a game and a crowd near the snacks.
