The benefits of tabletop RPGs are easy to miss from the outside, because from the outside it looks like six adults in a dining room arguing about an imaginary door. From the inside, it is three hours of full attention, shared decision-making, and the kind of laughter that only happens when people have stopped protecting themselves.

This article is for the person who is curious but a little embarrassed, and for the partner or friend of a player who wants to know why the Thursday game is non-negotiable. The short answer is that pretend is not the opposite of serious. It is one of the oldest ways people have of practicing being people.

## Why does playing pretend feel embarrassing for adults?

Because somewhere around twelve, most of us were taught that imagination is for children and that adults do real things. Games survived that lesson only when they looked like competition: sports, poker, chess. A game with no winner, where the point is to make up a story with friends, did not fit the frame.

The frame is wrong, and it was always wrong. Adults rehearse conversations in the shower. They read novels, which is pretend with the lights on. They watch actors pretend for a living and call it culture. The only unusual thing about a tabletop RPG is that the pretending is done out loud, in a group, with the outcome unknown.

Once you see it that way, the embarrassment fades. What is left is a hobby that does several things adults need and rarely get.

## What does an RPG do that other hobbies do not?

Most leisure is either solitary or passive. You scroll alone, or you watch something together. A tabletop RPG is neither. It is active and shared, and it demands a kind of sustained mutual attention that has become rare.

Consider what a session asks of each person at the table:

- **Listen closely for hours.** The best move you can make is usually built on something another player just said. Drift, and you miss it.
- **Make decisions with incomplete information.** You do not know what is behind the door. You decide anyway, and you live with the roll.
- **Hold another person's perspective.** You spend the evening asking "what would she do?" about someone who is not you.
- **Tolerate failure in public.** The dice will humiliate you in front of friends. The group will laugh. You will laugh. Nothing bad happens.
- **Create something that did not exist.** By the end of the night there is a story nobody wrote, and everyone at the table owns a piece of it.

No single one of those is unique to RPGs. Doing all five at once, for three hours, with your phone face down, is close to unique. If you want the mechanics of how it works, [our tabletop RPG explainer](/articles/what-is-a-tabletop-rpg/) covers the basics.

## What does the research say?

Carefully, because the research is younger and smaller than the hobby's fans sometimes suggest.

There is a small but growing body of work on tabletop RPGs and wellbeing, including studies and case reports on their use in therapeutic settings with adolescents and adults, often focused on social skills, confidence, and communication. A [2025 systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12242152/) of RPGs as a tool for neurodivergent players found promising gains in social functioning and communication, but only five studies, all small. Therapists who use them describe the game as a low-stakes rehearsal space: a client who struggles to assert themselves in life can practice it as a character first.

The broader evidence on social connection is firmer. The [Harvard Study of Adult Development](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2017/04/over-nearly-80-years-harvard-study-has-been-showing-how-to-live-a-healthy-and-happy-life/), which has followed participants since 1938, has consistently found that close relationships predict long, healthy lives better than social class, IQ, or genes do; satisfaction with relationships at 50 forecast health at 80 better than cholesterol did. The U.S. Surgeon General's [2023 advisory on loneliness](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf) put the mortality effect of social disconnection on par with smoking up to 15 cigarettes a day, and above that of obesity or physical inactivity.

An RPG group is one of the most reliable relationship engines adults have. It meets on a schedule. It has a reason to exist that is not "let's catch up," which is the phrase that kills most adult friendships. And it generates shared history, week after week. That is the mechanism. Whether the dice themselves do anything is a smaller question than whether the table does.

## How do the benefits show up in real life?

Players and the people who live with them describe a consistent set of carryover effects. None of these are guaranteed, and none of them are the reason anyone plays. They are what happens on the side.

| At the table | Off the table |
|---|---|
| Deciding in character under pressure | Less agonizing over small decisions |
| Playing someone unlike yourself | More patience with people unlike yourself |
| Watching a plan fail and improvising | Lower panic when a real plan fails |
| Speaking up in a group of five | Speaking up in a meeting of five |
| Laughing at your own critical failure | Laughing at your own actual failures |
| Three hours of attention on others | Noticing when you have stopped listening |

The pattern is that the table is a practice field with the stakes removed. You can be brave as a paladin before you are brave at work. You can try being the person who talks first and see how it feels. The character is a costume, and the costume lets you rehearse.

## Is it escapism?

Partly, and that is fine. Everyone needs somewhere to go that is not their inbox. But escapism is a weak description of what actually happens at a good table.

An RPG does not help you avoid other people. It requires them. It does not numb attention the way a feed does; it demands it. And the story you escape into is one you are building with friends in real time, not one handed to you. If you leave a session, you leave with new inside jokes, a plan for next week, and the mild exhaustion of having paid close attention for three hours.

Compare that with the other common adult escape, which is watching something alone and remembering none of it a week later. We have written about [why game night memories last](/articles/why-game-night-memories-last/), and the reason applies even more strongly to RPGs: the memories are of things you did, with people, that could have gone another way.

## Why is the hobby growing now?

[Dungeons & Dragons](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0786969687?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) has had a visible resurgence over the past decade, helped by streaming shows, by its appearance in popular television, and by a pandemic that sent people looking for something to do together over video; Wizards of the Coast [reported a 33 percent revenue jump in 2020](https://www.axios.com/local/seattle/2025/01/29/dnd-wizards-2025-roadmap-dungeons-dragons-50-years) and now counts tens of millions of D&D Beyond accounts. But the deeper reason is probably simpler. The hobby offers exactly what the moment is short on.

Americans report [fewer close friends than they did in 1990](https://www.americansurveycenter.org/research/the-state-of-american-friendship-change-challenges-and-loss/): the share with no close friends rose from 3 to 12 percent, and the share with ten or more fell from a third to about one in eight. The third places where people used to meet by habit have thinned out. A weekly RPG group is a third place that fits in a dining room. It is one reason we are building a lounge in Indianapolis, and one reason this category of our writing exists at all.

People are not playing pretend because they have regressed. They are playing because it is one of the few structures left that reliably gets a group of adults in a room, paying attention to each other, every week, for years.

## What should a skeptic do with this?

Try one session. Not a campaign; a one-shot, which finishes in an evening and asks nothing further. Go with the expectation that the first twenty minutes will feel strange and the last twenty will not. Notice how long it has been since you spent three hours with friends and did not check your phone.

If it is not for you, you have lost an evening and gained a story about the time you tried. If it is for you, you have found something a lot of adults are quietly looking for. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### Are tabletop RPGs good for mental health?

Early research and clinical use suggest benefits for social confidence and anxiety, though the evidence base is still developing. The firmer benefit is regular, structured time with friends, which is well linked to wellbeing.

### Why do adults play D&D?

For the same reasons adults read novels or play team sports: shared attention, creative problem-solving, and a reliable reason to see friends every week.

### Do tabletop RPGs improve social skills?

Many players report it, and therapists use RPGs for that purpose. The game rehearses listening, speaking up, and perspective-taking in a low-stakes setting.

### Is playing D&D as an adult weird?

No more than joining a book club or a softball league. Millions of adults play, and the average age of players has risen as the hobby has grown.
