Leadership assessment games work because a cooperative board game creates a small, honest version of a crisis. In Pandemic, four people share one goal, limited time, and incomplete information. Someone has to propose a plan. Someone has to disagree. Someone has to notice the thing everyone missed. Within forty-five minutes, you will have seen each of those moments, and you will know who did what.

This is a guide for managers and HR leads who want a better read on emerging leaders than a performance review provides. It is not a replacement for proper assessment. It is a lens that most organizations never think to use.

## Why Pandemic, and not a competitive game?

Competitive games tell you who wants to win. That is a trait, but it is not leadership. Cooperative games tell you how someone behaves when the group's outcome depends on coordinating with people they do not control. That is much closer to the real job.

Pandemic in particular is a good fit for a few reasons:

- **Roles are asymmetric.** The Medic, the Scientist, the Dispatcher, and the Researcher each have different powers. Nobody can win alone, and the best plan uses everyone.
- **The pressure escalates.** Outbreaks cascade. A quiet early game turns into a scramble, and people behave differently when the board is on fire.
- **Information is public.** Every card and cube is visible, so the only variable is how the group processes it. Confusion is a group failure, not a hidden-information trick.
- **It is losable.** Pandemic beats new groups often. Watching how people respond to a loss is as informative as the game itself.

Forbidden Island and Forbidden Desert work the same way with simpler rules. The Crew works for teams that need to communicate under constraints. For broader options, see our list of [team-building board games](/articles/team-building-board-games/).

## What leadership behaviors show up at the table?

Here is what to watch for. The important thing is behavior, not volume. The person talking most is not necessarily leading.

### Framing the problem

In the first few turns, someone usually says a version of "okay, blue is the problem, let's focus there." That person is framing. Notice whether they are right, and notice whether they invited others to check them.

### Allocating roles

A strong play in Pandemic is recognizing that the Medic should be doing one thing and the Scientist another. The player who says "you take Asia, I'll handle the cure" is delegating based on capability. That instinct transfers.

### Updating when the plan breaks

An epidemic card lands, and the careful plan collapses. Some players freeze. Some double down on the old plan. Some say "new situation, what matters now?" That third response is the one you are looking for.

### Making space for the quiet player

Watch what happens when the least vocal person at the table has an idea. Does anyone ask them? Does the loudest player steamroll them? The player who turns and says "what do you think?" is doing something most managers have to be trained to do.

### Owning the loss

When the game is lost, listen to the first sentence each person says. "We should have cured blue earlier" is analysis. "I should have moved to Chicago" is ownership. "You wasted two turns" is blame. You will hear all three.

## What is the quarterback problem, and why does it matter here?

Cooperative games have a known weakness: one experienced or confident player can dictate every move, and the rest of the table becomes passive. Gamers call this the quarterback problem, and we have a full piece on it in [co-op games that don't have a quarterback problem](/articles/co-op-games-without-quarterback-problem/).

For a leadership assessment, the quarterback problem is a feature. It happens in real teams constantly. The question is not whether someone tries to quarterback. The question is what everyone else does about it, and whether the quarterback notices.

A player who quarterbacks and gets results might be a strong individual contributor. A player who quarterbacks, notices the table has gone quiet, and pulls back is showing something rarer.

## How do you read the table fairly?

A game is a small sample. Here is how to avoid over-reading it.

| What you observe | Fair reading | Unfair reading |
|---|---|---|
| Player takes charge immediately | Comfortable with ambiguity | Natural leader |
| Player stays quiet early | Gathering information or new to the game | Lacks initiative |
| Player disagrees with the plan | Willing to voice dissent | Difficult |
| Player defers constantly | May be new, junior, or polite | Follower |
| Player gets frustrated at a loss | Cares about outcomes | Poor temperament |
| Player jokes through the crisis | Manages tension | Not serious |

Experience with games is a confound. Someone who has played Pandemic twenty times will look like a strategist. Someone who has never played a modern board game will look lost for ten minutes. Correct for it: ask at the start who has played before, and weigh the second game more than the first.

Seniority is the other confound. Junior staff defer to directors at the table just as they do in meetings. If you want to see the junior staff, keep directors at a different table.

## How do you run a leadership assessment with a game?

A few choices make the observation more useful.

1. **Use tables of four, seated by design.** Mix departments and keep direct reporting lines apart.
2. **Have a neutral host teach and run each table.** The host explains rules, keeps the game moving, and takes no side. They are also your observer.
3. **Play twice.** Game one is learning. Game two is the one that tells you something. Swap one or two players between games if you can.
4. **Do not announce the purpose.** Say it is a team activity, because it is. The moment people know they are being assessed, the behavior changes.
5. **Observe, then ask.** After the session, ask participants who they thought made the best calls. The answers often surprise managers.
6. **Write down specifics within the hour.** "Priya reallocated the Medic after the second epidemic" is useful in six months. "Priya seemed engaged" is not.

Google's [Project Aristotle](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/understand-team-effectiveness) concluded that how a team worked together mattered more than who was on it, and put psychological safety at the top of its list of what separates effective teams. A Pandemic table makes that dynamic, who speaks and who gets heard, visible in a way a meeting does not.

## What are the ethical limits?

Treat this as one input among many, never as a gate. A board game should not decide a promotion. It can prompt a conversation, flag someone you had overlooked, or confirm a hunch you already had.

Be careful about personality. Introverts, non-native speakers, and people unfamiliar with games are all disadvantaged in a loud, fast table. If you want to see them lead, give them a quieter game or a second session. A fair assessment looks for the behavior in a setting where the person can show it.

And tell people afterward, if you used what you saw. Most will be pleased that someone noticed.

## What does this look like in practice?

A regional services company ran two rounds of Pandemic at a manager offsite. The person their VP expected to dominate did, in game one. In game two, after a brutal loss, a logistics coordinator who had said almost nothing laid out a plan that won. Two of the three other players said afterward that they had wanted to follow her from the start but she had not pushed.

That is the whole value in one anecdote: the leader was there the entire time. The table just gave her a reason to speak.

The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### What is the best board game for assessing leadership?

Pandemic is the standard choice because its roles are asymmetric and its pressure escalates. Forbidden Desert is simpler, and The Crew tests communication under tight constraints.

### How many people should be at each table?

Four. Pandemic supports two to four, and four gives enough interaction to observe without anyone getting lost.

### Should employees know they are being observed?

No. Present it as a team activity, which it is. Tell people afterward if their behavior shaped a decision about them.

### Can a game really predict leadership ability?

It can show leadership behavior under mild pressure in a single setting. Treat it as a prompt for further observation, not as proof.
