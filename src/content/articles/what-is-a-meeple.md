A meeple is a small wooden figure shaped like a simplified person, with a round head, stubby arms, and a solid base where the legs would be. It is the standard playing piece in Carcassonne and has since spread to hundreds of other games. If you have ever asked "what is a meeple" after someone at the table used the word like everyone should know it, this is the answer, along with the story of how a joke became the most recognizable object in the hobby.

The short version: the word is a blend of "my" and "people," coined at a game table in 2000. The shape came from the first edition of Carcassonne. Everything else is what happened when a lot of people decided they liked both.

## Where did the word "meeple" come from?

The accepted origin story credits [Alison Hansel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meeple), an American player, with inventing the word in November 2000 during a game of Carcassonne. She reached for one of her wooden figures, started to say "my people," and the two words collapsed into "meeple." Her group kept using it, she proposed it on a gaming forum that same month, and it stuck.

That is an unusually clean origin for a piece of slang. Most hobby words drift in from somewhere nobody can trace. This one has a name, a month, and a game attached to it, which is part of why people enjoy retelling it.

The word spread through BoardGameGeek and game-store conversation over the next few years. Carcassonne went on to sell [more than 12 million copies](https://www.meeplemountain.com/articles/carcassonne-20th-anniversary-a-history-and-celebration-of-carcassonne/), and long before that milestone "meeple" was the default word for any wooden person-shaped piece, whether or not the game's rulebook used it.

## Which game did the meeple come from?

Carcassonne, designed by Klaus-Jürgen Wrede and published by Hans im Glück in 2000. <!-- AFFILIATE --> It won the [Spiel des Jahres](https://www.spiel-des-jahres.de/en/games/carcassonne/), Germany's game of the year award, in 2001, which pushed it into mainstream shops across Europe and then the United States.

In Carcassonne you draw a tile, add it to a growing medieval map, and may place one of your wooden followers on the tile as a knight, monk, thief, or farmer. The rulebook calls them followers. Players called them meeples. The players won.

The piece needed to do a lot with very little. It had to stand up on its own, lie flat on its back when placed as a farmer, read clearly in five or six colors, and be cheap to produce in the thousands. A blocky person with no legs and no face does all of that.

## Why does a meeple look the way it does?

The shape is a product of wood and cost. Wooden pieces are stamped or cut from flat boards, so a meeple is really a silhouette with thickness. Fine details like separated legs or fingers would snap off or cost too much to cut, so the design keeps everything thick and rounded.

The result is a figure that is both abstract and human. It has no gender, no age, and no expression, which means it can be a farmer in Carcassonne, a worker in Agricola, or a family member in Stone Age without anyone objecting. <!-- AFFILIATE --> That flexibility is a big reason the shape outlived its original game.

Hans im Glück has treated the specific figure as part of its brand: it holds an EU trademark on the shape of the original Carcassonne meeple and has [registered the word itself](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meeple) in some territories, though not in the United States. That is why other publishers' "meeples" often have slightly different proportions: a rounder head, wider arms, a taller base. They are close relatives, not copies.

## What counts as a meeple and what doesn't?

Players use the word loosely, and that is fine. But there is a rough hierarchy, and knowing it helps you follow conversations in a game store.

| Term | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Meeple | A person-shaped wooden piece, flat-cut with a head, arms, and base | Carcassonne followers |
| Animeeple | A meeple shaped like an animal | The sheep, cattle, and boar in later Agricola editions |
| Standee | A flat cardboard figure in a plastic base | Many adventure games |
| Pawn | A simple upright marker, usually a cone or cylinder | Pandemic, Sorry! |
| Miniature | A sculpted plastic figure with detail | Gloomhaven, Zombicide |
| Token | Any small flat marker | Coins, resource chips |

Strictly, a Catan settlement is not a meeple and neither is a Ticket to Ride train. They are wooden pieces, but they are not people. Most players will not correct you. A few will, and now you know why.

The "animeeple" coinage shows how productive the word became. There are also "beeples" (bee meeples), "sheeple," and other puns that only make sense once the base word is universal.

## Why did the meeple become the symbol of the hobby?

A few reasons stack up.

**It is friendly.** A meeple looks like a toy, not a weapon or a war counter. For a hobby trying to tell newcomers that modern board games are not Risk or Monopoly, that matters. It is the opposite of intimidating.

**It is tactile.** People like holding them. Picking up a wooden piece and setting it down on a tile is a small physical pleasure, and games that offer it feel better than games that do not. Our piece on [why components change how a game feels](/articles/board-game-components-table-presence/) goes into this more.

**It is a person.** The meeple stands for the player at the table in a way a cube cannot. When you place your farmer, that is you claiming a field. That little act of identification is doing more work than it looks like.

**It arrived with the right game.** Carcassonne is a gateway game, easy to teach and quick to play, and it came out as the modern hobby was building its first real audience outside Germany. The piece was in the right box at the right time.

Today you will find meeples on logos, T-shirts, cafe signs, tattoos, and the covers of books about the hobby. Board game cafes use them the way coffee shops use a cup silhouette. They have become shorthand for "games are played here."

## Can you buy meeples on their own?

Yes. Several component companies sell meeples by color, in a [16 mm standard size](https://meeplesource.com/products.php?cat=13) that matches the Carcassonne follower, and in larger or smaller variants. People buy them to replace lost pieces, to upgrade games that ship with cardboard tokens, or to prototype their own designs.

Custom meeples are also a thing. Designers often add a shape unique to their game, such as a meeple holding a tool or wearing a hat, as a small signature. If you are working on your own design, standard meeples are the cheapest way to get a prototype on the table. ## Is "meeple" in the dictionary?

It has made it into at least one major dictionary. Oxford's online dictionary added "meeple" in its [August 2015 update](https://www.fortune.com/2015/08/27/oxford-online-dictionary), defining it as a small figure used as a playing piece in certain board games. That is a fast climb for a word coined at a kitchen table fifteen years earlier.

Dictionary inclusion is a lagging indicator. By the time lexicographers notice a word, it has already been in heavy use for years. For the hobby, the entry was a nice bit of recognition that board games had grown big enough to produce their own vocabulary.

## FAQ

### What does meeple stand for?

Nothing officially. It is a blend of "my" and "people," [coined in 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meeple) by a Carcassonne player who ran the two words together.

### What is the plural of meeple?

"Meeples" is standard, though some players use "meeple" as both singular and plural, like "sheep." Either is understood.

### Are Catan pieces meeples?

No. Catan uses houses, cities, and roads. A meeple is specifically a person-shaped piece. People say it loosely anyway, and that is fine.

### What size is a standard meeple?

The Carcassonne follower is [about 16 mm tall](https://www.carcassonnecentral.com/community/index.php?topic=28.0), and most "standard" replacement meeples match that. Larger and smaller sizes exist for specific games.

## One word, one table

The meeple is a small thing with a big job. It turns a flat map into a place where you have a stake, and it does it in one piece of painted wood. That is also what a game does for the people around it: a reason to sit down, a stake in the next hour, and someone to share it with. The game is the reason. The people are the point.
