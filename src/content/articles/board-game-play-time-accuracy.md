The board game play time printed on the box is almost never the time the game will take at your table. "60–90 minutes" usually means ninety minutes for a group that already knows the rules, with no setup, no teach, no bathroom break, and no one asking whether they can trade sheep for ore. For a new group on a first play, double it and add the time it takes to punch cardboard.

This is not a scandal. It is a measurement problem, and once you understand how the number gets on the box, you can plan game nights that end on time and pick games that fit the evening you have. Here is what the number means, what it leaves out, and how to estimate the real thing.

## Why is the board game play time on the box so wrong?

Publishers are not lying on purpose. The number comes from a specific context that rarely matches yours.

**It is measured by people who already know the game.** Playtesters have played dozens of times. Their turns are fast because the decisions are familiar. Your group is reading the card text for the first time.

**It excludes setup and teardown.** The clock starts when the first turn begins and stops when the winner is announced. Sorting Wingspan's bird cards back into the box is not on the clock. Neither is laying out the Gloomhaven map.

**It excludes the teach.** Explaining Catan to new players takes ten minutes. Explaining Terraforming Mars can take twenty-five. None of that counts.

**It is a marketing number.** A box that says "45 minutes" sells better than one that says "two hours on your first play." Publishers know a short number lowers the barrier, so the range tends to sit at the optimistic end of the truth.

**Player count stretches it.** Many boxes print one range for all player counts. A four-player game of Ticket to Ride is a different length than a two-player game, and the downtime between turns grows with every extra chair.

## What actually counts toward a real session?

If you want an honest number, you have to count everything from "let's play" to the box going back on the shelf. A full session has six parts.

1. **Choosing the game.** Five to fifteen minutes of a group standing in front of a shelf, longer if nobody will decide. This is real time and it is the most common reason a game night starts late.
2. **Setup.** Shuffling, sorting, dealing, building the board. Azul takes two minutes. Twilight Imperium takes closer to an hour.
3. **The teach.** Reading the rules aloud or explaining from memory. Varies wildly with the teacher. We wrote a whole piece on doing this well in [The Five-Minute Teach](/articles/how-to-teach-a-board-game/).
4. **The first-play tax.** New players take longer turns, make rules mistakes that need unwinding, and ask questions. A first play commonly runs 1.5 to 2 times the box number.
5. **The game itself.** The number on the box, roughly, once everyone knows what they are doing.
6. **Teardown.** Sorting components back into bags and inserts. Games with many small pieces, like Everdell or Scythe, take ten minutes to put away properly.

A rough rule for a first play with a new group: setup plus teach plus twice the box time plus teardown. For Pandemic, a box that says 45 minutes, that lands near ninety minutes end to end. For Wingspan at 70 minutes on the box, expect two and a half hours the first time.

## How do different game types compare?

Not all games lie by the same amount. The gap between the box and the table depends mostly on how much the game asks a new player to learn and how much happens between turns.

| Game | Box play time | Realistic first-play session (4 players) | Where the time goes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Codenames | 15 min | 20–30 min | Almost honest. Teach is two minutes. |
| Azul | 30–45 min | 45–60 min | Short teach, fast setup, a little analysis paralysis late. |
| Ticket to Ride | 30–60 min | 60–90 min | Teach is easy; the first game runs long because nobody blocks early. |
| Catan | 60–120 min | 90–150 min | Board setup, trading, and the slow player who keeps the dice. |
| Pandemic | 45 min | 75–100 min | Group discussion on every turn roughly doubles the clock. |
| Wingspan | 40–70 min | 120–150 min | Card text, engine building, sorting at the end. |
| Terraforming Mars | 120 min | 180–240 min | Long teach, 200-plus unique cards, significant downtime. |
| Gloomhaven (one scenario) | 60–120 min | 150–200 min | Setup alone can take thirty minutes. |

These are estimates from experience, not measurements, and your group will vary. The pattern holds: the more unique text in the game, the further the box drifts from the table.

## Why does an honest play time matter?

Because the length of a game night is the thing most people are actually deciding on. Nobody skips game night because the games are bad. They skip because the last one ran until 12:40 on a Tuesday.

**Honest times protect the group.** A standing game night that reliably ends when it says it will is one people can commit to. The ones that drift become the ones people quietly stop attending. The mechanics of groups that last are in [The Standing Game Night](/articles/how-to-start-a-game-night-group/), and a predictable end time is near the top of the list.

**Honest times prevent the half-finished game.** The worst outcome at a table is not losing. It is stopping at 11 p.m. with the game two-thirds done because someone has to drive home. Nobody remembers who was winning. The evening ends on a shrug.

**Honest times help you match the game to the room.** A group that arrives at 7:30 and starts leaving at 10 has about two hours of real play. That is one medium game or three short ones, not Twilight Imperium.

**Honest times are respectful to new players.** Telling a first-timer "this takes about an hour" and then holding them for three is how people decide board games are not for them.

## How should designers and publishers print play time?

A few publishers are already doing this better, and the practices are worth copying.

- **Print a range by player count.** "30 min per player" or "2p: 45 min, 4p: 90 min" is far more useful than one number.
- **Separate setup from play.** "Setup 10 min. Play 60 min." Two numbers cost nothing and remove the biggest surprise.
- **Flag the first play.** "First game: add 30 minutes for the rules" is a sentence that would save thousands of game nights a year.
- **Measure with new players, not the design team.** The box time should be the median of a first play by a group that has read the rules once. That is the number a buyer actually needs.
- **Design the setup down.** Good inserts, pre-sorted decks, and a setup card that fits on one side of a sheet cut real minutes. Some of the best-loved games of the last decade, like Azul and The Crew, are loved partly because they are on the table in two minutes.

For designers, time every playtest from the moment players sit down. The number you are afraid to print is the one your players will experience.

## How do you plan a game night around real play times?

A practical method that works for most groups:

1. **Count your real window.** From the time the last person usually arrives to the time the first person usually leaves. Be honest. It is shorter than you think.
2. **Subtract thirty minutes.** Greetings, snacks, choosing a game, and the one person who is always late.
3. **Pick games that fit the remainder using session time, not box time.** For a two-hour window, that means box times of 45 minutes or less for a group learning something new.
4. **Have a short closer ready.** A fifteen-minute game like Codenames, Just One, or Wavelength lets you end on a high note instead of a half-finished board.
5. **Call the end before you start.** "We stop at 10 either way" removes the awkwardness later. Groups that say this out loud finish more games, not fewer.

## FAQ

### How accurate is the play time on a board game box?

Usually optimistic by a wide margin. It measures experienced players and excludes setup, teaching, and teardown. For a first play with a new group, expect 1.5 to 2 times the printed number plus setup time.

### How long does a board game actually take to play?

End to end, a "45-minute" game typically takes 75 to 100 minutes on a first play once you count setup, the rules explanation, slower new-player turns, and putting it away. Short games with little text, like Codenames or Azul, come closest to the box.

### Does player count change board game play time?

Yes, often significantly. Each additional player adds turns and downtime, so a four-player game can run nearly twice as long as the same game with two. Look for boxes that print time per player.

### What is the best way to shorten a long board game?

Set up before guests arrive, teach from a short script, use a turn timer only if the group agrees, and agree on an end time before starting. Playing a game a second time is the single biggest reduction; most games drop close to the box number by the third play.
