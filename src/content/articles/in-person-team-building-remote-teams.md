In-person team building for remote teams has a constraint no office team faces: you get one day, maybe two, and then everyone flies home to a grid of video tiles. Whatever happens in that room has to last six or twelve months. That changes what the day is for. The goal is not a productive meeting. The goal is that the next Slack message from a colleague lands as a message from a person you know.

This guide is for managers and people leads planning a distributed team's gathering. It covers what to prioritize, what to cut, and how to structure the hours so they stick.

## What does a remote team actually need from a day together?

Remote teams are often good at work and thin on everything else. They have shipped projects together without knowing who has kids, who plays in a band, or who is quietly struggling. Video calls strip out the hallway, the lunch, the walk to the parking lot. Those were where the knowing happened.

So the day needs to rebuild three things:

1. **Faces to names.** Obvious, but it goes deeper than recognition. People need to have sat next to each other, heard each other laugh, seen each other think.
2. **A shared memory.** Something that happened to all of you, that you can reference later. Remote teams have almost none of these.
3. **A reason to reach out.** Two people who discovered they both grew up in Ohio, or both hate cilantro, or both play the same game, now have a thread to pull on.

Notice what is not on the list: the strategy session. You may need one. But if the strategy session consumes the day, you will have flown twenty people in for something that could have been a document.

## How should you split the day?

A rough split that has worked for distributed teams of eight to sixty:

| Block | Time | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Morning | 3 hours | Real work: the planning or decisions that justified the flights |
| Lunch | 90 min | Long, seated, assigned tables mixed across sub-teams |
| Early afternoon | 1 hour | Short, mixed-table game session to reset energy |
| Late afternoon | 2 hours | Main activity, the thing people will remember |
| Evening | Open | Dinner, then games available for those who want them |

The morning is for the work because people are freshest. The afternoon shifts to connection because energy drops and because connection should lead into the evening, when the best conversations happen.

If you have two days, keep this shape and add a second morning of work. Do not add a second afternoon of activity. One strong shared experience beats two diluted ones.

## Why are games the right tool for this?

A remote team's problem is that people do not know how each other think. Games make thinking visible. Thirty minutes of Codenames shows you that the backend engineer gives beautifully precise clues and that the account manager guesses on instinct. You will remember that the next time you are on a call with them.

Games also solve the initiation problem. Nobody at a remote-team meetup wants to walk up to a near-stranger and start a conversation. A table with a game on it hands everyone a reason to talk. The conversation about the game becomes a conversation about anything.

And games produce the shared memory. The moment someone drew a catastrophically bad picture in Telestrations, or the table lost Pandemic on the last turn, will come up in Slack for months. ## Which games fit a remote team's one day?

The constraint is time. Nobody should spend twenty minutes learning rules when they have six hours to build a year's worth of connection. A short list:

**For the early-afternoon reset (15–30 min each):**

- Just One. Cooperative, fast, impossible to embarrass yourself.
- Wavelength. Produces opinions and laughter immediately.
- Codenames. Mixed teams, quick rounds, reveals thinking styles.

**For the main activity (60–120 min):**

- A hosted game session with three or four games rotating across mixed tables. This works for any headcount and lets people find their own pace.
- Blood on the Clocktower for groups of ten to twenty, with a skilled storyteller. It is the single most memorable large-group game we know.
- A cooperative game like Pandemic or Forbidden Desert at each table, with a shared debrief. Good for teams that want to see how they handle pressure together.

**For the evening (open):**

- A shelf of games with a host who can teach them. Azul, Ticket to Ride, The Crew, Sushi Go!. No obligation. The people who want to play will, and they will be the last to leave.

Avoid long strategy games and anything with a steep teach. A remote team's day is too short for Gloomhaven.

## What mistakes do remote teams make with in-person days?

The same ones, repeatedly.

- **Over-scheduling.** Every hour filled, no gaps. The hallway conversation is the point, and you have scheduled it out.
- **Making the dinner the only social time.** Dinner is loud. People talk to the two neighbors they can hear. Build connection in the afternoon so dinner builds on it.
- **Running the day like a conference.** Presentations, panels, a keynote from leadership. People flew in to see each other, not slides.
- **Seating sub-teams together.** They already know each other. Mix every table, every time.
- **Forgetting the new hires.** A remote new hire's first in-person day is enormous for them. Make sure they have a host, a table, and a reason to meet people across the org. We cover that in [onboarding new hires with a game night](/articles/new-hire-onboarding-game-night/).
- **Skipping the follow-up.** The day ends and nothing changes online. Pick one thing to carry forward: a monthly remote game hour, a photo channel, a running Codenames rivalry.

## How do you carry it back online?

The in-person day plants seeds. A little tending keeps them alive.

- **Name the memories.** Post the photos. Give the Telestrations disaster a channel. Reference it in the next all-hands.
- **Keep one low-effort ritual.** A thirty-minute optional game hour on video every two weeks, using a browser version of Codenames or a quick party game. Attendance will be partial. That is fine.
- **Pair people who clicked.** If two people from different sub-teams got along, give them a reason to work together in the next quarter.
- **Ask the quiet ones.** Some people connect best one-on-one. A follow-up coffee chat, remote, between two people who sat at the same table is worth more than another group call.

## What does a good day feel like at the end?

People are tired and do not want to leave. Someone proposes "one more game." Two colleagues who had never spoken are deep in a conversation about something unrelated to work. The manager has not said a word in an hour and does not need to.

That is what the flights were for. The strategy doc could have been shared. The people could not. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### How much of a remote team's in-person day should be team building?

About half. Use the morning for the work that required being in a room, and the afternoon and evening for connection.

### What is the best activity for a remote team meeting in person?

A hosted game session with mixed tables and short games. It scales to any headcount, needs no fitness or special skill, and produces the shared memories remote teams lack.

### How do you include remote new hires in an in-person day?

Seat them with people outside their sub-team for the first activity, assign a host, and make sure a tenured colleague introduces them around at dinner.

### How often should a remote team meet in person?

Once or twice a year is typical. The less often you meet, the more the day should lean toward connection over meetings.
