Area majority is a scoring system where several players can have pieces in the same region at once, and the player with the most pieces there scores the most, often with smaller rewards for second and third place. Area control, in the strict sense, means one player holds a region outright and nobody else is in it. The two terms get used interchangeably, and the games they describe can look alike on the table. But the way you play them is almost opposite, and knowing which one you are sitting down to saves you from a long, confused first game.

This article explains both terms, where the line between them falls, what each one asks of you, and which games teach each best.

## What is area control?

In area control, a region belongs to whoever occupies it. If you are there, nobody else is. To take a region from someone, you remove them, usually by combat.

Risk is the common ancestor. <!-- AFFILIATE --> Each territory has one owner. Armies move in, dice are rolled, and the loser's pieces leave. Scoring, such as it is, comes from holding territories and continents at the start of your turn.

Modern examples with the same bones:

- **Small World**, where you conquer regions by paying tokens and the previous occupant is pushed out.
- **Kemet** and **Blood Rage**, where battles have winners and losers and the loser retreats.
- **Root**, where factions fight for clearings and the cat player's warriors are removed when they lose.
- **Twilight Imperium**, where a system has one controller.

The defining feel: exclusive ownership and displacement. The map is a patchwork of single colors.

## What is area majority?

In area majority, a region can hold pieces from every player at the same time. Nobody is pushed out. At scoring time, you count who has the most pieces in each region and award points by rank: most gets the big prize, second gets a smaller one, third maybe gets something.

El Grande (1995) is the game that defined the style, and it remains the reference. <!-- AFFILIATE --> Players place caballeros into the regions of medieval Spain. At three scoring rounds, each region pays out to first, second, and third place. Nobody ever removes anyone else's pieces. The whole game is about where to be the most, where to settle for second, and where to not bother.

Other well-known area majority games:

- **Carcassonne's farms**, where several players' farmers can share a field and the one with the most takes all the points.
- **Blood Rage's provinces** at Ragnarok, which combine combat with majority scoring.
- **Ethnos**, where tribes placed in regions score by rank at the end of each age.
- **Inis**, which uses presence and majority in territories for its win conditions.
- **Tigris & Euphrates**, where leader strength in kingdoms is a form of majority.
- **Chinatown** and **Acquire**, which apply the idea to shares in a company rather than pieces on a map.

The hobby sometimes calls this "area influence" to make the distinction clearer. That is a good name, because influence is exactly what you are placing. You are not taking the region. You are making it yours more than anyone else's.

## Where is the line between them?

The cleanest test is one question: can two players have pieces in the same region at the same time, and both score from it?

- If no, it is area control.
- If yes, it is area majority.

Many games blur it. Blood Rage has combat (control) but scores provinces by presence (majority). Root is mostly control but scores some things by presence. Scythe has territories that one player holds (control) but almost no combat (so it plays like a positional game). The labels describe tendencies, not boxes.

Here is how the two compare in play.

| | Area control | Area majority |
|---|---|---|
| Who can be in a region | One player | Several |
| How you gain a region | Remove the occupant | Outnumber them |
| What second place gets | Nothing | Often points |
| Main currency | Combat strength | Placement count and timing |
| Typical conflict | Direct, with losers | Indirect, with rankings |
| Classic example | Risk | El Grande |
| Feel | War | Election |

The election comparison is useful. In an area control game you invade. In an area majority game you campaign: you decide which districts to contest, how many pieces to commit, and where you can win cheaply because nobody else is trying.

## How does the difference change how you play?

This is the practical part. Players who treat an area majority game like a control game lose, and vice versa.

**In area majority, second place is a strategy.** A region that pays eight points for first and four for second is worth four points to a player with a single piece in it, as long as nobody else has a single piece. Spreading thin to collect second places across the map often beats stacking everything into one region to win it by a landslide. New El Grande players pile ten caballeros into one province and lose to the player who placed two in every province.

**In area majority, overkill is waste.** Winning a region by one is the same as winning it by five. Every piece beyond what you need to hold first place is a piece that could have claimed second somewhere else. Good majority players count their margin obsessively.

**In area majority, timing is everything.** Because nobody is removed, the only thing that changes a region's ranking is new pieces arriving. If scoring happens at fixed moments, the last placement before scoring is the one that matters. Games like El Grande and Ethnos are largely about knowing when the count happens and being the last to move before it.

**In area control, commitment pays.** Holding a region means defending it, and a half-defended region is just a gift. Control games reward concentration of force and punish spreading thin, which is the exact opposite of majority games.

**In area control, the loser leaves.** Fights have consequences. A bad attack costs you pieces you do not get back. In majority games, a lost region costs you points but your pieces are still there, possibly scoring second.

**In both, read the map for what nobody wants.** The most profitable region is often the one others have overlooked. In control games, that is the unguarded province. In majority games, that is the region where a single piece claims first.

## Why do designers like area majority?

It solves the two biggest problems of area control: elimination and runaway leaders.

Because nobody is removed, nobody is out. A player can be behind in every region and still be in the game. And because second and third place score, a dominant player cannot lock down the map; the more they win first places, the more second places are left for everyone else. The scoring itself is a mild catch-up mechanic, which our piece on [catch-up mechanics](/articles/catch-up-mechanics-board-games/) discusses in general.

Majority scoring also produces tension without combat. There are no dice, no losers of a battle, no removed pieces, yet every placement matters and every player affects every other. For designers who want interaction without a fight, it is one of the best tools available.

The weakness is kingmaking. A player who cannot win first in a region can still decide who does by where they drop their last piece. El Grande has this, and most majority games do. Experienced groups accept it as part of the politics.

## Which games teach each style best?

For area majority, start with El Grande if you can find it, or Ethnos for a faster teach. Carcassonne's farming rule is the gentlest introduction, since the rest of the game is simple and the farm scoring shows the idea in one clear case. <!-- AFFILIATE -->

For area control, Small World is the friendliest: short, funny, and built around the rhythm of conquering and declining. <!-- AFFILIATE --> Kemet and Blood Rage are the next step for groups who want real fights. Root is for groups ready to each learn a different rulebook.

For the blend, Blood Rage and Inis both show how a designer can take the best of each: battles with consequences, scoring by presence.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between area majority and area control?

In area control, one player owns a region and others must remove them to take it. In area majority, several players can share a region and score by who has the most pieces there, often with points for second and third.

### Is Risk area control or area majority?

Area control. Each territory has exactly one owner, and you take it by defeating their armies.

### Is Carcassonne an area majority game?

Partly. Cities, roads, and cloisters are mostly single-owner, but farms and shared cities use majority scoring, where the player with the most meeples takes the points.

### What is area influence?

Another name for area majority. It stresses that players are adding influence to a region rather than owning it outright.

## Most, not all

Area majority is a quiet lesson in not needing to own everything. You can share a region, score second, and still win the game, which is a better description of how people actually get along than most war games manage. The game is the reason. The people are the point.
