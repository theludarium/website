Semi-cooperative board games put everyone on the same team, mostly. Either one player is secretly working against the group, or every player has a private goal that may conflict with the shared one. You cooperate because you must and suspect because you should.

Done well, it is the most dramatic thing that happens at a game table. Done badly, it is a mess of hurt feelings and a game nobody wanted to finish. This article explains the two kinds of semi-co-op, which games handle each well, and how to run one so the table stays friends.

## What makes a game semi-cooperative?

Pure co-ops have one win condition for everyone. Competitive games have a winner and losers. Semi-co-ops sit between, and there are two distinct flavors.

**Hidden traitor.** Most players are loyal; one or more are secretly against the group. The traitor wins if the team fails. Shadows over Camelot, Battlestar Galactica, and Dead of Winter are the classics. Social deduction games like Werewolf and The Resistance are the stripped-down version of the same idea, and we wrote about [why social deduction works](/articles/why-social-deduction-games-work/) separately.

**Personal objectives.** Everyone is loyal, but each player has a secret goal they must also complete to win. The team can succeed while you personally fail. Dead of Winter does this too, which is why it is on both lists. Archipelago and CO2 use similar structures.

The two feel different. A traitor game is about trust and accusation. A personal-objective game is about priorities and small selfishness. Both introduce doubt into a team, which is the whole point.

## Why do semi-co-ops create such strong feelings?

Because they use the trust that co-ops build and then put it at risk.

In a pure co-op, you can take a teammate's suggestion at face value. In a semi-co-op, every suggestion carries a question: why are they saying this? That question is exhausting and thrilling in equal measure. It makes ordinary moves, like discarding a card or choosing where to go, into tells.

The feelings that follow are real. Being accused when you are loyal stings. Being caught when you are the traitor is a specific flavor of embarrassment. Being the traitor and winning feels wonderful and slightly guilty. These games generate stories because they generate emotion.

That is also why they can go wrong. If one player takes the accusation personally, or another cannot lie without feeling bad, the game stops being a game. Know your table.

## How do you run a semi-co-op without ruining friendships?

Frame it before the first card is dealt.

1. **Say the words.** "Someone at this table may be lying tonight, and that is the game. Nobody is a bad person for lying well." It sounds obvious. Saying it out loud gives everyone permission.
2. **Let the traitor lose gracefully.** When the traitor is caught, cheer the catch, not the catcher. Avoid "I knew it was you" after the fact.
3. **Keep accusations about moves, not people.** "That discard was suspicious" is fine. "You always do this" is not.
4. **Play short ones first.** The Resistance or Spyfall in thirty minutes teaches the emotional shape of the thing before you spend three hours on Battlestar.
5. **Debrief.** After the game, let the traitor explain their choices. It is the best part, and it converts frustration into appreciation.

Some people simply do not enjoy deceiving friends, and no framing fixes that. Give them the personal-objective version instead. In Dead of Winter, a loyal player with a secret goal gets to be a little selfish without ever lying to anyone's face.

## What is the difference between a traitor game and a social deduction game?

Overlap is large, but the distinction is useful.

Social deduction games are nearly all talk. Werewolf, The Resistance, and Avalon have minimal mechanics; the game is in the conversation. Semi-cooperative board games put the deduction inside a real game with resources, a board, and a threat. The traitor in Dead of Winter is also managing food and zombies. Their lies are expressed through play, not just speech.

That makes board-based semi-co-ops harder to read and more forgiving of quiet players. Someone who hates being interrogated in Werewolf can be a fine traitor in Shadows over Camelot by simply making slightly worse plays. The game gives them cover.

## Are semi-co-ops good for groups who hate losing to each other?

Surprisingly, yes, with the right framing. The pure co-op removes competition entirely; we covered that shelf in our list of the [best cooperative board games](/articles/best-cooperative-board-games/). The semi-co-op puts a little competition back, but it is competition against a role, not a person. When the traitor is revealed, the players who lost can blame the traitor card rather than their friend.

It is a useful middle step for groups moving from cooperative to competitive play, or back again. The losing feels shared. The winning, when the team catches the traitor in time, feels like a heist pulled off together.

## FAQ

### What is a semi-cooperative board game?

A game where players mostly work together but at least one may be secretly working against the team, or each has a private goal that can conflict with the shared one.

### What is the best semi-cooperative game for beginners?

The Resistance or Saboteur for short sessions, and Dead of Winter for a full evening. Shadows over Camelot is a good middle option.

### Can you play Dead of Winter without a traitor?

Yes. The rules include a no-betrayer variant, and even in the standard game there may be no traitor in a given round, which keeps everyone guessing.

### Is Betrayal at House on the Hill a semi-cooperative game?

Yes, in a late-reveal form. Players explore cooperatively until a "haunt" triggers, at which point one player usually becomes the traitor with a secret goal.
