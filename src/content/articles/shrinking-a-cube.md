Most owners never shrink a cube. Adding is fun and cutting is not, so cubes drift upward until the owner declares a size and stops counting. But the best thing many 540s could do is lose ninety cards. A tighter cube drafts more consistently, supports its lanes more reliably, and is easier to keep sharp. And 450, the size nobody plans to build, is often where a cube that grew too far should land.

This article makes the case for 450, explains how to tell whether your cube is bloated, and gives a method for cutting that removes cards without removing what works.

## Why 450?

Because it keeps most of what 540 offers and fixes most of what 540 costs.

At 450 with eight players and fifteen-card packs, 360 cards are opened, which is 80 percent of the cube. Compare 540, where two thirds are opened, and 360, where everything is. At 80 percent, any given card is in the draft four nights out of five. That is enough variety that drafts feel different and enough consistency that lanes are reliable and drafters can plan.

Other properties of 450:

- **Ten players fit.** 10 × 3 × 15 = 450 exactly. A 360 cannot seat ten at full packs; a 450 can with nothing left over.
- **Two pods of five.** 225 each, a decent five-player draft. Not as clean as 540's two pods of six, but it works.
- **Lane density is near 360 levels.** A lane that needs twelve cards on the table needs fifteen in the cube, not eighteen. Easier to fill, easier to keep strong.
- **Aggro holds up.** Cheap creature density stays close to what the 360 had.
- **It fits in one box.** Which matters more than it should.

Pauper cubes, among the longest-running community-maintained lists, often settle at 450; The Pauper Cube's own list on CubeCobra sits at 450 cards ([CubeCobra](https://cubecobra.com/cube/overview/thepaupercube)). It is not an eccentric size. It is the size a lot of cubes become when their owners stop and count.

## Is your cube bloated? Six signs

You do not need to shrink a cube because it is big. You need to shrink it because it has stopped being sharp. Here is how to tell.

1. **Last picks repeat.** Across five drafts, the same twenty or thirty cards keep going thirteenth, fourteenth, fifteenth. They are not being drafted; they are being passed.
2. **You cannot name a role for a card.** Pull twenty cards at random. If more than two or three have no answer to "what is this for," the cube has a layer of "good cards" that are not doing a job.
3. **Lanes are sometimes there and sometimes not.** A drafter who committed to a lane in pack one found no support in pack three, and it was not because another drafter took it. The lane is too thin for the size.
4. **The aggro drafter complains.** Red or white aggro is a fine deck one night and a pile of three-drops the next. Cheap creature density is below the size's requirement.
5. **New cards go in and nothing comes out.** If your last ten updates were adds, the count has drifted. Check it. A "540" that is actually 560 is common.
6. **You have not cut a card you like in a year.** Not a sign of a perfect cube. A sign the owner has stopped making hard calls.

Three or more of these and the cube will play better smaller. If you want to run the full diagnostic before deciding, [your cube's first draft](/articles/first-cube-draft-test/) describes the observation method and it works at any age.

## The cut method: 540 to 450 in four passes

Cutting ninety cards at once is paralyzing. Cutting in passes with different criteria is manageable. Do them in order and stop when you reach the number.

**Pass one: the data.** Pull your last-pick records if you have them, or CubeCobra's pick data if your drafts are logged there. Any card that went in the bottom three picks in most of the drafts it appeared in is cut. Expect twenty to thirty cards from this pass. No judgment required; the drafters already made it.

**Pass two: the duplicates.** For every role in every color, list the cards that fill it, best to worst. Cut the worst card in any role that has more than its 450 count. If white wipes should be three at 450 and you have five, the two weakest go. This pass is where the spreadsheet earns its keep. Expect twenty to forty cards.

**Pass three: the narrow.** Any card that needs three or more specific other cards to be good is cut unless its lane is one of the ones you are keeping at full depth. Build-arounds that are "fun when it works" are the first things a smaller cube cannot afford. Expect ten to twenty cards.

**Pass four: the ratio.** Count what remains by section and compare to a 450 template (about 58 per color, 40 gold, 30 colorless, 60 lands as a house baseline). Trim the over-full sections to size, cutting the weakest card in the largest role. Lands come last and should lose the fewest; a 450 needs about five sixths of a 540's fixing, not less.

Keep every cut card in a labeled box. Some will come back when you discover a lane got too thin, and that is fine.

## What should you never cut?

Four categories survive every pass.

**Signposts.** The two cards per color pair that announce the lane. Cutting one makes the lane invisible even if the rest of its support is intact.

**Cheap removal.** One- and two-mana removal is what keeps the format honest. It is also unglamorous and tempting to trim in favor of a splashy creature. Keep the count as a ratio of the new size.

**One- and two-drops in the aggro colors.** Same reason. A 450 needs about 83 percent of a 540's cheap creature count, and the cheap creatures are the least exciting cards to protect.

**Fixing lands.** Cut utility lands freely. Cut duals only in proportion.

If the cut passes leave you short in any of these, put the cards back and cut from the midrange four- and five-drops instead. There are always more of those than the cube needs.

## How do you shrink without the group noticing the wrong things?

Announce it, then draft it twice before taking feedback.

Drafters notice cuts emotionally before they notice them structurally. Someone's favorite card left and the first draft after will feature a complaint about it. Let the cube play. By the third draft, the conversation is usually about how clean the lanes feel, and the missing favorite has been forgotten or has a case for coming back on merit.

Publish the cut list. A changelog that says "cut for pass one: last-pick data" removes the sense that the owner played favorites. Keep it in the primer.

## When 450 is wrong

It is not a universal answer. Stay at 540 or larger if you regularly need two pods of six or more, if your group has told you they value surprise over consistency, or if the cube's identity is built on variety (chaos cubes, nostalgia cubes, very large Commander cubes). Go all the way to 360 if your group is six or fewer and you want full visibility.

450 is for the common case: a cube that grew because adding was easy, drafted by a group of six to ten, whose owner wants it to play like it did when it was tight. For that cube, ninety cards is the right number to lose.

The game is the reason. The people are the point. A smaller cube is not a smaller game. It is the same game with less in the way.

## FAQ

### Is 450 a good cube size?

Yes. It opens 80 percent of the cube with eight players, seats exactly ten at fifteen-card packs, keeps lanes nearly as dense as a 360, and fits in one box. It suits groups of six to ten.

### How do I know if my cube is too big?

Repeated last picks, cards with no clear role, lanes that appear only some nights, unreliable aggro, and a count that has drifted above the stated size are the main signs.

### How many cards should I cut from a 540 to get to 450?

Ninety, roughly 12 per color, 10 gold, 8 colorless, and 10 lands if the sections were in standard proportion. Cut by last-pick data, role duplicates, narrow build-arounds, and section ratio, in that order.

### Should I cut lands when shrinking a cube?

Cut utility lands in proportion, but keep fixing lands close to their original ratio. A smaller cube still needs enough duals per draft for the decks you want to exist.
