The best board games for 4 year olds are short, need no reading, and let everyone finish smiling. Start with a cooperative game, where the whole table wins or loses together: [First Orchard](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004BW8TYC?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Count Your Chickens!](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004HVKAAS?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Feed the Woozle](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00L3DQ1B0?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Busytown: Eye Found It!](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B002V3RCE6?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), or [Hoot Owl Hoot!](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004HVKAAI?tag=cubecommonw0a-20). Add a race where nobody is ever far behind, like [Snail's Pace Race](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004KZ8P2Q?tag=cubecommonw0a-20). When your child is ready for a small dose of winning and losing, try [Pengoloo](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0014BDYDE?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Chicken Cha Cha Cha](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00806PI1W?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Spot It! Jr. Animals](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0075XNGGK?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Animal Upon Animal](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B073CD98D6?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), and [Robot Turtles](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00HN2BXUY?tag=cubecommonw0a-20).

**The quick answer:** for a first game, buy **Count Your Chickens!** (cooperative, counting, about 15 minutes). For a second, add **Pengoloo** or **Animal Upon Animal**, which bring in gentle competition with real choices.

Below are 11 picks, a short guide to what four-year-olds can and cannot do at a table, a comparison table, and a "which one first" guide. Every game here is rated for age 4 or younger on the box.

## What can a 4-year-old handle in a board game?

Four is a big year. Most four-year-olds can sit for a short game, take turns with a reminder, and follow one rule at a time. They are not yet ready for strategy, scoring, or losing often. Children vary a lot at this age, so treat this as a typical picture, not a test.

1. **Reading: none.** Every game for this age should run on colors, pictures, and shapes. If a card has words, a grown-up has to read it, and the game slows down.
2. **Counting: small numbers.** Counting out three or four pieces works well. Adding two dice together usually does not.
3. **Turns: short and simple.** One action per turn is ideal. "Roll and move the matching snail" is the right size. Waiting through three other players for a long turn is not.
4. **Attention: about 10 to 15 minutes.** Pick games that end before the wiggles start. A game that finishes in ten minutes gets played twice. A game that runs thirty gets abandoned.
5. **Losing: hard, and normal.** Most four-year-olds find losing genuinely upsetting. That is why cooperative games are the backbone of this list. Our guide to [teaching kids to lose at games](/articles/teaching-kids-to-lose-at-games/) explains how to build the skill slowly.
6. **Hands: still learning.** Small pieces, tweezers, and stacking all train fine motor skills, which is part of the fun. Check the box for small parts if a younger sibling is around.

One more thing to know: four-year-olds love rules once they learn them. They will hold you to the rules more strictly than any adult. That is a good sign. It means the game is working.

## Cooperative games: everyone wins together

These five put the whole table on one team against the game. Nobody loses alone, and a grown-up can coach out loud without it feeling like taking sides. For older siblings, our list of [cooperative games for kids](/articles/cooperative-games-for-kids/) picks up where this one leaves off.

### First Orchard

[First Orchard](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004BW8TYC?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) is HABA's "My Very First Games" version of its long-running Orchard game. It plays 1 to 4, rated 2 and up, in about 10 minutes. Players roll a color die and pick the matching chunky wooden fruit from the trees, trying to fill the basket before the raven reaches the orchard.

**Why it fits:** it is the gentlest game on this list, and it is built for the youngest players. The pieces are big, the choice is "find the red one," and the whole game fits inside a four-year-old's attention span. If your child is a young four or has never played a board game, start here. If they are an older four who already plays, skip ahead to Count Your Chickens!.

### Count Your Chickens!

[Count Your Chickens!](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004HVKAAS?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) from Peaceable Kingdom plays 2 to 4, rated 3 and up, in about 15 minutes. Spin the spinner, move Mother Hen to the next matching picture, and count how many spaces she moved. That is how many chicks you bring back to the coop. Watch out for the fox, who can send a chick running.

**Why it fits:** counting is the whole game, so a four-year-old practices numbers without noticing. The counting stays small and concrete, one chick per space. Because everyone is on the same team, a child who miscounts gets help instead of a penalty. It is the best first board game for most four-year-olds.

### Feed the Woozle

[Feed the Woozle](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00L3DQ1B0?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) from Peaceable Kingdom plays 2 to 5, rated 3 and up, in about 15 minutes. Roll the die, load that many snacks onto a spoon, and walk them over to the hungry Woozle without dropping any. Feed it 12 snacks before the snacks run out, and everyone wins.

**Why it fits:** it gets a four-year-old out of the chair. The game has three levels, and the harder ones add silly challenges to the walk. It is a good pick for a child who cannot sit still yet, and for a mixed group where a toddler sibling wants to join.

### Busytown: Eye Found It!

[Busytown: Eye Found It!](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B002V3RCE6?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), based on Richard Scarry's books, plays 2 to 4 and is rated 3 and up. Players move along a long, busy board together, trying to reach the ferry together. Along the way, search cards send everyone hunting the board for hidden pictures.

**Why it fits:** it is a picture book you can play. The searching gives a four-year-old something they are often better at than the adults, which is a wonderful feeling at this age. The board is huge, so clear a floor rather than a table.

### Hoot Owl Hoot!

[Hoot Owl Hoot!](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004HVKAAI?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) from Peaceable Kingdom plays 2 to 4, rated 4 and up, in about 15 minutes. Players draw color cards and fly any owl to the next matching space, trying to get every owl back to the nest before the sun comes up.

**Why it fits:** it adds the first real choice. Which owl should fly? Moving the one at the back might help the team more than moving the one in front. That is a big idea for a four-year-old, and it is the bridge from "roll and move" to thinking ahead. It also appears in our [board games for 5-year-olds](/articles/board-games-for-5-year-olds/) list, because it keeps working for another year.

## A race where nobody falls far behind

### Snail's Pace Race

[Snail's Pace Race](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004KZ8P2Q?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) was designed by Alex Randolph and first published by Ravensburger in Germany in 1985 as Tempo, kleine Schnecke. It plays 2 to 6, rated 3 and up, in about 15 minutes. Roll two color dice and move the matching wooden snails. Whichever snail crosses the line first wins the race.

**Why it fits:** in the basic game the whole table cheers the snails on together, so nobody loses. Kids cheer for a favorite, learn their colors, and practice taking turns. An optional variant has each player pick a snail to root for, which is a soft first step into "my snail lost." It is a classic for good reason: it has been in print for four decades.

## Gentle competition: first games with a winner

When a four-year-old starts asking "who won?", these games bring in winning and losing in small, quick doses. Play them in sets of two or three so one loss is never the last word. If losing still ends in tears, our guide on [how to play games with a kid who hates losing](/articles/kid-who-hates-losing/) has a stage-by-stage plan.

### Pengoloo

[Pengoloo](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0014BDYDE?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) from Blue Orange Games plays 2 to 4, rated 4 and up, in 10 to 15 minutes. Wooden penguins hide colored eggs underneath them. Roll two color dice, then lift penguins to find eggs that match. Find a match and the penguin joins your iceberg. The first player with six penguins wins.

**Why it fits:** it is memory with a twist, and four-year-olds are often very good at memory games. That evens the table: a child can genuinely beat a parent without anyone holding back. Rounds are fast, so a loss turns into "again!" quickly.

### Chicken Cha Cha Cha

[Chicken Cha Cha Cha](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00806PI1W?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) was designed by Klaus Zoch and first published in Germany as Zicke Zacke Hühnerkacke. It plays 2 to 4, rated 4 and up, and won a special children's game prize from the Spiel des Jahres jury in 1998. Chickens race around a ring of egg tiles. On your turn, flip a face-down tile in the middle; if it matches the tile in front of your chicken, step forward and flip again. Catch up to another chicken and you can steal its tail feathers.

**Why it fits:** turns can chain, so one great memory streak feels thrilling. The feather stealing adds just enough drama. Rio Grande Games publishes the English edition, so it is easy to find.

### Spot It! Jr. Animals

[Spot It! Jr. Animals](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0075XNGGK?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) is the junior version of the matching game Spot It!. It plays 2 to 8, rated 4 and up, in about 15 minutes. Any two cards share exactly one animal. Spot it first, say its name, and grab the card.

**Why it fits:** it is a speed game, but four-year-olds are often faster than adults at spotting pictures. The cards show animals instead of abstract symbols, and there are several mini-games in the box. It travels in a small tin, which makes it the best pick here for restaurants and car trips.

### Animal Upon Animal

[Animal Upon Animal](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B073CD98D6?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) was designed by Klaus Miltenberger and first published by HABA in 2005. It plays 2 to 4, rated 4 and up, in about 15 minutes. Roll the die and stack one or two wooden animals on the shared pile. If the pile falls, you take some animals back. The first player to place all their animals wins.

**Why it fits:** stacking is a real skill, not luck, and four-year-olds take it very seriously. The penalty for a collapse is small, and a collapse is funny. Grown-ups get a genuine challenge too, which is rare at this age.

### Robot Turtles

[Robot Turtles](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00HN2BXUY?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) was designed by Dan Shapiro, launched on Kickstarter in 2013, and is now published by ThinkFun. It is rated 4 and up and plays 2 to 5. Kids lay down "forward," "turn left," and "turn right" cards to steer their turtle to a jewel. The grown-up acts as the computer and moves the turtle exactly as instructed.

**Why it fits:** it is a first taste of coding with no screen and no reading. The rules grow in steps, adding walls and shortcuts as kids get better, so the same box lasts for years. Note that it works best with one adult running it, so it is more of a guided activity than a game a four-year-old plays alone.

## Which board game should you buy first for a 4-year-old?

| If your child... | Get this | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Has never played a board game | First Orchard | Big pieces, one simple choice |
| Is starting to count | Count Your Chickens! | Counting is the whole game |
| Cannot sit still | Feed the Woozle | Walk, balance, and giggle |
| Loves picture books | Busytown: Eye Found It! | A huge board to search together |
| Is ready to make a choice | Hoot Owl Hoot! | Which owl helps the team most? |

| Loves building | Animal Upon Animal | Real skill, small penalty |
| Needs a game for a bag | Spot It! Jr. Animals | Small tin, many mini-games |

If you buy only two, make them Count Your Chickens! and Pengoloo: one cooperative game for the hard days, one gentle competitive game for the good ones.

## How do you teach a board game to a 4-year-old?

Skip the rulebook speech. Set the game up, then play the first turn out loud: "I rolled blue, so I find a blue fruit." Then hand over the die. Four-year-olds learn by copying.

- **Teach one rule at a time.** Play the first game with only the basic rule. Add the fox, the raven, or the scoring next time.
- **Let them do the setup.** Sorting pieces is half the fun at four, and it builds ownership of the game.
- **Stop while it is still fun.** If attention fades halfway, let the game end. Coming back tomorrow beats forcing a finish.
- **Play the same game a lot.** Repetition is the point at this age. The tenth game of Count Your Chickens! is where the counting clicks.

## FAQ

### What is the best board game for a 4-year-old?

Count Your Chickens! is the best first pick for most four-year-olds. It is cooperative, it teaches counting, and a game takes about 15 minutes.

### Can a 4-year-old play a competitive board game?

Yes, in short doses. Pick games with lots of luck or games where kids have an edge, like memory and spotting games, and play several quick rounds instead of one long one. Pengoloo and Spot It! Jr. Animals are good starts.

### Are cooperative games better for 4-year-olds?

For most four-year-olds, yes. Everyone wins or loses together, so a bad roll stings less and a grown-up can help without it looking like cheating. Mix in a competitive game now and then so losing becomes small and normal.

### How long should a board game be for a 4-year-old?

About 10 to 15 minutes. Short enough to play twice, and short enough to finish before attention runs out.
