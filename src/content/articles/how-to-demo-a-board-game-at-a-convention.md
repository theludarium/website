To demo a board game at a convention well, you need to do three things in order: get a stranger to stop, get them to sit, and get them to the first interesting decision inside ten minutes. Everything else, from the booth graphics to the sell sheet, exists to support those three moments.

This guide is for designers and small publishers running their first convention table, and for volunteers who have been handed a game and a stack of demo copies. It applies to Gen Con, Origins, and the dozen smaller regional shows around the Midwest where most new games get their first hundred plays.

## What is the goal of a demo?

Not a full game. The goal is for the player to understand what kind of decisions the game asks for and to feel one of them land. That is it.

Think of Codenames <!-- AFFILIATE -->. You do not need to play to 9 words. You need one clue, one guess, and one "oh, I see" when the guesser picks the wrong card. The whole design is in that moment. Most games have an equivalent: the first trade in Catan, the first bird that triggers a chain in Wingspan, the first outbreak in Pandemic.

Find that moment in your game. Build the demo to reach it fast.

## How do you get people to stop?

Convention aisles are a river. People drift past at a steady pace, scanning, with no intention of stopping anywhere in particular. You have about three seconds.

What works:

- **Stand, do not sit.** A person seated behind a table reads as closed. Stand at the aisle edge of your space.
- **Have a game in progress.** An active table with players laughing pulls more people than an empty table with a sign. If nobody is playing, get a friend to sit and play against you.
- **One sentence, aimed at a person.** Not "want to try a game?" Instead, "This is a two-player bluffing game that takes ten minutes. Want to lose to me?" Make eye contact with one person, not the crowd.
- **Show the prettiest component.** Hold it. Hands are magnets.

What does not work: a laminated rules summary, a looping video, or a question that can be answered "no thanks" without slowing down.

## How do you teach a game in ten minutes?

The teach is where most demos die. The instinct is to explain everything before play starts. Resist it.

A structure that works for almost any game:

1. **The hook (20 seconds).** What the game is about and how you win. "You're rival bakers. Most prestige at the end of five rounds wins."
2. **The turn (90 seconds).** The single thing a player does on their turn. Say it, then show it with real components. Skip the exceptions.
3. **The first decision (immediately).** Hand them the pieces and have them take a turn. Guide it. "You could take the flour or the sugar. Flour is safer, sugar is riskier. Your call."
4. **Rules on demand.** Introduce every other rule the first time it comes up, and not before. "Oh, and when you fill a row, this happens."
5. **The exit (at the interesting moment).** After the moment lands, stop. "That's the game. From here, it gets tighter every round. Full game takes about 40 minutes."

Prep a short script and say it the same way forty times. You will be bored by the third hour. The player is hearing it for the first time.

## How should the table be set up?

A demo table is a stage, and the audience is the aisle.

- **Pre-set the game to turn three.** Nobody wants to shuffle. Set up a mid-game state where the first decision matters, and reset to it between groups. Keep a photo of the setup on your phone.
- **Point the board at the aisle.** The open seat faces into the booth; the game faces out so passers-by can see it being played.
- **Reduce the component count.** For a demo, remove expansions, variant cards, and advanced modules. Fewer pieces, faster reset, less confusion.
- **Put the price and the sell sheet within reach but not in front.** They answer the question after the demo, not before.
- **Keep a reset kit.** Spare cards, a bag of each token type sorted, a small cloth to wipe the table. Reset time between demos should be under two minutes.

If you have two tables, run one with a full game among friends who can answer questions, and one for demos. The full game is the billboard.

## How do you handle the people problems?

Most demos are pleasant. A few patterns need a plan.

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| A player who wants to explain the rules back to you | Thank them and move on to the next turn. Do not debate. |
| A group where one person is interested and three are bored | Demo for the one person; invite the others to watch or wander. |
| A rules lawyer who finds an edge case | "Good question. The rulebook covers that; for the demo we'll keep it simple." Write the case down. |
| A fellow designer who wants to talk shop for twenty minutes | Offer to talk after hours. Your table is for players. |
| Someone who clearly will not buy | Demo anyway. They tell friends, and they post. |
| A child alone at the table | Ask where their adult is before starting. |

Be kind to the exhausted. By Saturday afternoon, everyone at a convention is running on coffee and goodwill.

## How do you survive a four-day show?

Demoing is a physical job. Plan like one.

- **Voice.** Speak from the chest, not the throat. Drink water constantly. Keep throat lozenges in your pocket. Consider a quieter demo on day three.
- **Feet.** A floor mat behind the table is the single best investment for a booth. Two pairs of shoes, alternated.
- **Shifts.** No one should demo more than three hours without a half-hour off the floor. If you are solo, schedule breaks as hard stops and put a "back at 2:30" sign on the table.
- **Food.** Eat a real breakfast. Keep protein in the booth. Lunch will not happen on time.
- **Count.** Track demos per hour and sales per demo with a tally sheet. By day two you will know which pitch line works and which hour is dead. Our [Gen Con survival guide](/articles/gen-con-survival-guide/) covers the rest of the body-maintenance list.

## What should you measure?

A demo table produces data if you let it. Count:

- Aisle stops per hour (people who paused)
- Sit-downs per hour (people who played)
- Sales or sign-ups per sit-down
- The question people ask most after the demo

The last one is the most useful. If everyone asks "how long is the full game?" it belongs in your hook. If everyone asks "is there a solo mode?" you have a product decision to make.

A newsletter sign-up sheet or QR code turns a "maybe later" into a reachable person. Follow up within a week, while they still remember the table. And if a publisher stops by and asks for a meeting, that is a different conversation, which we cover in [how to pitch a game to publishers at Gen Con](/articles/pitch-board-game-at-gen-con/).

## A demo-day checklist

- Game set to the mid-game state, reset photo on phone
- Reset kit: sorted tokens, spare cards, cloth
- Two-sentence hook and a 90-second turn explanation, practiced out loud
- Sell sheet, price sign, sign-up sheet or QR code
- Tally sheet and pen
- Water, lozenges, snacks, floor mat, second pair of shoes
- Break schedule written down

Conventions are the one place where a game gets played by strangers who owe you nothing. The feedback is honest because the stakes are low. Use it. If you are planning a circuit, our guide to [board game conventions in the Midwest](/articles/board-game-conventions-midwest/) lists the regional shows where a first table is cheaper and the aisle is slower.

The game is the reason. The people are the point. A demo that ends with a stranger smiling and knowing your game's name did its job, sale or no sale.

## FAQ

### How long should a convention board game demo take?

Aim for ten to fifteen minutes from sit-down to exit. Long enough to reach one meaningful decision, short enough that the next group does not walk away.

### Should I teach the full rules during a demo?

No. Teach the turn structure and the win condition, then introduce other rules only as they come up in play.

### How do I get a demo table at Gen Con?

Gen Con sells exhibitor booths and also runs [Entrepreneurs' Avenue](https://www.gencon.com/gen-con-indy/entave), a section of the Exhibit Hall for companies making their Gen Con debut, with a handful of marketing fellowships awarded to accepted applicants each year; application windows are posted on the [exhibitor pages](https://www.gencon.com/exhibits) and change each year. Smaller regional conventions often have cheaper or free designer tables.

### What if nobody stops at my table?

Start a game with a friend so the table looks alive, stand at the aisle edge, and pitch one person at a time with a specific sentence about the game.
