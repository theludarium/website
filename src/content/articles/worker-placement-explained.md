Worker placement board games give each player a handful of pieces, a shared board full of action spaces, and one hard rule: once a space is taken, nobody else can use it this round. That is the entire mechanic. Everything interesting about it comes from what that rule does to a table of people who all want the same thing at the same time.

Rather than survey a dozen titles, this article walks through one game in detail. Lords of Waterdeep is the best teaching example because it is clean, popular, and does nothing fancy. Once you can see the machinery there, you will recognize it in Agricola, Viticulture, Everdell, and most of the euro-game shelf.

## What is worker placement, in plain terms?

Picture a job board. Each morning a few jobs are posted. You send a worker to claim one, and it is yours for the day. Your neighbor wanted that job too. Too bad. They send their worker somewhere else.

In game form, the "jobs" are action spaces printed on the board. Each one gives you something: resources, cards, money, points, a special move. Players take turns placing one worker at a time. The round ends when everyone has placed all their workers. Then the workers come home and the board resets.

Three things define the genre:

1. A shared pool of actions.
2. Exclusive use. Taking a space blocks it for everyone else.
3. Workers as a scarce currency. You never have enough to do everything you want.

Point two is what separates worker placement from a simple action menu. In Wingspan, you can gain food on your turn no matter what your opponents did. In a worker placement game, the food space might be gone. That absence is the design.

## How does Lords of Waterdeep actually work?

Lords of Waterdeep is set in a fantasy city, but the theme is thin and that is fine for our purposes. Here is the structure.

Each player controls a hidden Lord with a secret preference for certain kinds of quests. Players have a small number of agents, which are the workers. Over eight rounds, you place agents on buildings around the city to collect adventurers in four colors, gold, quest cards, and intrigue cards. Adventurers and gold are spent to complete quests. Quests score points. At the end, your hidden Lord gives bonus points for the quest types they favor.

A typical turn is a single decision: pick an open building, place an agent, take what it gives. Some buildings hand out a specific color of adventurer. One gives gold. One lets you draw quests. One lets you buy and build new buildings that then belong to you and pay you a small reward whenever an opponent uses them. One gives you the first-player marker for next round, which matters more than it sounds.

That is it. No combat, no dice, no map movement. The game is about deciding which of several good spaces to take, knowing that whatever you do not take now may not be there when your next agent comes up.

## Where does the tension come from?

Four pressures stack on top of one another. Each is simple. Together they make every placement feel consequential.

**Scarcity of workers.** You have only a few agents per round, and in most player counts there are more desirable spaces than agents at the table. You will not get everything you need this round. You have to rank your wants.

**Blocking.** When an opponent takes the space that gives the adventurer color you need for your quest, your plan for the round changes immediately. Sometimes they took it because they needed it. Sometimes they took it because you did. Both are legitimate.

**Turn order.** The player who goes first each round gets first pick. In Lords of Waterdeep, you earn that privilege by spending an agent on it, which means giving up a resource now for position later. That trade is one of the most interesting recurring decisions in the game.

**Timing of quests.** Many quests need a specific mix of adventurers and gold. Holding partial sets is wasted capacity. You want to complete quests the same round you gather the last piece, which means reading what will still be available two or three placements from now.

None of these pressures requires a complicated rule. The complexity lives in the interaction between players, not in the rulebook. This is why worker placement games are often easier to teach than their reputation suggests. If you are hosting one for the first time, the [five-minute teach](/articles/how-to-teach-a-board-game/) approach works well: the goal is points from quests, a turn is place-an-agent-and-take-the-thing, and the exceptions are a short list.

## Why do designers love this mechanic?

Because it solves several design problems at once.

**It creates interaction without conflict.** Players affect each other constantly, but nobody attacks anyone. You cannot lose a piece or have your progress destroyed. You can only be denied. For many groups, especially mixed-experience groups, this is the right level of friction. The sting of being blocked is real but it rarely feels personal.

**It controls the pace.** Rounds have a fixed number of placements, so the game cannot drag indefinitely. Lords of Waterdeep runs eight rounds. Everyone knows when the end is coming, which sharpens the late game.

**It makes scarcity visible.** In many games, the resources players compete over are abstract. Here, the competition is physical. You can see your opponent's agent sitting on the space you wanted. That visibility makes the game legible to newcomers and gives experienced players something to read.

**It scales decision quality with player skill.** A new player can take whatever looks useful and have a fine time. An experienced player can count available spaces, infer what opponents need from the quests in front of them, and block with purpose. Both are playing the same rules. This is close to the pattern we describe in [luck sets the problem, skill solves it](/articles/luck-vs-skill-board-games/), with the random element here being the draw of quests and buildings rather than dice.

## What are the common variations?

Once you understand the base form, the variations are easy to spot. Designers tweak a handful of levers.

| Lever | Base version (Lords of Waterdeep) | Variation | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exclusivity | One worker per space | Spaces that hold several, or cost more for the second visitor | Caverna, Tzolk'in |
| Worker count | Fixed, grows slightly | Must be grown and fed, or workers have different strengths | Agricola, Raiders of the North Sea |
| Action availability | Same board every round | New spaces appear or rotate | Lords of Waterdeep's buildings, Everdell's seasons |
| Round structure | Fixed number of rounds | Variable end, or player-controlled season changes | Viticulture, Everdell |
| Feeding or upkeep | None | Workers must be fed each harvest or you take penalties | Agricola, Caverna |

Agricola deserves a sentence because it is the other game people name when they think of this mechanic. Agricola adds upkeep. Your workers are family members who must be fed at harvest time, and growing your family gives you more actions but more mouths. That one addition turns a placement puzzle into a survival puzzle. It is heavier and more punishing than Lords of Waterdeep, and for a first worker placement experience, most groups are better served by the lighter game.

## What does the mechanic feel like at the table?

The characteristic moment in a worker placement game is the pause before a placement. The player looks at the board, looks at their quests, looks at the agents their opponents still have in hand, and does a small forecast. "If I take gold now, will the fighters still be there when I come back around? Probably not. She needs fighters too." Then a decision, and a small reaction from the table when it lands.

That reaction is the social payload. A good block gets a groan. A well-timed first-player grab gets a nod. The game gives people a steady stream of small, shared moments without anyone having to be loud about it.

This is also why the genre is a reliable choice for groups that are still getting to know each other. The interaction is constant but it runs through the board, not through the people. Nobody has to bluff, negotiate, or confront. They just have to want the same space.

## Which worker placement game should a group start with?

Lords of Waterdeep, for the reasons above. It plays in about an hour with experienced players, teaches in ten minutes, and the decisions are visible without being overwhelming.

After that, the branches:

- Want a tighter, more punishing puzzle? Agricola.
- Want gorgeous production and a gentler curve? Everdell.
- Want a strong theme and a lighter touch? Viticulture.
- Want to see the mechanic bent in strange directions? Tzolk'in, where the board itself rotates.

## FAQ

### What is a worker placement board game?

A game where players take turns placing a limited number of pieces on shared action spaces, and each space can only be used by one player per round. The competition for spaces is the core of the game.

### Is Lords of Waterdeep good for beginners?

Yes. It is one of the cleanest introductions to the mechanic, plays in roughly an hour, and has minimal rules overhead beyond "place an agent, take the reward."

### How is worker placement different from action selection?

In action selection games, every player can usually take any action on their turn. In worker placement, taking a space blocks it for others, so the available actions shrink as the round goes on.

### Do worker placement games have direct conflict?

Rarely. The interaction comes from denial and timing rather than attacking. That makes the genre a good fit for mixed groups and for people who dislike confrontational games.
