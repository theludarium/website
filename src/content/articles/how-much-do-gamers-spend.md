Nobody talks about the number. You will hear gamers debate rules, rank publishers, and argue about whether a game is better at three or four players, but ask how much they spend on board games per year and the room goes quiet. That silence is a problem, because the number is the single most useful thing to know before you buy your next game.

This is the fourth article in The Math of Game Night, a Ludarium series about what game night really costs. The series pillar, [Cost Per Play, Explained](/articles/cost-per-play-explained/), covers the formula for a single game. This one zooms out to the whole year and asks the question most people never answer: what are you actually spending, and is it the right amount?

## What the survey spread looks like

Here is what we know about how much board gamers spend. Survey data on hobby spending shows that only about 6% of players spend under $100 per year on board games. About 22% spend over $1,000 per year. The large majority, something like seven in ten, fall somewhere in between.

A few things stand out in that spread.

- **The floor is low but rarely used.** Under $100 a year is two or three games. It is a perfectly good hobby at that level. Almost nobody stays there.
- **The ceiling is higher than most people admit.** More than one in five hobbyists spends more than $1,000 a year. That is at least 25 games at typical prices, or a dozen big ones.
- **The middle is wide.** Between $100 and $1,000 is a tenfold range. "Most people are in the middle" tells you very little about any individual person in it.

What the spread does not tell you is whether any of those bands is right. A $1,000 a year gamer who plays five nights a week with a big group is getting a bargain per hour. A $200 a year gamer whose games sit in shrink wrap is overspending. The number only means something next to the plays it produces.

## Why most gamers cannot say their number

The quiet in the room is not embarrassment, mostly. It is that nobody is counting. Games arrive one at a time: a $25 card game here, a $60 box there, a crowdfunding pledge that charged the card eight months ago. None of them feels like a big purchase. Added up over a year, they can be a mortgage payment.

A plan fixes this, and a plan does something different for each band.

### If you spend under $100 a year

A plan tells you which two or three games to buy so that each one covers a role your group lacks. At this level every purchase matters, and the worst outcome is spending your whole year on two games that fill the same slot. Follow the [play before you pay](/articles/play-before-you-pay/) approach and your handful of plays per game becomes dozens.

### If you spend $100 to $1,000 a year

A plan is the difference between a collection and a pile. In this band you are buying enough games that overlap, duplicates, and unplayed boxes start to show up without anyone deciding to buy them. A simple annual number plus a short list of roles to fill keeps the shelf coherent and keeps [the Shelf of Shame](/articles/shelf-of-shame-infinite-cost/) from growing.

### If you spend over $1,000 a year

A plan is what makes the spending defensible. At this level you are a curator whether you meant to be or not, and the question shifts from "what should I buy" to "what should I own." A collection cap, a one-in-one-out rule, and a trading habit turn a large budget into a great library instead of a large one.

The useful question is which tier you are in now, and whether your annual number is moving you to the next one or just adding width to the one you are in. Five or six well-chosen games cover most occasions. Past that point, every new game should be filling a gap, not repeating a role.

## How to set your own annual number

There is no correct figure. There is a correct method, and it takes about ten minutes.

1. **Count last year, truthfully.** Go through your purchases: retail, used, crowdfunding, expansions, convention hauls. Most people are surprised, and the surprise is the point.
2. **Count last year's hours.** Even roughly. How many sessions, how long, how many people. If you did not track, estimate from your calendar. [Tracking your plays](/articles/tracking-plays-changes-what-you-buy/) makes next year's version of this step easy.
3. **Divide dollars by hours.** One hobbyist's back-of-envelope landed at about $300 spent against about 300 hours played, roughly $1 per hour before dividing by the friends at the table. If your number is near that, your spending is in good shape. If it is several dollars per hour, your shelf has more boxes than your calendar has nights.
4. **Pick next year's number from the hours, not the wishlist.** If you play 100 hours a year, a $100 to $200 budget keeps you near a dollar an hour. If you play 400 hours, you can spend more and still be getting value. Set the number, write it down, and tell your group.
5. **Spend the number on roles, not titles.** Decide what the collection is missing before you decide what is exciting. A budget without a role list becomes a wishlist with a limit, which is better than nothing but not by much.

The number is a ceiling, not a target. A year in which you spend half your budget and play twice as much is a win, not a missed opportunity.

## How a shared shelf lowers everyone's number

Here is the part the survey cannot show you, because it asks individuals. The cheapest way to cut your annual spending is not to buy less. It is to buy as a group.

[The One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/) says a play group needs one copy of a game, not one per person. Follow it and the arithmetic changes immediately. Say four friends each spent $300 a year buying games separately, with the usual overlap: two of them own Catan, three own Codenames, and so on. The group spent $1,200 and owns perhaps 30 distinct games.

Now have each friend [claim a lane](/articles/claim-a-lane/) and buy only in their genre, with the games living wherever they are played. Each still spends $300. The group now owns about 40 distinct games with no duplicates, and each friend has access to all 40 for the price of 10. Or they each drop to $150 a year and still own more distinct games together than they did apart.

Either way, every person's effective cost per game, and per hour, falls, because the ÷ players step in the cost per play formula is doing its job across the whole library instead of one box at a time. That is the shared shelf: one collection spread across several homes, paid for by everyone and available to everyone. A Magic: The Gathering Cube is the purest version, one curated card pool that eight people draft from for years. [Two Groups, Two Years, Same Money](/articles/two-groups-two-years/) walks through a case study of what this looks like in practice.

So when you set your annual number, set it with your group in the room. The survey bands measure what individuals spend. What matters is what a table of friends gets for it. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### How much do board gamers spend per year?

Survey data shows only about 6% of board gamers spend under $100 per year and about 22% spend over $1,000 per year. The majority fall somewhere between $100 and $1,000. The figure varies widely with how often a person plays and how big their regular group is.

### How much should I spend on board games?

Set your number from your hours, not your wishlist. One common benchmark is about $1 spent per hour played; a hobbyist who spends about $300 and plays about 300 hours in a year is right on it. If you play 100 hours a year, a budget of $100 to $200 keeps you near that mark.

### Is $1,000 a year too much to spend on board games?

Not necessarily. About 22% of hobbyists spend over $1,000 a year. The question is plays, not dollars: a $1,000 collection played several nights a week with a group costs little per hour, while the same money spent on unplayed games is expensive. A collection cap and a trading habit keep it reasonable.

### What is the cheapest way to build a board game collection?

Play before you pay, buy one game per role, and share one copy of each game across your play group rather than everyone owning their own. Four friends who each collect a different genre can own a 40-game library for the price of 10 each, which lowers every person's annual number.

### How many board games do I actually need?

Five or six well-chosen games cover most occasions: a gateway game, a short filler, a two-player game, a party game for larger groups, a cooperative game, and one step-up game. Past that, each new game should fill a gap in your group's shelf rather than repeat a role you already have.
