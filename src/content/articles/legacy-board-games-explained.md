Legacy board games are games you permanently alter as you play. You tear up cards. You put stickers on the board. You open sealed boxes when the rules tell you to. After a dozen sessions, your copy is unlike anyone else's, and you cannot play it again from the start.

That idea offends some people and thrills others. This article explains how legacy games work, why designers took the risk, which ones are worth your group's time, and what it takes to finish one.

## What is a legacy board game?

The format was introduced by [Rob Daviau with Risk Legacy in 2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legacy_game). The pitch was simple: what if the consequences of a game carried over to the next one? If you won a game of Risk, you could name a continent. If a city got nuked, it stayed nuked.

The defining features are:

- **Permanent changes.** Cards are destroyed, components are marked, and the board is altered. Not "reset at the end," but gone.
- **A campaign arc.** The game is played as a series of sessions, usually ten to twenty, and the story advances with each.
- **Sealed content.** Boxes, envelopes, or decks are opened at scripted moments, introducing new rules and components you did not know existed.
- **A finite life.** When the campaign ends, so does that copy, more or less. Some games include a way to keep playing the final state.

Pandemic Legacy: Season 1, released in 2015, took the format mainstream. It paired the legacy idea with the most popular co-op of the previous decade and turned a forty-five-minute game into a year-long story.

## Why would anyone destroy a board game?

Because permanence creates stakes that nothing else can.

In a normal game, a bad outcome resets. You lost; you shuffle; you try again. In a legacy game, the city you failed to protect in March is still scarred in April. The character you let die is gone. The decision your group made about which cure to prioritize shapes what the next six sessions look like.

That weight changes how people play. Groups argue harder, celebrate louder, and remember the sessions years later. We wrote about why some game nights stick and most do not in our piece on [why game night memories last](/articles/why-game-night-memories-last/), and legacy games are a shortcut to that effect. The game remembers, so you do.

There is also a simpler pleasure. Opening a sealed box that says "Do not open until a player dies" is a thrill that no reset-able game can offer.

## How long does a legacy campaign take in real life?

Longer than the box says. A twelve-session campaign is twelve evenings, and most adult groups cannot meet every week. Expect three to six months for Pandemic Legacy and a year or more for Gloomhaven.

That is the real cost of legacy games. Not the money, but the commitment. A campaign needs the same people, in the same room, repeatedly. Life gets in the way. In our experience, the main reason legacy campaigns end unfinished is not boredom but scheduling.

We have a whole article on [what to do when someone misses a campaign session](/articles/campaign-game-missed-session/), and the short answer is: decide the policy before the first session, not when it happens.

## What should you agree on before session one?

A few conversations save a lot of grief later.

1. **Who is in.** Legacy games work best with a fixed group. Agree on the roster and whether substitutes are allowed.
2. **How often.** Pick a cadence and put the first four sessions on calendars before you open the box.
3. **Who keeps the box.** One home, one shelf. The box does not travel, because lost components in a legacy game cannot be replaced from another copy.
4. **Spoiler rules.** Nobody reads ahead, nobody looks up the campaign online, and nobody peeks in the sealed boxes. One leak flattens the whole experience.
5. **Decision rules.** Many legacy games ask the group to make permanent choices. Decide now whether it is majority vote, consensus, or rotating authority.

The decision-rules conversation matters more than people expect. A permanent choice made by the loudest player breeds a resentment that lasts the whole campaign. Making the choice together is part of the point.

## Can you replay a legacy game?

Mostly no, and that is by design. Some games include a reset or a way to keep playing the final board, and Gloomhaven's scenarios can be replayed even though the campaign choices are permanent. Charterstone ships with a recharge pack you can buy to play again.

For the pure legacy titles, the honest answer is that you are buying one long experience, not a reusable product. Measure the price against twelve to twenty evenings with friends, not against a game you will play forever. On that math, a legacy game is one of the cheapest things a group can do together.

Legacy games also opened the door for campaign games more broadly: titles like Gloomhaven and its heirs that tell long stories without destroying components. ## FAQ

### What was the first legacy board game?

Risk Legacy, designed by Rob Daviau and released in 2011, is widely credited as the first. Pandemic Legacy: Season 1 made the format popular four years later.

### Do you have to destroy the cards in a legacy game?

The rules tell you to, and the game assumes you did. Some players sleeve cards and set them aside instead, which preserves the components but does not change how the game plays.

### How many people do you need for a legacy game?

Most play with two to four. The real requirement is the same people every session, so pick a group that can commit.

### Are legacy games good for families?

Some are. My City and Machi Koro Legacy are short, light, and designed with families in mind. Pandemic Legacy and Gloomhaven are better suited to adults and older teens.

## Start a campaign, keep the people

The game is the reason. The people are the point. A legacy campaign is one of the best excuses adults have for seeing the same friends twelve times in a year.
