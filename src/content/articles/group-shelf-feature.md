Four friends who play together every week do not have four board game collections. They have one shared board game library that happens to be stored in four places. The trouble is that nobody can see it, so everyone keeps buying as if the other three shelves did not exist.

The group shelf is the fix. It is a single list that pools every member's games, flags duplicates, assigns lanes, answers "who has it?" in one lookup, and routes expansion wishlists to the person who owns the base game. This is the final article in our Library Science for Gamers series, which began with [organizing by role](/articles/organize-by-role-not-box-size/). The group shelf is where the whole series lands, because it is [The One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/) made visible: one copy, many players, and a list that proves it.

You can run a group shelf today with a shared spreadsheet, and we will give you the template. The Ludarium app, which is in development, is building the same thing as a feature. First, what the feature needs to do.

## What a group shelf does for a shared board game library

A group shelf has five jobs. If your list does all five, you have one, whatever tool it lives in.

### Pool every member's collection

Each person adds their games to the same list, tagged with their name. That is the foundation. The moment the list exists, the group can see that it owns forty games rather than four shelves of ten, and that number changes the conversation. "Should I buy Azul?" becomes "do we already have Azul?"

### Flag duplicates

Two members own the same game, and the list says so. This is the finding that pays for the whole exercise. Most groups discover two or three duplicates on the first pass, usually gateway games that everyone bought before the group existed. Each one is a candidate for the [second-copy test](/articles/when-a-second-copy-is-worth-it/): does the extra copy create plays that would not otherwise happen? If not, one copy gets traded, and the money comes back as a game nobody owns yet.

### Assign lanes

Each member takes a role or genre to collect: one person owns the party games, one owns the co-ops, one owns the heavy stuff. That is [Claim a Lane](/articles/claim-a-lane/), and the group shelf is where the lane assignment is written down so that nobody forgets who is buying what. A lane column also tells a newcomer which role is uncovered, which is the best possible answer to "what should I buy first?"

### Answer "who has it?"

Game night is at Sam's place and the group wants to play Wingspan. Who owns Wingspan? With a group shelf, that is a filter, not a group text. The same lookup handles loans: the list shows where a game lives right now, which is not always the same as who owns it.

### Route wishlists to base-game owners

Expansions are the best gifts in the hobby, as we argue in [gifting as library strategy](/articles/gifting-as-library-strategy/), but only if they go to the person who owns the base game. A group shelf with a wishlist column makes that automatic. When someone wants the Wingspan expansion, the wish attaches to whoever owns Wingspan. Birthday shopping solves itself.

## How to run a group shelf with a shared spreadsheet today

You need one spreadsheet that every member can edit. Any cloud spreadsheet works. One tab for the collection, one tab for wishlists, and that is the whole system.

### The collection tab

One row per copy of a game. If two people own Catan, Catan gets two rows, and the duplicate becomes obvious the moment you sort by title. Here is the template:

| Game | Owner | Role | Ludarium code | Players (real) | Minutes (real) | Lives at | Lane owner | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Catan | Sam | Gateway | G2 3-4 75 X | 3-4 | 75 | Sam | Sam | Keep |
| Catan | Priya | Gateway | G2 3-4 75 X | 3-4 | 75 | Priya | Sam | Trade candidate |
| Pandemic | Dev | Co-op | C2 2-4 45 A | 2-4 | 45 | Sam (on loan) | Dev | Keep |
| Codenames | Priya | Party | P1 4-8 15 T | 4-8 | 15 | Priya | Priya | Keep |
| Wingspan | Sam | Stretch | S3 2-4 90 X | 2-4 | 90 | Sam | Maria | Keep |
| Patchwork | Maria | 2-player | D2 2-2 30 X | 2 | 30 | Maria | Maria | Keep |
| Gloomhaven | Dev | Campaign | M5 2-4 120+ A | 3 | 150 | Dev | Dev | In progress |

A few notes on the columns:

- **Role** and **Ludarium code** come from the [classification system](/articles/ludarium-classification-system/). The code is optional, but it makes the sort-by-role step far more useful because it carries weight and length too.
- **Players (real)** and **Minutes (real)** are what your group experiences, not the box. If you keep a [play log](/articles/tracking-plays-changes-what-you-buy/), these columns come straight from it.
- **Lives at** is the current location, which changes when a game goes on loan. Update it when the box changes hands and the "who has it?" question is answered forever.
- **Lane owner** is the member responsible for that role going forward. It may differ from the game's owner, as with Wingspan above, where Sam bought it before Maria claimed the stretch lane.
- **Status** is where the group records decisions: Keep, Trade candidate, On loan, In progress, Retired.

### The wishlist tab

One row per wish. The key column is the base game, because that decides who the wish is routed to.

| Wish | Type | Base game | Routes to | Requested by | Fills a gap? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wingspan expansion | Expansion | Wingspan | Sam | Maria | No (depth) |
| A 2-player co-op | New game | none | Dev (co-op lane) | Priya | Yes (D + A) |
| Second Cube draft set | New game | none | Dev | Dev | No (one Cube serves eight) |

The "fills a gap?" column is the group's conscience. A wish that fills a role the shelf lacks goes to the top of the buying order. A wish that adds depth to a full role waits for a trade to make room. And a wish for a second copy of something the group already owns usually answers itself, as the Cube line shows: one curated Cube already serves eight drafters, which is the [best proof we have](/articles/cube-perfect-shared-library/) that one copy is enough.

### Running the audit

Twice a year, sort the collection tab by role and look at the results together. The [group shelf audit](/articles/audit-your-group-shelf/) article walks through the full process with a template, but the short version is: find duplicates, find empty roles, confirm lane owners, and clear the trade candidates. An hour over pizza does it. The spreadsheet makes the hour productive because nobody has to remember anything.

## Why a shared library is worth the bookkeeping

It is fair to ask whether a spreadsheet is really necessary for a group of friends. The answer depends on how much the group spends. Only about 6 percent of players spend under $100 a year on games, and about 22 percent spend over $1,000. Multiply the middle of that range by four members and a play group is easily putting a couple of thousand dollars a year into its collective shelf. A list that prevents even two duplicate purchases a year pays for itself many times over.

The bigger return is not financial. A group that can see its whole library plays differently. The quiet member whose shelf nobody knew about turns out to own the only co-op. The person who was about to buy a third party game buys a 2-player game instead and starts hosting Tuesday nights. The collection stops being four people's stuff and becomes the group's library, which is the thing we have been describing all along.

The game is the reason. The people are the point. The group shelf is just the list that lets the people find the game.

## FAQ

### What is a group shelf for board games?

A group shelf is a single list that pools every member of a play group's board game collection into one shared library. It shows who owns what, flags duplicate copies, records which member collects which role, tracks where each game currently lives, and routes expansion wishlists to the person who owns the base game.

### How do I track board games my friends own?

Create a shared spreadsheet with one row per copy of a game and columns for title, owner, role, real player count, real play time, current location, lane owner, and status. Sort by title to find duplicates and by role to find gaps. Add a second tab for wishlists, keyed by base game so each wish routes to its owner.

### How do I find duplicate board games across a group?

Put every member's games into one list with one row per copy, then sort by title. Any title that appears twice is a duplicate. For each one, ask whether the second copy creates plays that would not otherwise happen. If it does not, trade one copy for a game that fills a role the group lacks.

### Who should buy an expansion in a game group?

The person who owns the base game. Expansions only work attached to a base copy, so routing the wish to the base-game owner keeps the group from buying an expansion nobody can use. A wishlist column keyed by base game makes this automatic and turns expansions into easy, useful gifts.
