The cube mana curve is the single number set that most shapes how your games feel. A cube heavy at four and five mana plays slow, rewards ramp and removal, and makes aggressive decks impossible. A cube heavy at one and two plays fast, punishes stumbles, and makes six-drops look silly. Most first cubes are top-heavy without the owner noticing, because expensive cards are the exciting ones.

This piece gives target counts by mana value, explains why the two-drop slot carries more weight than any other, and shows how to measure your own curve in ten minutes.

## What does a healthy cube curve look like?

Here is the per-color distribution we use as a house guideline for a 360-card cube with 50 cards per color. It is close to what many well-drafted unpowered lists run, but every owner shifts it a few cards for their archetypes.

| Mana value | Cards per color | Share |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 7 to 9 | about 16% |
| 2 | 13 to 15 | about 28% |
| 3 | 11 to 13 | about 24% |
| 4 | 7 to 8 | about 15% |
| 5 | 4 to 5 | about 9% |
| 6+ | 3 to 4 | about 7% |

Two things stand out. Roughly 45 percent of each color costs two or less. And the slot at six-plus is small, under ten percent, because a draft deck wants one or two expensive cards, not five.

Gold cards skew higher in cost than mono cards, because they tend to be payoffs. Count them separately and keep the gold section's average mana value under three and a half. Colorless is where mana rocks live, so it skews toward two.

## Why do two-drops matter more than any other slot?

Three reasons, and they stack.

**Every deck plays them.** A control deck wants two-mana removal and rocks. An aggro deck wants two-mana creatures. A midrange deck wants both. The two-drop slot is the only one that every archetype draws from heavily, so its depth determines whether eight drafters can all build a deck with a functioning early game.

**They decide who is on the play.** In cube, the player who deploys a threat or an answer on turn two sets the tempo for the game. A cube where half the decks have nothing to do until turn three produces games that start late and end in a topdeck war.

**They are the pressure that keeps big spells honest.** Six-drops are fine in a cube with good two-drops, because a deck full of six-drops loses to a deck that curves out. Cut the two-drops and six-drops become free. The piece on [why aggro dies in most cubes](/articles/aggro-in-cube/) makes this argument from the aggro side; this is the same argument from the curve side.

The practical consequence: when you are short on cards for a color, the slot to protect is two mana. When you are cutting, the slot to cut from is five and six.

## How do first cubes go wrong on curve?

The pattern repeats across every first cube we have seen, including ours.

1. **Rares are expensive.** The cards that feel worth including are the splashy four-, five- and six-drops. Commons and uncommons supply the cheap cards, and a rare-heavy list drifts upward.
2. **Two-drops feel boring.** A 2/2 for two with a minor ability looks like filler on a spreadsheet. In a pack it is the glue.
3. **One-drops get cut first.** When an owner needs room, the 2/1 for one looks weakest. Cut three of them and aggro loses its opener.
4. **Gold is all payoffs.** If every gold card costs four or more, no two-color deck has a cheap reason to commit, and drafters stay open too long.

The fix for all four is the same: fill the curve top-down, not bottom-up. Choose the six-plus slot first, limit it to three or four per color, and discover you have lots of room below.

## How do you measure your cube's curve?

Export your list from CubeCobra, which shows mana value distribution by color on its analysis page, or do it by hand in ten minutes.

- Sort each color by mana value and count the piles.
- Compute each color's average mana value. For an unpowered cube, our house target is a per-color average a little under three; a color averaging much above that is top-heavy.
- Count cards at two or less. Below 40 percent of the color, the color will struggle to curve out.
- Count cards at five or more. Above 20 percent, the color is slow.

Do this per color, not for the whole cube. A cube can have a reasonable total curve while white is all one-drops and green is all five-drops. That is not balance; that is two broken colors averaging to a fine-looking number.

## Should different colors have different curves?

Yes, within limits. White and red should lean low, with ten or more one- and two-mana creatures each. Blue and black can run slightly higher, because their cheap slots include removal and card draw rather than just creatures. Green runs its two-drop slot heavy on mana creatures.

But no color should abandon the two-drop slot. A blue section with four two-drops is a blue section that only drafts as a control support color. If you want blue tempo to exist, blue needs cheap flyers and cheap interaction in the numbers above.

## How does cube size change the curve?

It does not change the percentages. A 540 cube with 75 cards per color should run the same shares: around 45 percent at two or less. What changes is that a bigger cube can afford a deeper six-plus slot in absolute terms, which is exactly where owners overshoot. Scale the top end by count, not by eagerness.

The percentages also hold for pauper and peasant cubes. Commons have plenty of good two-drops and few good six-drops, so a pauper cube's problem is usually the opposite: a thin top end that makes games stall into board states nobody can break. That cube wants its handful of six-drops to be real finishers, and it wants more removal, which we cover in [how much removal a cube needs](/articles/cube-removal-suite/).

## What should a drafter's curve look like?

Owners should know the deck target too, because the cube curve exists to produce it. A 40-card cube deck with 16 or 17 lands usually wants two to four one-drops, six to eight two-drops, four to six three-drops, two to four four-drops, and one to three at five or more. Count the two- and three-drop slots across eight such decks and you get 80 to 110 cards at two and three mana demanded from a 360 cube, which is most of what the cube holds at those costs. That demand is why the two-drop slot can never be deep enough.

## FAQ

### What is a good mana curve for a cube?

As a house guideline, about 45 percent of each color at two mana or less, about a quarter at three, and under ten percent at six or more. The per-color average mana value lands between about 2.6 and 3.0 in a typical unpowered cube.

### How many two-drops should a cube have?

Thirteen to fifteen per color in a 360-card cube, plus two-mana gold cards and mana rocks. Two-drops are the slot every archetype draws from, so it is the one to protect when space is tight.

### Why is my cube so slow?

Usually because the list is rare-heavy and rares skew expensive. Count cards at five or more mana per color; above 20 percent, the cube is top-heavy and games start late.

### Should gold cards follow the same curve?

They can run a little higher, since many are payoffs, but keep the gold section's average mana value under about 3.5 and include cheap gold cards so drafters have a reason to commit early.

## Protect the twos

Pick the top end first, cap it, and let the rest of the color be cheap. Your drafts will start on turn two and your six-drops will feel earned. The game is the reason. The people are the point.
