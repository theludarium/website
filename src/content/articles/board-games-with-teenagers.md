Most parents of teenagers want the same thing: a way to be in the same room, doing something together, where the conversation can happen without being forced. Board games for teenagers and parents work because they supply exactly that. The game gives you both a reason to sit down, a place to look, and something to talk about that is not grades or curfew. This article covers which games work, how to propose them without getting an eye roll, and what to expect once you are at the table.

## Why is it so hard to talk to a teenager?

It is not personal, even when it feels personal. Adolescence is the stage when kids are supposed to pull away from parents and toward peers. Developmental psychologists have described this for decades. The job of a teenager is to become a separate person, and separate people do not want to be interviewed about their day.

So the standard parental move, the direct question across the dinner table, runs straight into the thing teens are wired to resist. "How was school?" is an interrogation. "Fine" is the only safe answer.

What works instead is parallel activity. [Lisa Damour](https://drlisadamour.com/resource/how-do-i-get-my-teen-sons-to-talk-to-me/), a psychologist who writes about adolescents, has noted that direct questions put teens in the hot seat, and that they talk more freely when they do not have to look at you. Plenty of parents will recognize the pattern from the car, where nobody has to make eye contact and the conversation can stop at any moment without awkwardness. A game table has the same properties. Attention is on the board. Silences are normal. Talk comes and goes.

There is a longer-term reason to bother. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/healthyyouth/protective/youth-connectedness-important-protective-factor-for-health-well-being.htm) reports that teens who feel connected to their families and schools have lower rates of substance use, violence, and poor mental health, and that the protection lasts well into adulthood. That connection is not built in big talks. It is built in accumulated low-stakes time, which is precisely what a weekly game provides.

## What makes a game work with a teenager?

Not every game will survive contact with a 15-year-old. The ones that do tend to share a few traits.

**It is not a kids' game.** The fastest way to lose a teen is to pull out something they played at age eight. Candy Land is an insult. Catan is a peace offering.

**It has real decisions.** Teens are developing judgment and they want to use it. A game where their choices matter, where they can outthink you, is respectful in a way they will feel even if they never say so.

**They can win.** This is important. If you have been playing Ticket to Ride for 20 years, your teen will lose every time and stop playing. Pick games where experience matters less, or play cooperative games where you win or lose together.

**It fits in under an hour.** Attention is a finite resource, and so is their patience with you. A tight 30-minute game they enjoyed beats a three-hour epic they endured.

**It has some edge.** Bluffing, betrayal, and trash talk are features here. A game where your kid gets to lie to your face and get away with it is a game they will ask to play again.

## Which board games work best for teenagers and parents?

Here is a short list that reliably works across ages 13 to 18, grouped by what kind of evening you want.

| Game | Players | Time | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Codenames | 4+ | 15 min | Teams, quick laughs, mixed friends and family |
| The Crew | 3 to 5 | 20 min | Quiet cooperation, no arguing |
| Wavelength | 2+ | 30 min | Learning how your teen actually thinks |
| Skull | 3 to 6 | 15 min | Bluffing, nerve, almost no rules |
| Catan | 3 to 4 | 60 min | Negotiation, a little conflict |
| Azul | 2 to 4 | 30 min | Calm, pretty, surprisingly cutthroat |
| Pandemic | 2 to 4 | 45 min | Shared stakes, a real challenge |
| Coup | 3 to 6 | 15 min | Lying to each other and loving it |

Wavelength deserves a special mention. The game asks a player to give a clue that places a concept between two extremes, like "hot to cold" or "overrated to underrated." You learn more about how your teenager sees the world in one round than in a month of dinners.

If your teen is already into video games, do not treat that as competition. Many of the best tabletop games share DNA with what they play on a screen. Gloomhaven feels like a dungeon crawler. Dungeons & Dragons is the source code for half the games they love. Meet them there.

## How do you suggest it without getting an eye roll?

Delivery matters more than the game.

1. **Do not call it family game night.** That phrase carries a decade of baggage. Say "I got this game, want to try it?" and leave it there.
2. **Let them pick, or at least veto.** Hand them two options. Choice is respect.
3. **Invite their friend.** A teen who would never play with just you will play if a friend is there. The friend is also a buffer that makes conversation easier, not harder.
4. **Put your phone away first.** If you want their attention, model it. A [phone-free game night](/articles/phone-free-game-night/) is the fairest rule you can set, and it only works if it applies to you.
5. **Do not debrief.** When the game ends, do not say "that was nice, we should do this more." Just put the box away. Let them be the one to ask next time. They might not, for a while. Then they will.
6. **Lose well.** When they beat you, and they will, be pleased. Teens watch how adults handle losing more closely than adults realize. It teaches them [something they will need for the rest of their lives](/articles/how-to-be-a-good-loser/).

## What should you expect at the table?

Expect the first game to be a little stiff. Expect your teen to be on their best behavior in a slightly bored way, or to be aggressively competitive, or to go quiet. All of these are fine. You are not trying to have a breakthrough. You are trying to establish that this is a thing that happens.

Then expect the talk to come sideways. Somewhere around the third or fourth session, during setup or while someone counts points, your teen will mention something. A teacher they cannot stand. A friend who has been weird lately. Something about a kid at school. It will be brief and it will be offered casually.

The right move is to receive it casually. Ask one small question, or just say "huh, that sounds frustrating," and let the game resume. Do not stop everything and turn toward them. That is the interrogation posture, and it will close the door you just watched open.

Over months, these small moments add up to a teenager who knows they can tell you things. That is the whole point, and it cannot be rushed.

## What if they say no?

They might. Do not push. Leave the game visible. Play it with your partner or their younger sibling where they can see it. Curiosity is a powerful force in a bored teenager, and watching other people laugh over a game is a stronger invitation than anything you can say.

You can also go wider. A family game night that includes grandparents or cousins changes the dynamic completely, because your teen is no longer the center of your attention. Our guide to [games that work across generations](/articles/multigenerational-board-games/) has options for exactly that.

And if your teen is in a stretch where everything you suggest is wrong, that is a stage too. Keep the games on the shelf. Stages end.

## FAQ

### What are the best board games for a 14-year-old to play with parents?

Codenames, The Crew, Wavelength, Catan, and Coup all work well for early teens and adults together. Choose games with real decisions and avoid anything they played as a small child.

### How do you get a teenager to play board games with you?

Offer a specific game rather than "family game night," let them choose or veto, invite a friend of theirs, and put your own phone away. Do not make a big deal of it afterward.

### Why do teenagers talk more during activities than at dinner?

Side-by-side activities remove the pressure of eye contact and let conversation start and stop naturally. Teens tend to open up when they do not feel interviewed.

### How often should you play games with your teenager?

Once a week is ideal, but even twice a month builds the habit. Consistency matters far more than length.
