Most Wingspan strategy advice is about which birds are best. That is not why you keep losing by ten. You are losing by ten because your engine comes online one round too late, you treat eggs as an afterthought, and you spend round four doing the same thing you did in round one.

This guide is for the player who understands the rules, enjoys the game, and would like to stop finishing second to the same person. The fix is mostly about tempo. <!-- AFFILIATE -->

Rules are described in general terms for the base game. The expansions add birds and some new mechanics, but the shape of a winning game is the same.

## How does Wingspan actually score?

Start with where points come from, because the answer surprises people.

| Source | How it scores | Typical share of a winning score |
|---|---|---|
| Birds | Printed value on each card | About half |
| Eggs | One point per egg on your board | Often a fifth or more |
| Bonus cards | Per the card's condition | Variable, often five to fifteen |
| End-of-round goals | First, second, third place each round | Up to about twenty |
| Cached food and tucked cards | One point each | Depends on your birds |

Winning scores in a four-player game commonly land somewhere in the high double digits. If your birds score forty and the winner's birds score forty-five, the other ten points of the gap came from eggs, goals, and tucked cards, not from bird quality.

## Why does your engine come online too late?

Wingspan has four rounds. You get eight actions in round one, seven in round two, six in round three, and five in round four. That is twenty-six actions total, and the shrinking count is the whole design.

A bird played in round one triggers its power every time you take that habitat action for the rest of the game, potentially a dozen times. A bird played in round four triggers it once or twice. The value of an engine bird falls off a cliff as the game goes on.

So the early rounds should be spent building, not scoring:

- **Round one:** Play two or three birds, mostly in the forest and wetland, where their powers give you more food and more cards. Take food and card actions in between as needed. Resist the urge to lay eggs.
- **Round two:** Play more birds, now including the grassland. Your rows are getting longer, so each action gives more.
- **Round three:** Transition. Play your last high-value birds, start laying eggs where columns have egg capacity, pay attention to the round goal.
- **Round four:** Score. Lay eggs, tuck cards, cache food. Play a bird only if it is worth more than the eggs you would otherwise lay.

Players who lose by ten usually lay eggs in round one because they need eggs to play a bird in the second or third column. That is a real constraint. The answer is to play your first few birds in the leftmost columns across different habitats, where egg costs are zero or one, rather than stacking them in one row.

## Which habitat should you build first in Wingspan?

Forest first, most of the time. Food is the bottleneck in the opening. A forest with two birds gives you two food per action plus whatever the birds add, and food is what lets you play everything else.

Wetland second. Cards are the other bottleneck. A wetland that draws two or three cards per action lets you find the birds that fit your bonus cards and the round goals, instead of playing whatever you were dealt.

Grassland last, but not never. The grassland row is where eggs come from, and eggs are both a cost and a scoring source. Two or three birds in the grassland by round three turns the lay-eggs action into three or four points per turn, which is competitive with anything else you could do in round four.

A warning on the "forest first" rule: if your opening hand has strong grassland birds with egg-laying powers and the round-one goal rewards eggs in the grassland, follow the hand. The rule is a default, not a law.

## Are you ignoring eggs in Wingspan?

Probably. Eggs are the least glamorous part of the game and one of the largest sources of points.

Each bird has a nest capacity, usually two to six eggs. Every egg on your board at the end is one point. A board with eight birds averaging three eggs each has twenty-four egg points. That is more than most players' bonus cards and round goals combined.

Three ways to stop leaving egg points on the table:

1. **Prefer birds with high egg capacity when the other factors are close.** A three-point bird that holds five eggs is often worth more than a five-point bird that holds one.
2. **Plan round four as an egg round.** With five actions and a developed grassland, four lay-eggs actions can be worth fifteen to twenty points. Nothing else in the game pays that well that late.
3. **Watch nest types for the round goals and bonus cards.** Goals like "most eggs in bowl nests" are easy to win if you noticed them in round one and planted a bowl-nest bird in each habitat.

If you ever find yourself with a round-four action and nothing good to play, lay eggs. It is never a wasted turn.

## How should you pick a bonus card?

At setup you keep one of two bonus cards. Pick the one you can reach with the birds you are likely to see, not the one with the biggest payout.

Bonus cards fall into rough groups. Some reward bird types you can easily find, such as birds with a particular nest type or wingspan range, and those are reliable. Some reward narrow traits, like birds with a color or a body part in their name, and those are a gamble unless your opening hand already has two. A bonus card that scores five points reliably beats one that might score twelve and might score zero.

Birds that let you draw more bonus cards in play are worth more than their printed points suggest. A second bonus card scored at the end is a free five to eight points, and it is the most common way an experienced player pulls ahead quietly.

## How do you handle the end-of-round goals?

The four round goals are public from setup. Read them before you play your first bird.

- Pick the two goals you intend to win outright and ignore the other two. First place in a goal is worth considerably more than third, and spreading effort across all four tends to earn third in each.
- Goals that reward birds in a habitat are easiest to plan around, because you were going to play birds anyway.
- Goals that reward eggs or specific nest types are easiest to win in the round where they occur if you built toward them earlier.
- Check what your opponents are doing in round three. If someone has five birds in the wetland and the goal is wetland birds, let them have it and take second cheaply or skip it.

Wingspan rewards attention more than cleverness. The information is all face up. The player who reads the goals, reads the bonus cards, and counts turns usually wins, and that player is not always the one who knows the most birds.

## FAQ

### What is the most common Wingspan strategy mistake?

Laying eggs in round one and playing birds in round four. The game rewards the reverse: build the engine early while actions are plentiful, then convert it to eggs and tucked cards late.

### Which habitat is best in Wingspan?

The forest early, because food is the first bottleneck. By round three a developed grassland is often the strongest row because every lay-eggs action is worth several points.

### How many birds should you play in a game of Wingspan?

There is no published benchmark, but a competitive board usually fills most of its fifteen habitat slots without filling all of them. Far fewer birds than that usually means a weak engine, and a full board usually means too many actions spent playing low-value birds late.

### Are bonus cards worth chasing in Wingspan?

A bonus card you can reliably score is worth building toward. One that depends on drawing specific rare birds is not. Birds that draw extra bonus cards are worth more than their printed points.
