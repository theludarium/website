If you want to know how to win at Catan, start with the uncomfortable truth: most games are decided in the first five minutes, before anyone rolls. Your two starting settlements set your income for the whole game. Everything after that, trading, building, and development cards, is about making the most of what those first placements gave you.

This guide covers the three skills that separate players who win a third of the time from players who win once a year. None of it requires memorizing anything. It requires looking at the board a little longer than everyone else.

Rules are described in general terms for the standard four-player base game. Your edition may differ in small ways. <!-- AFFILIATE -->

## Why does starting placement matter so much in Catan?

Catan has no catch-up mechanic worth the name. The robber slows a leader down, but a player who starts with better income builds more, which earns more, which builds more. Your opening is the one moment when you choose your economy rather than inherit it.

Three things to look at when you place:

1. **Pips, not pictures.** Each number token represents a probability. Six and eight are rolled most often, then five and nine, then four and ten. Count the dots (pips) on the tokens around a spot. A settlement touching 6, 8, and 5 gives you roughly four times the income of one touching 2, 12, and 3.
2. **Diversity of resources.** The five resources are brick, lumber, wool, grain, and ore. A spot on three different resources with decent numbers usually beats a spot on two resources with slightly better numbers, because it keeps you out of the trading market early.
3. **Where your second settlement can go.** Your first placement is about income. Your second should fill the gap. If your first spot has no ore, your second needs ore or a path to it.

A common beginner mistake is to place both settlements on high numbers and end up with lumber and wool and nothing else. Lumber and wool build nothing on their own. You will spend the whole game begging for brick.

## What is the best Catan opening strategy?

There are two broad openings, and the board tells you which one to run.

**The road builder.** Brick and lumber on good numbers. You build roads and settlements fast, claim the best remaining spots, and usually take Longest Road. This wins when the board has open coastline and a few good third and fourth settlement sites that nobody else can reach.

**The city builder.** Ore and grain on good numbers. You turn settlements into cities early, which doubles your income from those hexes, and then buy development cards. This wins when the board is cramped and there is nowhere to expand anyway.

A rough comparison:

| | Road builder (brick, lumber) | City builder (ore, grain) |
|---|---|---|
| Early game | Fast expansion, more settlements | Slow, often stuck at two or three settlements |
| Mid game | Needs ore to convert to cities | Cities come online, income doubles |
| Points from | Settlements, Longest Road | Cities, Largest Army, hidden victory points |
| Weakness | Runs out of space; robber on the 6 brick hurts | Blocked in early; depends on card luck |
| Best when | Open board, coastal gaps | Cramped board, strong 6 and 8 on ore |

Most winning players run a hybrid: one placement for expansion, one for ore and grain. If you are new, pick a lane and commit. The player who dabbles in both usually builds the second-best version of each.

## How should you trade in Catan without helping the leader?

Trading is where Catan stops being a dice game and becomes a social one. The rules are simple: on your turn, you may trade with other players or with the bank at four of one resource for one of any other. Ports improve the bank rate to three-to-one or two-to-one for a specific resource.

A few principles that hold up:

- **Every trade helps two people.** The question is which one it helps more. If you are trading with the player in the lead, you are almost certainly helping them more, because they can convert resources into points faster than you.
- **Trade with the player in last.** They are the most willing, and giving them a card rarely decides the game.
- **Do not announce what you need.** "Anyone have brick?" tells the table you are one brick from a settlement. Ask "Does anyone want wool?" instead, and see who bites.
- **Use the bank more than you think.** Four-to-one feels terrible. It is often better than handing the leader the exact ore they needed for a city.
- **Ports are a strategy, not a bonus.** A settlement on a two-to-one wool port next to a decent wool hex turns the game's worst resource into a currency. Players who build around a port rarely need the table at all.

Late in the game, expect the table to stop trading with you if you are winning. That is not rudeness. That is the game working. Plan for it by stockpiling what you need two turns before you need it.

## When should you buy development cards?

Development cards cost one ore, one grain, and one wool. The deck contains Knights (the majority), five Victory Point cards, and a handful of Progress cards: Road Building, Year of Plenty, and Monopoly.

Buy them when:

- You have ore and grain income but nowhere to build. This is the city builder's natural second phase.
- Largest Army (three or more Knights played, worth two points) is unclaimed. Three Knights is cheap for two points compared with a city.
- You are holding resources at seven cards and a seven roll would cost you half of them. A development card converts three cards into a safe, hidden asset.

Do not buy them when you have a settlement available to build. A settlement is a guaranteed point and more income. A card is a one-in-five shot at a point.

One more thing about Knights: play them to move the robber off your best hex and onto the leader's. A Knight that moves the robber from your 8 to their 6 is worth more than the point it contributes to Largest Army.

## How do you read the table and manage the robber?

The robber is the only direct attack in the game, and the table uses it badly. Most players put it on whoever annoyed them last. Put it where it costs the most.

When you roll a seven or play a Knight:

1. Find the player closest to ten points. Not the one with the most visible points, the one with the most income. Count their cities and the pips they touch.
2. Block their highest-pip hex that you do not also touch.
3. Of the players touching that hex, steal from the one with the most cards in hand. They are the most likely to have what you need and the least able to complain.

Reading the table also means counting hidden points. If someone has bought four development cards and played one Knight, assume at least one of the other three is a Victory Point. A player who is "at eight" may be at nine. Do not give them the trade that gets them to ten.

## How do you close out a Catan game?

Games end at ten points, and the final two points are the hardest to get because the table is now watching you.

- **Hold your last point in hand.** If you have nine points and the resources for a settlement, consider waiting a turn if the spot is not at risk. Revealing nine invites the robber and a trade embargo.
- **Longest Road is fragile.** It can be stolen by one road. If you hold it at nine points, count every opponent's roads before you relax.
- **Victory Point cards are the quiet finish.** Buying a card at eight points that turns out to be a Victory Point ends the game on your next turn with no warning. This is why city builders win more than their boards suggest.

Catan rewards the player who is patient in the opening, generous with the wrong people in the middle, and quiet at the end. It also rewards the player who keeps the table happy, because an angry table trades against you. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### What is the single most important Catan tip?

Place your first settlement on three different resources with high-pip numbers, preferably including ore or brick. Income diversity in the opening prevents the mid-game resource drought that loses most games.

### Should you go for Longest Road or Largest Army?

Whichever the board makes cheap. Longest Road suits open boards with brick and lumber; Largest Army suits cramped boards where you have ore, grain, and wool and no room to expand.

### Is it better to build settlements or cities first?

Settlements first, as a rule, because each new settlement adds new hexes to your income. Switch to cities once you have three or four settlements or once you run out of legal spots.

### How do you deal with a player who never trades?

Build toward a port. A two-to-one port removes your dependence on the table and turns one plentiful resource into anything you need.
