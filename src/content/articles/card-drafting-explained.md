Card drafting is a way of dealing out cards where every player chooses what they get instead of taking what they are dealt. In the most common form, each player holds a hand, picks one card to keep, and passes the rest to a neighbor. Repeat until the cards run out. Everyone ends with a set they chose, built one decision at a time from hands that other people were also picking from.

This article explains how card drafting works, where it came from, the main variations you will meet, and the skill that separates a good drafter from a lucky one: reading what the people around you are taking.

## How does pick-and-pass drafting work?

The core loop is simple enough to explain in four steps.

1. Each player is dealt a hand of cards, usually between seven and fifteen.
2. Each player picks one card from their hand and sets it aside, face down.
3. Each player passes the remaining hand to the player on their left (or right).
4. Repeat steps two and three until the hands are empty.

That is the whole mechanic. What makes it interesting is step three. Every card you pass is a card someone else may take, and every hand you receive has already been picked over by the people who held it. Your first pick from a fresh hand is a free choice. Your fifth pick is a choice among leftovers, and the leftovers tell you what your neighbors wanted.

7 Wonders is the cleanest board game example. <!-- AFFILIATE --> Three ages, seven cards per hand, pick one and pass. The game has no other turns. Every decision you make is a draft pick, and the game has sold [more than two million copies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7_Wonders_(board_game)) on the strength of it.

## Where did card drafting come from?

The mechanic predates board games as a hobby. Sports leagues have drafted players for decades, and the word carries that sense: a structured way for competitors to take turns claiming from a shared pool.

In card games, the format was popularized by Magic: The Gathering's booster draft, which was already a [Pro Tour format by 1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Magic:_The_Gathering_Pro_Tour_events). Players open a fresh pack of fifteen cards, take one, and pass the rest around the table, opening three packs in total. The forty-card decks they build from those picks then play against each other. Booster draft became one of Magic's most popular formats because it made the game fair: nobody could buy their way to a better deck when everyone opened the same packs.

Board game designers borrowed the format once they saw how much decision it packed into a small space. [Fairy Tale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairy_Tale_(game)) (2005) is an early example, and [7 Wonders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7_Wonders_(board_game)) (2010) took it mainstream. Since then, drafting has shown up as a phase or a core engine in dozens of games.

## What are the other kinds of drafting?

Pick-and-pass is the most common, but it is not the only way to let players choose from a shared pool.

| Format | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Pick and pass | Take one, pass the hand to a neighbor | 7 Wonders, Sushi Go!, Magic booster draft |
| Open draft (market row) | Cards face up in a row; take one on your turn, row refills | Ticket to Ride's train cards, Splendor, Century: Spice Road |
| Rochester draft | All cards face up; players pick in snake order | Some Magic formats, Agricola's occupation draft variant |
| Winston draft | Two players take turns choosing from small face-down piles that grow until someone takes them | Two-player Magic |
| Dice drafting | Same as cards, but with rolled dice | Sagrada, Grand Austria Hotel |
| Draft and write | Draft from a shared pool, then write results on a personal sheet | Many roll-and-writes with a draft step |

The key difference is information. In pick-and-pass, you only see the hand in front of you, so you must infer what others are doing. In an open draft, everyone sees the same row, so the game becomes about timing: take the card you want now, or wait and hope it is still there.

Dice drafting deserves a mention because it fixes one of the oldest complaints about dice. Rolling a bad number hurts less when you get to pick from everyone's rolls instead of being stuck with your own. Sagrada and Grand Austria Hotel both do this well. <!-- AFFILIATE -->

## What does it mean to "read signals" in a draft?

This is the skill, and it is the part newcomers miss.

Every hand you receive in a pick-and-pass draft has been filtered by the players upstream. If the sixth hand you see still contains a strong blue card, the five people who held it before you did not want blue. That is a signal: blue is open on your side of the table. If every hand arrives stripped of green, someone upstream is taking green. That is also a signal: do not fight them for it.

Three habits make you a better reader.

**Notice what is late, not just what is good.** A strong card in a late hand is more informative than a strong card in an early hand. Early hands are full of good cards. Late hands with good cards are telling you something.

**Send signals on purpose.** When you pass a hand, you are informing the player downstream. If you take a card and pass three strong red cards, you have told them red is open. If you want red for yourself later, consider taking a weaker red card early to muddy that signal. This is where drafting becomes a game of its own.

**Commit at the right time.** Drafters who commit to a plan with their first pick fight everyone. Drafters who never commit end with a pile of unrelated cards. The usual advice: stay flexible for the first few picks, then commit hard once the signals agree.

Direction matters too. In 7 Wonders, hands pass left in the first age, right in the second, and left again in the third. The player who was downstream of you becomes upstream. What you denied them last age, they can deny you this one. Our [7 Wonders strategy guide](/articles/7-wonders-strategy/) covers how to use this.

## What is hate-drafting?

Hate-drafting is taking a card you do not need to keep an opponent from getting it. The name is more dramatic than the act. Most of the time it is just denial.

The question is always whether the card you deny is worth more to them than the best card you could take is worth to you. Early in a draft, almost never: there are too many picks left, and your own plan matters more. Late in a draft, when your remaining picks are weak anyway, denying a key card can be the best use of a turn.

In 7 Wonders specifically, the player on your left and right are the ones you compete with directly for military, so denying them a strong military card is often correct. Denying a player across the table matters less. Context decides.

## Why do designers like drafting so much?

A few reasons, all practical.

**Every turn is a decision.** No player waits for a good draw. You are always choosing.

**Turns are simultaneous.** Everyone picks at once, so a seven-player game of 7 Wonders finishes in thirty minutes. Few other mechanisms scale that well.

**Luck is shared.** A good card in the pool is available to someone. The question is who, not whether.

**Interaction without attacks.** You affect your neighbors constantly, but you never take anything they already have. For groups that dislike direct conflict, drafting is a gentle way to compete.

That last point is why drafting shows up so often in games meant for mixed groups. It gives the competitive players something to chew on and the casual players a simple instruction: pick the one you like.

## FAQ

### What is card drafting in board games?

A method of distributing cards where players choose from a shared pool, most often by taking one card from a hand and passing the rest to a neighbor.

### What is the easiest drafting game to learn?

Sushi Go! Each hand is small, the cards are clear, and a full game takes about fifteen minutes. <!-- AFFILIATE -->

### What is a booster draft?

Magic: The Gathering's version: players open packs, take one card, and pass the rest, then build decks from their picks. It is the format most hobbyists mean by "draft."

### Is Ticket to Ride a drafting game?

Partly. Taking train cards from the face-up row is an open draft, but the rest of the game is route building, so it is not usually classified as a drafting game.

## Pass to the left

A draft is a conversation held in cards. Every pick you make says something to the player beside you, and every hand you receive tells you what the players before you wanted. It rewards attention to other people more than almost any other mechanic in the hobby. The game is the reason. The people are the point.
