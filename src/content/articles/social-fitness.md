Social fitness is a simple idea with an uncomfortable implication. Relationships, like muscles, weaken when you stop using them, and most adults have been skipping the workout for years. The term comes from Robert Waldinger and Marc Schulz, the current directors of the Harvard Study of Adult Development, who used it to describe what their 85 years of data kept showing: the people who aged best were the ones who kept actively tending their relationships, not the ones who happened to have good ones at 30.

This article explains what social fitness means, what the research suggests about why it matters, and why a weekly game night is one of the best training programs available. It is not a replacement for a therapist or a doctor. It is a way of thinking about friendship that makes it easier to keep.

## What is social fitness, and where does the idea come from?

The [Harvard Study of Adult Development](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2023/02/work-out-daily-ok-but-how-socially-fit-are-you) began in 1938 with 724 participants: 268 Harvard sophomores and 456 young people from Boston's poorest neighborhoods. It has since expanded to roughly 1,300 of their descendants and is one of the longest studies of adult life ever run.

Its headline finding, which Waldinger has repeated in talks and in the book The Good Life, is that satisfaction in close relationships in midlife best predicted who would be healthy and happy at 80, a better predictor than cholesterol or blood pressure at 50.

The second finding is the one that leads to social fitness. Relationships don't maintain themselves. The study's participants who stayed connected did so through repeated small acts: calls, visits, standing plans. The ones who drifted did not usually have a falling-out. They just stopped. Waldinger and Schulz coined "social fitness" to make the point that connection is a practice, not a possession.

That framing changes what you do about it. You don't wait to feel like calling a friend any more than you wait to feel like going to the gym.

## Why does social fitness matter for physical health?

This is where the research gets striking, because the effects show up in the body, not just in mood.

Julianne Holt-Lunstad's [2010 meta-analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316), which pooled 148 studies covering more than 300,000 people, found that those with stronger social ties had about a 50 percent greater likelihood of survival over the study periods, an effect the authors described as comparable to quitting smoking and exceeding physical inactivity or obesity. A [2015 analysis](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691614568352/) by the same group found that social isolation, loneliness, and living alone were each associated with higher mortality risk, on the order of 26 to 32 percent higher.

The [U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf) drew on this body of work to describe loneliness as a public health concern, associating poor social relationships with a 29 percent higher risk of heart disease and a 32 percent higher risk of stroke, and chronic loneliness or isolation with roughly 50 percent higher dementia risk in older adults, more than double the odds of depression, and premature death.

The proposed mechanisms are still being studied, but researchers point to chronic stress. Loneliness appears to keep the body in a low-grade state of alert, with effects on sleep, inflammation, and blood pressure. Regular, trusted company seems to turn that down. The research suggests the relationship is bidirectional, but the direction from isolation to worse health is well supported.

So social fitness is not a soft concept. By the numbers, it is in the same conversation as exercise and diet.

## Why is friendship so hard to maintain as an adult?

Because adult life removes every structure that made it automatic.

School put you in a room with the same people every day. Early jobs and apartments did a version of the same thing. By your mid-thirties, most of those structures are gone. People move, have kids, change jobs. Friendship goes from a default to a project, and most adults never pick up the project.

Sociologists who study friendship point to a few conditions under which it forms: proximity, repeated unplanned interaction, and a setting that encourages people to let their guard down. Adult life has almost none of those by default. If you want them, you have to build them. <!-- EDITOR: could not reach a primary source for the Rebecca Adams attribution; softened to unattributed -->

Which is why the fitness metaphor is useful. Nobody expects to stay strong without a routine. Social fitness says the same about friendship: pick a routine, show up, repeat.

## How does a weekly game night train social fitness?

Look at what a good training program needs and compare it to a standing game night.

| Training principle | In the gym | At the game table |
|---|---|---|
| Regular schedule | Same days each week | Same night each week |
| Repetition | Sets and reps | Repeated contact with the same people |
| Progressive challenge | Heavier weight over time | Deeper conversations, harder games, shared history |
| Low barrier to showing up | Gym near home | Fixed place, game already chosen |
| Something to do | The exercise itself | The game itself |

The last row is the one most social advice misses. "Call a friend" is good advice that almost nobody follows, because an open-ended call has no structure. A game supplies the structure. You do not need something to talk about. You need to decide whether to take the blue tiles.

Games also give repeated unplanned interaction, which friendship researchers treat as the engine of the whole thing. Over six months of Thursdays, you learn how a person handles losing, what makes them laugh, when they're tired, when something is wrong. That is intimacy built sideways, which is how most adult men in particular are willing to build it. ## What does a social fitness routine look like in practice?

Keep it as plain as a workout plan.

1. **One standing weekly commitment with the same people.** This is the main lift. A game night, a run club, a dinner. Games have the lowest barrier and the most built-in structure.
2. **A few small reps during the week.** A text about the game. A link. A "you coming Thursday?" These are the warm-up sets. They keep the connection alive between sessions.
3. **One stretch goal a month.** Invite someone new. Have one real conversation past the surface. Reach out to someone you've drifted from.
4. **Track it loosely.** Waldinger suggests a simple inventory: who are the people you'd call at 3 a.m., and when did you last see them? If the answer is "months," that's a muscle you've stopped training.
5. **Expect it to feel effortful at first.** Like any training, the first few weeks are the hardest. Social fitness compounds; the tenth Thursday is easy in a way the first is not.

## FAQ

### What does social fitness mean?

Social fitness is the practice of actively maintaining your relationships through regular effort, the way you maintain physical fitness through exercise. The term was popularized by Robert Waldinger and Marc Schulz of the Harvard Study of Adult Development.

### What did the Harvard Study of Adult Development find?

Since 1938, the study has found that the quality of people's close relationships at midlife predicted their health in old age better than their cholesterol or blood pressure did, and that close relationships mattered more to a good life than money or fame.

### How often should I see friends for social fitness?

There is no established dose, but the research on habits and the Harvard study's own findings favor regular, repeated contact. A weekly standing commitment with the same small group is a realistic and effective baseline.

### Can board games really count as social fitness?

Yes. A weekly game night provides fixed people, a fixed time, repeated interaction, and a built-in activity, which covers the main conditions researchers associate with forming and keeping friendships.
