Lovecraftian board games are the one corner of the hobby where losing is part of the fun. You play ordinary people in the 1920s who stumble onto something ancient and enormous, and the game is honest about your odds. Sometimes you seal the gate. Sometimes the sky opens and the thing behind it wakes up. Either way, everyone at the table remembers the night.

There are a lot of these games. By our count, more than 350 Lovecraftian board and card games exist, and most of them came out after 2010. This guide picks the 15 worth your time and answers the questions that matter at the table: what makes each one fun, whether you play together or against each other, and how hard the monsters are to beat.

**The quick answer:** for a first Lovecraftian game, play Pandemic: Reign of Cthulhu or Elder Sign. For a story you play across many nights, Arkham Horror: The Card Game. For one big, dramatic evening, Eldritch Horror or Cthulhu: Death May Die. If your group would rather play the monsters, Cthulhu Wars.

## Why Lovecraft works so well on a game table

H.P. Lovecraft wrote horror stories for pulp magazines in the 1920s and 1930s. His big idea was cosmic horror: the universe is vast, it does not care about us, and some of what lives in it would end the world by simply waking up. His heroes are librarians, professors, and sailors, not warriors, and many of them do not make it to the last page.

That turns out to be a great fit for games, for four reasons.

1. **Losing feels right.** In most games a loss is a letdown. In a Lovecraftian game, a loss is the story working as written. Groups laugh about the Ancient One that ate the world.
2. **Sanity is a resource.** Nearly every game here tracks your character's grip on reality. You spend it, risk it, and sometimes lose it, and losing it often changes how your character plays instead of just knocking you out.
3. **Investigators are ordinary people.** You are not a superhero. You are a scared scholar with a lantern and a hunch, which makes every small win feel earned.
4. **The monsters are shared.** Lovecraft encouraged his writer friends to borrow his creatures, and game makers have been borrowing them ever since. Cthulhu, Nyarlathotep, Shub-Niggurath, and the town of Arkham, Massachusetts show up again and again, so learning one of these games gives you a head start on the rest.

A little history: the tabletop roleplaying game Call of Cthulhu came out in 1981, and the board game Arkham Horror followed in 1987. Fantasy Flight Games rebuilt Arkham Horror in 2005, and that edition kicked off the boom. Today the theme spans co-op epics, quick card games, party games, and miniatures battles.

## Big cooperative games for a long night or a long campaign

These are the games people mean when they say "Lovecraftian board game." Everyone plays on the same team against the board, and they reward groups who like a big story.

### Arkham Horror: The Card Game

<AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GK349WJ7?tag=cubecommonw0a-20">Arkham Horror: The Card Game</AffiliateLink> is a horror story told across a campaign of linked scenarios. Each player has their own deck for their investigator, and you play through a mystery where every choice carries forward. Find a clue, save a friend, or read the forbidden book, and the next scenario changes because of it.

**What makes it fun:** the chaos bag. Instead of rolling dice, you pull a token from a bag to see whether a test succeeds. The bag holds numbers and symbols, and the campaign adds nastier tokens as things go wrong. You always know your odds, and you still hold your breath on every pull. Characters also gain scars that last the whole campaign, so the story you live through is yours.

**How hard are the monsters?** Hard. Scenarios can be lost, and the campaign keeps going when they are, with consequences. You can pick an easier or harder setting by changing what goes in the bag.

**Best for:** one or two players who want a story to come back to every week. It is excellent solo. Our free [Arkham Horror: The Card Game course](/academy/arkham-horror-lcg/) teaches it from the first scenario up.

### Eldritch Horror

In <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/1616617667?tag=cubecommonw0a-20">Eldritch Horror</AffiliateLink>, the whole world is the board. Your investigators travel from Arkham to Istanbul to Tokyo by train and ship, closing gates and chasing clues, while one Ancient One tries to wake. You win by solving three mysteries before the Doom track runs out.

**What makes it fun:** the encounters. Every location has short story cards that read like scenes from a pulp novel, and each one asks you to test a skill. Your character might come home with a rare tome, a new friend, or amnesia. Each Ancient One also brings its own mysteries, so fighting Cthulhu feels nothing like fighting Azathoth.

**How hard are the monsters?** Hard. The game throws a lot at you, and a bad run of cards can end it early. Most groups lose often at first and win more as they learn which threats to ignore.

**Best for:** groups of three to five who want a globe-trotting adventure and do not mind a long evening.

### Arkham Horror (Third Edition)

<AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07G3Y95R9?tag=cubecommonw0a-20">Arkham Horror (Third Edition)</AffiliateLink> keeps the action inside one town. Each scenario builds Arkham from neighborhood tiles, and doom spreads street by street while your investigators race to stop it.

**What makes it fun:** each scenario plays like a chapter of a story, with its own goal and its own surprises read from a card deck as you go. The headline cards, little newspaper stories drawn each round, make the town feel alive. It plays faster and tighter than the 2005 edition, which was famous for long sessions.

**How hard are the monsters?** Hard. Doom builds fast if you let it, and splitting up to cover the town is both necessary and dangerous.

**Best for:** groups who love Arkham itself and want a story that lives on one map.

### Mansions of Madness: Second Edition

<AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01J4NB6CO?tag=cubecommonw0a-20">Mansions of Madness: Second Edition</AffiliateLink> runs on a free app. The app builds the haunted house room by room as you explore, controls the monsters, and decides what happens when you open a drawer you should have left closed.

**What makes it fun:** surprise. Because the app holds the secrets, nobody at the table knows what is behind the next door, including the person who set up the game. It also hides small puzzles in the scenarios, like rearranging tiles or matching symbols to pick a lock, which gives your brain something to do besides fight.

**How hard are the monsters?** Fair. Scenarios are tense but beatable, and the app tells a good story whether you win or lose.

**Best for:** groups who want the feel of a horror movie without anyone having to run the game.

## Cooperative games you can finish in one evening

These keep the teamwork and the dread but fit in a normal game night.

### Cthulhu: Death May Die

<AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07XJDYR1T?tag=cubecommonw0a-20">Cthulhu: Death May Die</AffiliateLink> has a premise that makes people grin: your investigators are not trying to stop the ritual. They are going to let the Elder One arrive, and then kill it.

**What makes it fun:** going insane makes you stronger. As your character's sanity slips, they unlock more of their abilities, so you are tempted to push deeper into madness for power. Lose one investigator to death or madness before the Elder One arrives, though, and the whole team loses. Each game also pairs a scenario with an Elder One, so the mix keeps changing. There is no campaign to track, which makes it easy to pull off the shelf.

**How hard are the monsters?** Fair, with big swings. The custom dice can turn a fight in one roll, for you or against you.

**Best for:** groups who want action, big monsters, and a fight they can finish in two hours.

### Pandemic: Reign of Cthulhu

<AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01DN2BM9O?tag=cubecommonw0a-20">Pandemic: Reign of Cthulhu</AffiliateLink> takes the famous co-op game Pandemic and swaps disease for cultists. You travel between Arkham, Innsmouth, Dunwich, and Kingsport, sealing four gates before the Old Ones wake one by one.

**What makes it fun:** it is the friendliest door into the theme. If you have played Pandemic, you can play this in minutes, and if you have not, it teaches itself fast. The twist is sanity: lose it, and your investigator flips to an insane side with a changed, often awkward, ability. Shoggoths wander toward the gates, and every Old One that wakes changes a rule for the rest of the game.

**How hard are the monsters?** Fair. Wins are common once the group plans together, but Cthulhu waking up is always one bad draw away. New to Pandemic? Start with [our explainer](/articles/pandemic-board-game-explained/).

**Best for:** families with teens and anyone new to the theme.

### Elder Sign

<AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/1616611359?tag=cubecommonw0a-20">Elder Sign</AffiliateLink> is a dice game set in a museum full of strange exhibits. Each room is a card with a row of tasks, and you roll special dice to match the symbols, a little like Yahtzee with tentacles. Finish a room and you earn its reward, maybe an Elder Sign, the seal that keeps the Ancient One asleep.

**What makes it fun:** every roll is a small gamble. Fail to match a task and you lose a die and roll again, so you are always deciding whether to push your luck. A clock ticks each turn, and when it strikes midnight, something bad happens.

**How hard are the monsters?** Swingy. Some games are easy wins and some end in a few unlucky rolls. That is part of the charm, and why it works as a lighter game.

**Best for:** a short horror fix for any group size, and players who like dice more than rules.

### Arkham Horror: Final Hour

<AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07WVNP1K2?tag=cubecommonw0a-20">Arkham Horror: Final Hour</AffiliateLink> starts at the end. The cultists have already finished their ritual, monsters are pouring onto the Miskatonic University campus, and you have a handful of rounds to find the components to reverse it.

**What makes it fun:** you cannot talk strategy. Each round, players lay down numbered priority cards, and those cards are the only way to signal what you want to do. The team then carries out the actions those cards pick. It feels like a frantic, wordless plan in the dark, which suits the theme perfectly.

**How hard are the monsters?** Hard. Monsters arrive every round, and a team that miscommunicates gets overrun.

**Best for:** groups who like co-op puzzles with a twist, like The Crew or Hanabi, and want a horror version in about an hour.

### Mountains of Madness

<AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B071VP8CCZ?tag=cubecommonw0a-20">Mountains of Madness</AffiliateLink> sends your expedition up an Antarctic mountain in a small plane, based on Lovecraft's novella of the same name. Each step, the team has a short timer to pool equipment cards and beat the challenge in front of them.

**What makes it fun:** madness cards change how you are allowed to talk. Fail a challenge or grab a relic and you might have to yell everything, whisper, sing, or speak in rhyme, all while the team tries to plan inside a 30-second sand timer. The madness gets worse the higher you climb. It turns a horror game into a party game, and it is often the loudest game of the night.

**How hard are the monsters?** Fair, and loud. The real enemy is your own team's growing list of strange rules.

**Best for:** groups of three to five who like laughing more than planning.

## Games where you play against each other

Not every group wants to win together. These put you in competition, sometimes as the monsters themselves.

### Cthulhu Wars

In <AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00NT5AI8C?tag=cubecommonw0a-20">Cthulhu Wars</AffiliateLink>, you are the monsters. Each player leads a faction of Great Old Ones and their cultists, fighting over a ruined Earth. You summon huge creatures, open gates, and race to end the world first.

**What makes it fun:** every faction plays by different rules. Great Cthulhu can sink beneath the waves and rise somewhere else, while other factions spread, swarm, or bargain their way across the map. You unlock powerful spellbooks by doing faction-specific tasks, so each game becomes a story of who grew strongest and when. The miniatures are enormous, and setting a towering Cthulhu on the map is half the thrill.

**How hard are the monsters?** You are the monsters. It is a competitive game of area control, so the challenge is the other players.

**Best for:** groups who love strategy games and want to be the villain for once.

### Unfathomable

<AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B096KW4QWX?tag=cubecommonw0a-20">Unfathomable</AffiliateLink> takes place on a steamship crossing the Atlantic in 1913. Deep Ones are climbing aboard, the ship is falling apart, and one or more of the passengers at your table may be secretly working for the monsters.

**What makes it fun:** the paranoia. When the group faces a crisis, everyone adds cards face down to solve it, and a hidden traitor can quietly sink the effort. You will argue about who played the bad cards, accuse your friends, and sometimes throw an innocent person in the brig. It is built on the same engine as the classic hidden-traitor game Battlestar Galactica, with a creepier coat of paint.

**How hard are the monsters?** It depends on the traitor. A clever hybrid can win games the humans thought were safe. If you like games that mix teamwork with a betrayal, see our guide to [semi-cooperative board games](/articles/semi-cooperative-board-games/).

**Best for:** groups of four to six who enjoy bluffing and do not mind a long evening.

## Short and light Lovecraftian games

These are quick to teach and easy to pull out after dinner. They lean into the theme with a wink.

### Lovecraft Letter

<AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B06Y49YR9S?tag=cubecommonw0a-20">Lovecraft Letter</AffiliateLink> is a Lovecraft version of Love Letter, the tiny card game where you hold one card, draw one, and play one, trying to be the last player standing in the round.

**What makes it fun:** the insanity cards. They are more powerful than the normal cards, but once you have played one, you must pass a sanity check at the start of each turn. Draw another insanity card during that check and you are out. You can also win a round while mad, and the game rewards that differently. It adds a delicious push-your-luck layer to a game that fits in a pocket.

**Best for:** a 20-minute filler for up to six players.

### Tides of Madness

Tides of Madness is a two-player card game from Portal Games. You draft cards back and forth over three rounds, building sets that score points, like a tiny version of a drafting game such as 7 Wonders.

**What makes it fun:** madness is both a tool and a threat. Some of the strongest cards carry madness symbols. Collecting them helps you score, but each one pushes you closer to the edge, and too much madness ends the game for you on the spot. Every hand becomes a question of how close you dare to go.

**Best for:** couples and pairs who want a quick, clever duel. For more ideas, see our [best two-player board games for date night](/articles/two-player-board-games-date-night/).

### Cthulhu Gloom

<AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/1589781244?tag=cubecommonw0a-20">Cthulhu Gloom</AffiliateLink> flips the usual goal. You want your own family of characters to suffer as much as possible before they meet their doom, while you cheer up your opponents' characters to ruin their score.

**What makes it fun:** the cards are clear plastic. You stack them on top of each other, and the numbers and text that show through change as the stack grows. The real fun is the storytelling: when you play a card, you explain how it happened to your character, and each new misfortune has to follow from the last. The best stories get the biggest laughs.

**Best for:** groups who like dark humor and telling stories.

### Smash Up: The Obligatory Cthulhu Set

<AffiliateLink href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00E3S8IZ4?tag=cubecommonw0a-20">Smash Up: The Obligatory Cthulhu Set</AffiliateLink> is a standalone box for Smash Up, the game where you shuffle two factions together, like pirates and ninjas, and fight over bases. This set adds Cthulhu cultists, Elder Things, Innsmouth, and Miskatonic University.

**What makes it fun:** you get to field a deck of Innsmouth townsfolk mixed with, say, dinosaurs from another box. The set also adds Madness cards that give you a quick boost when you play them, but every two still in your hand, deck, or discard pile at the end cost you a point. It is silly in the best way.

**Best for:** fans of Smash Up and anyone who wants Lovecraft without the dread.

## How to choose your first Lovecraftian board game

Start with three questions.

**Do you want to play together or against each other?** Most Lovecraftian games are cooperative. If your group likes competition, look at Cthulhu Wars, Lovecraft Letter, Tides of Madness, Cthulhu Gloom, or Smash Up. If you want a bit of both, Unfathomable.

**How much time do you have?** Under 30 minutes: Lovecraft Letter or Tides of Madness. About an hour: Pandemic: Reign of Cthulhu, Elder Sign, Final Hour, or Mountains of Madness. A full evening: Eldritch Horror, Mansions of Madness, Death May Die, or Arkham Horror. Many evenings: Arkham Horror: The Card Game.

**How much do you want to lose?** If losing a lot sounds fun, Eldritch Horror and Final Hour will happily oblige. If you want better odds while you learn, Pandemic: Reign of Cthulhu or Mansions of Madness.

## Lovecraftian board game FAQs

### Do I need to read Lovecraft before playing?

No. Every game here explains what you need to know as you play. Reading a few stories does add to the fun, though. "The Call of Cthulhu," "The Shadow over Innsmouth," and "At the Mountains of Madness" are good places to start, and you will recognize places and monsters from all three.

### Are these games scary?

They are creepy more than scary. Expect tentacles, cultists, and dark art, not jump scares. Most are rated for ages 14 and up, mostly for theme and complexity.

### Can kids play Lovecraftian board games?

Some can. Pandemic: Reign of Cthulhu and Elder Sign suit many younger teens, and Smash Up and Lovecraft Letter are light enough for families. Check the art first if you have a sensitive reader.

### What is the best Lovecraftian board game to play solo?

Arkham Horror: The Card Game is the strongest solo game here. Elder Sign, Eldritch Horror, Mansions of Madness, and Cthulhu: Death May Die all play well alone, too.

### What does sanity do in these games?

It is your character's mental health, and it works like a second health bar. Horror, spells, and strange sights drain it. In some games running out knocks your character out, and in others, like Death May Die and Pandemic: Reign of Cthulhu, losing your grip changes how you play instead.

### Why does Arkham show up in so many games?

Arkham, Massachusetts is the fictional town where many of Lovecraft's stories happen, home of the even more fictional Miskatonic University. Fantasy Flight Games built a whole shared world on it, which is why Arkham Horror, Eldritch Horror, Mansions of Madness, Elder Sign, and Final Hour share the same investigators.

### Is there a Lovecraftian tabletop roleplaying game?

Yes. Call of Cthulhu, first published in 1981, is one of the most popular tabletop RPGs ever made. If you like the stories these board games tell, it is the next step. Our guide to [what a tabletop RPG is](/articles/what-is-a-tabletop-rpg/) explains how they work.

Whichever you choose, set the lights low, pour something warm, and agree before you start that the Ancient One gets the last laugh sometimes. The game is the reason. The people are the point.
