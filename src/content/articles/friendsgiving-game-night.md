A Friendsgiving game night works best with three short acts: a quick opener while people are still at the table, one main event once the plates are cleared, and a loud closer before everyone heads home. For six to twelve adults, the safest picks are team and party games that teach in two minutes and do not care if someone gets up for pie.

Friendsgiving is a different crowd than family Thanksgiving. Everyone is an adult, most people know each other, and the group is often bigger than any game box expects. That gives you more room to be clever, a little competitive, and a little rude.

**The quick answer:** open with [Herd Mentality](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B093HBBMPT?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) or [Just One](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07GJ7NDZQ?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), make [Wavelength](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07T446163?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) or [Codenames](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B014Q1XX9S?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) the main event, and close with [Telestrations](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0F699VTB2?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) or [Skull](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08R2BPLSN?tag=cubecommonw0a-20). If the group is seven or more and cannot agree, let them vote from their phones with [Game Night Madness](/game-night-madness/).

## What makes a good Friendsgiving game?

Friendsgiving has its own shape. The host has been cooking since noon. Guests arrive in waves. Someone brings a plus-one nobody has met. Dinner runs long, and by the time the table is cleared, the room is full, happy, and a bit slow.

A game that fits the night does four things:

- **Seats the real headcount.** Six to twelve is common. Four-player games split the room. Party and team games keep everyone in one circle.
- **Teaches in two minutes.** The host should not spend dessert reading a rulebook out loud.
- **Lets people come and go.** Short rounds mean nobody is trapped when the dishwasher needs loading.
- **Gives the new person a way in.** Team games and cooperative games let the plus-one belong in the first round instead of the third.

Our list of [party games for large groups](/articles/party-games-for-large-groups/) covers the wider field. This piece narrows it to one evening and one order.

## How should you plan the night: opener, main event, closer?

The best Friendsgiving game nights follow the same arc as any good [game night lineup](/articles/game-night-lineup/): an opener, a main event, a closer. Each one has a different job.

**The opener** happens at the table, often before dessert. It should need almost no space and almost no rules. Its job is to get people laughing together, including the ones who do not think of themselves as game people.

**The main event** comes after a break. Clear the table, refill drinks, and give the room fifteen minutes to digest. This is the game with the most decisions and the most arguing, and it should run thirty to sixty minutes.

**The closer** is short and loud. It catches the people who stayed late and gives them one more story to take home. If the main event ran long, skip it. Nobody will mind.

## What should you play at Friendsgiving, by group size?

Group size changes everything. Here is a plan for the three most common Friendsgiving counts.

| 8 to 10 people | Herd Mentality | Codenames or Wavelength | Telestrations |
| 10 to 12 people | Herd Mentality | Wavelength or [Monikers](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00M07OQ1U?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) | Telestrations with the 12-player Party Pack |

### Six people

Six is a sweet spot. It is small enough for real strategy and big enough for party games to work.

**Opener: Just One**. A cooperative word game for three to seven. One person guesses a mystery word. Everyone else writes a one-word clue on a small easel, and any clues that match get thrown out before the guesser sees them. It takes a minute to teach and twenty minutes to play.

**Main event: Sushi Go Party!** or **Decrypto**. Sushi Go Party! seats two to eight and plays like a bigger, more flexible Sushi Go. Everyone takes one card from a hand and passes the rest, building sets of dishes for points. Decrypto is for a word-loving group of three to eight. Two teams each have four secret words and give coded clues that their own team should crack and the other team should not.

**Closer: Skull**. A bluffing game for three to six. Everyone stacks flower cards face down, with one skull hidden among them. Players bid on how many flowers they can flip without hitting a skull. It is all nerve and faces.

### Eight to ten people

This is the classic Friendsgiving size, and team games shine here.

**Opener: Herd Mentality**. A party game for four to twenty. A question gets read out, everyone writes an answer, and anyone who matches the most common answer wins a cow. The goal is not to be right. It is to think like the herd. Whoever gives the lone odd answer gets stuck with the pink cow, and you cannot win while you hold it.

**Main event: Codenames** or **Wavelength**. Codenames splits the room into two teams. One spymaster per team gives one-word clues to help their side find their agents on a grid of 25 words, while steering clear of the assassin. Our free [Codenames course](/academy/codenames/) has a five-minute teach. Wavelength plays two to twelve. One player sees where a hidden target sits on a spectrum, such as "underrated to overrated," and gives a clue. Their team argues about where to set the dial. The arguing is the game.

**Closer: Telestrations**. Telephone with drawings, for four to eight in the base box. Write a word, pass it, the next person draws it, the next guesses the drawing, and so on. Nobody is good at it. The reveal at the end is the point.

### Ten to twelve people

At this size, pick games that scale up rather than ones that stretch.

**Opener: Herd Mentality.** It handles a dozen people without changing a single rule.

**Main event: Wavelength** or **Monikers**. Wavelength tops out at twelve. Monikers plays four to sixteen in two teams and uses the same deck of names for three rounds: first say anything, then one word only, then charades. By round three, the jokes from round one come back, and the room is in tears.

**Closer: Telestrations with the 12-player Party Pack**. Same game, more sketchbooks, bigger reveals.

Over twelve? Split into two rooms for the main event, then bring everyone back together for Herd Mentality as the closer.

## How do you pick when nobody can agree?

Every Friendsgiving has the moment. The host holds up two boxes, half the room shrugs, and the other half wants a game nobody brought.

The fix is to let the group decide. [Game Night Madness](/game-night-madness/) is a free bracket app made by The Ludarium. The organizer loads their game collection from BoardGameGeek, and everyone votes round by round from their own phones until one game is left. It takes a few minutes, it turns the choosing into its own small game, and it ends the debate because the room picked the winner together.

A few ways to use it at Friendsgiving:

- **Set it up before guests arrive.** Pull the shelf into the app while the turkey rests.
- **Vote during dessert.** People are seated, phones are out anyway, and the result is ready the moment the plates are cleared.
- **Keep the bracket small.** Eight games is plenty. Leave out anything that seats fewer people than you have.

## What should you leave on the shelf?

Some games are great and wrong for this night.

- **Long strategy games.** A two-hour game asks people to commit right after a big meal. Save it for a smaller night.
- **Games with player elimination.** Knocked-out players drift to the kitchen and do not come back.
- **Anything that needs a long teach.** If you have to stop and look something up, the room loses interest.
- **Games that only seat four.** Unless you have a second table, half your guests become an audience.

If a few people want something deeper once the crowd thins, keep one medium game ready for the late table. Our picks for the [best board games for 6 players](/articles/best-board-games-for-6-players/) are a good place to look.

## How do you get people to actually play?

Do not announce "game time." Announcing invites people to opt out. Instead, start the opener at the table with whoever is closest, and keep it loud enough that the rest want in.

A few more host moves:

- **Teach by playing.** Run one practice round instead of explaining every rule.
- **Mix the teams.** Put old friends on opposite sides and plus-ones with the most welcoming people in the room.
- **Give the cook a break.** Whoever made dinner should not also be teaching. Hand the box to someone else.
- **End on time.** Name the last game out loud. It makes stopping feel like a choice.

## FAQ

### What is the best game for a Friendsgiving party?

Wavelength is our top pick for most groups because it seats up to twelve and creates the best arguments of the night. Codenames is a close second for eight or more.

### How many games should you plan for Friendsgiving?

Plan three: an opener, a main event, and a closer. If the evening runs short, drop the closer.

### What games work for a big Friendsgiving of 12 or more?

Herd Mentality seats up to twenty and Monikers up to sixteen. Beyond that, split into two groups for the main event and come back together for the closer.

### Are there Friendsgiving games that need no box at all?

Yes. Fishbowl, Twenty Questions, and Contact all need nothing but people, paper, and a bowl. Our [turkey games list](/articles/games-while-the-turkey-cooks/) explains how to play each one.
