Draft pod logistics are the problem you get as a reward for solving attendance. For most of Cube Commonwealth's three years as a weekly night in Indianapolis, we seated 12 to 16 players, which meant two pods nearly every week, usually with one host who was also trying to draft. It is doable. It is also the night where a sloppy process costs you an hour, and an hour on a weeknight is the difference between people coming back and people drifting.

Here is the system we settled into, including the parts we had to learn twice.

## Why are two pods harder than one big one?

Because a draft does not scale. Eight players is a clean table. Sixteen players in one pod is a two-hour draft where you see each pack twice and the signals mean nothing. Splitting into two pods of eight keeps the format intact, but it doubles everything the host does: two sets of packs, two timers, two round structures, two sets of pairings, two tables that finish at different times.

The host's job on a two-pod night is less "run the draft" and more "run the room." That shift in framing is most of the solution.

## How many pods, and how do you decide?

The decision happens at the door, not in advance, because the final count is never what the RSVP says. Our rule of thumb:

| Players | What we did |
|---|---|
| 6 to 9 | One pod. Nine drafts as one table with a bye in each round, or as nine with a round-robin build. |
| 10 to 11 | Two uneven pods (5 and 5, or 6 and 5). Thin signals, but two tables beats one slow one. |
| 12 to 14 | Two pods of 6 to 7. The most common night for us. |
| 15 to 16 | Two pods of 8, or 8 and 7. The best night, and the hardest to run. |
| 17+ | Three pods, and you need a second host. See the last section. |

Set a cutoff time for the split, announce it in the weekly post, and hold it. Holding the cutoff felt harsh the first few times. It was the single most popular rule we had, because it protected the people who came on time.

## How do you split players between pods?

Three methods, in rough order of how often we used them.

1. **Random, by draw.** Names from a cup. Fast, fair, and it breaks up the same four friends who would otherwise always draft together. Our default.
2. **New players together, with one veteran each.** On nights with several first-timers, we put them in one pod with two patient regulars and ran that pod a little slower. The other pod could play at full speed. More on that in [new-player cube night](/articles/cube-for-new-magic-players/).
3. **By preference, rarely.** Sometimes two people want to draft a specific matchup, or a couple arrived together and wants the same table. We honored it when asked and never advertised it, because once it becomes the norm, the draw stops being random.

Whichever you use, do it once, out loud, and do not renegotiate. "Can I swap with him?" is a five-minute delay times sixteen people.

## How do you run two drafts with one set of hands?

The host cannot be in two places, so the goal is a process that runs without the host for stretches of fifteen minutes.

**Before anyone arrives.** Packs are dealt and stacked by pod. Two colors of basic-land boxes, two sets of pairing sheets or a phone app set up for two events, a timer per table. If you own one cube, you need enough cards for sixteen players: 45 picks times 16 is 720 cards, so a 540-card cube does not seat two full pods. Our answer was two cubes. <!-- EDITOR: add detail (our cube list and sizes) --> The alternative is one 720-card cube or a 540 for one pod and a borrowed cube for the other.

**Opening.** One announcement for the whole room: the split, the pick clock, when round one starts, when the night ends. Then both pods open pack one at the same word.

**During the draft.** The host drafts in one pod and keeps one eye on the other. A visible timer on each table does more than a host can. We used a simple pick clock: pack one, slower; packs two and three, faster. The experienced pod self-polices; the newer pod gets the host's eye.

**Deck building.** Fifteen to twenty minutes, hard stop. Lands at each table, sleeves if your group uses them, and a clear announcement at five minutes left. This is the stage where nights die, because sixteen people building decks drift toward thirty minutes without a clock.

**Rounds.** Both pods start round one together. After that, they will drift apart in time, and that is fine. Each pod runs its own rounds independently. The host posts pairings for each pod on its own table, physically or in a per-pod Discord thread, so nobody has to find the host to learn who they play. Our full approach to pairings is in [pairings, records, and prizes](/articles/draft-pairings-and-records/).

## How do you keep the whole room on time?

Three rounds of fifty minutes, plus a forty-five minute draft and twenty minutes of building, is about three and a half hours. On a weeknight, that is the ceiling. What kept us inside it:

- **A round clock everyone can see.** Fifty minutes, then turns, then done. Draws are fine. An unfinished match is not a crisis.
- **Pods do not wait for each other.** The fast pod starts round two when it is ready. If it finishes thirty minutes before the slow pod, those players play casual games or go home. Nobody is held hostage to the other table.
- **A hard end time announced at the start.** Ours was the same every week. When the clock hit it, we stopped, including mid-round if needed, and recorded the standings as they stood.
- **The host does not play round three on heavy nights.** If one pod is behind or a dispute is brewing, the host takes a bye in the last round. It costs you one match and buys the room twenty minutes.

## What goes wrong, and what do you do about it?

A few recurring failures and their fixes.

**Uneven pod speeds.** One pod has two slow drafters and finishes the draft ten minutes after the other. Fix: let the fast pod start building. Do not hold them. Decks are built at the pace of the table.

**Someone leaves after the draft.** It happens. Life, a kid, a bad night. The pod drops to seven, round-robin pairings get a bye. Say "thanks for coming" and mean it. A group that punishes leaving early is a group people hesitate to join.

**A rules dispute in the pod you are not in.** Name a second judge before the night starts, ideally the most trusted rules player in the other pod. Most disputes in cube are settled by reading the card aloud. For the rest, a quick ruling and a note to look it up later beats a ten-minute debate.

**Decks mixed between cubes.** If you run two cubes, use different sleeve colors. We learned this one the hard way, with a sorting session at midnight.

**An odd number in both pods.** Combine the byes. The two players sitting out can play each other in a cross-pod exhibition match that does not count. Everyone prefers playing to sitting.

## When do you need a second host?

At seventeen players, or at any count where the host is not enjoying the night. We hit that point more than once and it taught us the lesson we built the relaunch around: a night that depends on one person has a ceiling and a failure point in the same place. The fix is not heroics. It is a second person who can deal packs, run pairings, and make a ruling, and who runs one pod end to end while you run the other.

We talk about why that matters for a community's survival in [relaunching a game community](/articles/relaunching-a-game-community/). The one-sentence version: train the second host while you still have the energy to, not when you have run out.

## FAQ

### How many players can one person host at a cube draft?

Sixteen, in two pods, with preparation and a visible clock. Beyond that, you need a second host.

### How do you split 12 players into draft pods?

Two pods of six, drawn at random. Six-player pods have thinner signals than eight, but two tables finish faster and play better than one table of twelve.

### How many cards do you need for two cube pods?

Each player takes 45 picks, so sixteen players need at least 720 cards. Use a 720-card cube, two separate cubes, or one cube per pod.

### How long should a 16-player cube night take?

About three and a half hours: 45 minutes to draft, 20 to build, and three 50-minute rounds. Announce a hard end time and keep it.
