A kids board game event is one of the easiest things to get wrong and one of the most appreciated when it goes right. Parents want their children off screens and around a table with other kids. Kids want to play. The gap between those two wishes is about ninety minutes of structure, and that is what this guide is about.

It covers the age bands that matter, the games that hold a table of eight-year-olds, how many adults you need, and the two rules that keep a room of twenty children from becoming noise. It is written for a library, a church, a community room, or a parent who said yes to hosting the class game day.

## What ages should a kids game afternoon serve?

Not all of them at once. A five-year-old and an eleven-year-old do not want the same afternoon, and a game that works for one bores or defeats the other. Pick a band, or run two rooms.

**Ages 4 to 6.** Short games, big pieces, cooperative where possible. Attention span is about fifteen minutes per game. Adults sit at every table.

**Ages 7 to 9.** The sweet spot for a game afternoon. Children this age can read enough to follow a card, take turns without reminders, and handle losing, mostly. Games can run twenty to thirty minutes.

**Ages 10 to 12.** Real strategy games are possible. Many want to play what the adults play. Attention span is an hour or more for the right game, and they will teach each other.

If you have to combine, 7 to 12 works with tables grouped by age. Combining 4 to 6 with anything older does not; the younger ones need a different kind of attention.

## Which games hold a table of kids?

The test for every game: can a child teach it to another child after playing once? If yes, the game runs itself after the first round. If no, it needs an adult at the table all afternoon.

A few notes from running these.

**Cooperative games for the youngest.** A table of five-year-olds playing a competitive game produces one happy child and four unhappy ones every fifteen minutes. Outfoxed! and Hoot Owl Hoot! put the whole table on one side, and the whole table wins or loses together. Our guide to [cooperative games for kids](/articles/cooperative-games-for-kids/) has more.

**Dexterity games as a release valve.** Rhino Hero and Animal Upon Animal are games children can play standing up, loudly. Put one on a table near the door for the child who cannot sit for a card game right now.

**Short games, more of them.** A thirty-minute game is the ceiling for anyone under ten. Several short games beat one long one; the reset between games is where kids move, talk, and settle again.

## How many adults do you need?

More than you think for the young ones, fewer than you think for the older ones.

- **Ages 4 to 6:** one adult per table of four. The adult teaches, referees, and keeps pieces off the floor.
- **Ages 7 to 9:** one adult per two tables. The adult teaches the first game and then floats.
- **Ages 10 to 12:** one adult per three tables, plus one adult who is just the host.

In every band, one adult should be the floor host who is not attached to a table: greeting, placing, handling the child who is upset, and watching the door. That person never sits down. The role is identical to the floor host at a [large group game night](/articles/large-group-game-night/) for adults, and it is just as important.

Recruit the table adults from the parents. Most will say yes to "can you sit at the Kingdomino table for the first half hour" when they would not volunteer for "can you help."

## What are the two rules that keep it from becoming chaos?

Children can handle many rules. Adults running a room can hold about two in their heads. These are the two.

**Rule one: every game has a table, and every table has a game.** Games do not travel. A child who wants to switch games switches tables. This stops the pile of mixed pieces that ends every kids' game event that ignores it, and it means the floor host can see at a glance what is running where.

**Rule two: the game ends when the game ends.** Nobody quits mid-game, and nobody is pulled out mid-game by a parent who is ready to leave. Parents are told this at the start. A child who learns that leaving a game early is not an option learns something useful about tables, and the other three kids at the table are spared the collapse of their game.

Everything else, from turn-taking to volume to sportsmanship, flows from those two. A table that stays together with its game to the end sorts out the rest.

## How long should it run, and what is the schedule?

Ninety minutes for ages 4 to 9. Two hours for 10 to 12. Longer than that and the last half hour is crying or wandering.

A ninety-minute afternoon for ages 7 to 9:

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0:00 | Arrival; children seated at a table as they come in; adults teach the first game to whoever is there |
| 0:10 | Most tables playing |
| 0:40 | Tables finishing; children switch tables; second game |
| 1:05 | Whole-room event: giant Jenga, a Telestrations round, a dexterity challenge |
| 1:20 | Snack, and pick-up window opens |
| 1:30 | End |

Snack at the end, not the middle. Snack mid-event means sticky components and a room that will not re-settle. Snack at the end means parents collect children who are sitting down eating.

## How do you handle the child who hates losing?

Every room has one. Sometimes three.

Do not avoid competition entirely; the ability to lose is the skill. But stack the odds. Seat that child at a cooperative game first so they have a win with the group before they face a loss alone. Keep the games short so a loss lasts ten minutes, not an hour. And have a line ready for the adult at the table: "That one got away. Want to set up the next one?" Reset fast and without a speech.

## What do parents actually thank you for?

Not the games. Three things.

**Their child played with children they did not know.** The floor host seating kids by arrival rather than by friend group makes this happen.

**Their child learned a game they can play at home.** Send each family home with the name of the game their child liked and a one-line note on who makes it. That is the whole marketing plan for the next event.

**Ninety minutes.** Parents who stay get a corner with coffee and a table of their own. Parents who leave get ninety minutes. Both are a gift. Say which one it is when you announce the event, because the staffing is different.

## FAQ

### What is a good age for a kids board game event?

Seven to nine is the easiest band to run: children can read a card, take turns, and handle most losses, and games that teach in three minutes hold them for half an hour.

### How many kids can one adult supervise at a game event?

Four at a table for ages 4 to 6; eight across two tables for ages 7 to 9; twelve across three tables for 10 to 12, with an extra adult as floor host in every case.

### What board games work for a group of kids?

For the youngest, cooperative games like Outfoxed! and Hoot Owl Hoot!. For 7 to 9, Sushi Go!, Kingdomino, and Zombie Kidz Evolution. For 10 to 12, Azul, Ticket to Ride, and King of Tokyo.

### How long should a kids game afternoon last?

Ninety minutes for children under ten, two hours for ten to twelve. End with the snack, not the middle.
