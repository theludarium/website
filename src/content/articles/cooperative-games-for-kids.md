Cooperative games for kids work on fighting siblings because they change the question from "who won?" to "did we win?" In Outfoxed!, Forbidden Island, My First Castle Panic, and The Crew, every player is on the same side against the game. A brother who would normally gloat over a sister's loss is instead cheering her draw, because her card is his card too.

That does not end sibling rivalry. Nothing ends sibling rivalry. But it gives two kids who mostly compete a regular, structured reason to collaborate, and the habit shows up outside the game sooner than you would expect.

## Why do competitive games make sibling fights worse?

Because they take a relationship that already has a running score and add an official one. Siblings compare constantly: who is taller, who got the bigger piece, who Mom likes more. A competitive game turns that into a public result with a winner and a loser, usually with the older sibling winning because they are older.

A few specific things go wrong:

- **The age gap decides the outcome.** A ten-year-old beats a seven-year-old at almost everything. After enough losses, the younger one quits or cheats.
- **Direct attacks become personal.** In games where you can take from another player, siblings always target each other. The game becomes a proxy for the fight they were already having.
- **Losing becomes ammunition.** "I beat you at Uno" gets repeated at dinner, in the car, and the next morning.

Cooperative games remove all three. There is no age-gap problem because you are not competing. There is nobody to attack. And a loss belongs to everyone, so it cannot be used against anyone.

## What actually changes when siblings play a co-op?

Three things, and parents notice them in roughly this order.

**They start talking to each other instead of at you.** In a competitive game, each kid talks to the parent: "Is it my turn? Did she cheat?" In Forbidden Island, they have to talk to each other to make a plan. "You get the fire crystal, I'll shore up the bridge." That is a conversation, and it is the first real one some siblings have had all week.

**The older one starts coaching instead of crushing.** Given a reason to want the younger one to do well, most older siblings will help. "Play the blue one, we need blue." It is bossy at first. It softens.

**Losing together is survivable.** When the island sinks, both kids are disappointed at the game, not at each other. Then they want a rematch, together. This is a different kind of loss than the ones that cause fights, and it is good practice. We wrote more about that in [teaching kids to lose gracefully](/articles/teaching-kids-to-lose-at-games/).

For siblings under eight, start with Outfoxed! and My First Castle Panic. For eight to twelve, Forbidden Island and then The Crew. The Crew in particular is special: it forces kids to pay attention to what their sibling needs without being allowed to say it out loud, which is a skill most adults lack. ## How do you run a co-op so one kid doesn't take over?

This is the real risk. In cooperative games, the oldest or loudest player can end up telling everyone what to do, and the younger one becomes a hand that moves pieces. Designers call it the quarterback problem, and there are ways to prevent it.

- **Pick games that limit talking.** The Crew and Magic Maze restrict communication by rule. Nobody can quarterback if nobody can talk.
- **Give each kid a secret.** In Mysterium and Forbidden Island, players have private information or unique roles. The team needs what only that player knows.
- **Rule: the player whose turn it is decides.** Others can suggest once. Then they stop. Enforce it gently and consistently.
- **Rotate the "captain."** Each round, a different player makes the final call on the plan. Younger kids love being captain and rise to it.
- **Let it go wrong.** If the younger one makes a bad move and the island sinks, that is a fine lesson for both of them. Rescuing the game by overriding them teaches the opposite.

A good sign you have it right: the older kid asks the younger one what they think before moving.

## Should you play as a parent or let them play alone?

Both, in that order. The first several games, play alongside them. You model how to suggest without commanding, how to lose as a team, and how to reset when it goes badly. Narrate your own thinking: "I could go here, but then you'd be stuck. What do you need?"

Then step back. Siblings who play a co-op without a parent at the table have to solve the whole thing themselves, including the arguing. That is where the real work happens. Stay in earshot the first few times, and resist the urge to referee unless something is actually breaking.

## Do cooperative games work for siblings far apart in age?

Yes, better than almost anything else. A twelve-year-old and a six-year-old cannot compete fairly at much, but they can play Outfoxed! as a team with the older one reading the clue cards and the younger one moving the detective. The age gap becomes a division of labor instead of a handicap.

Pick the game for the younger child and give the older one a bigger role in the planning. Most older kids accept a "little kid game" happily if they are the one running the strategy. And when the younger one is old enough, graduate to Forbidden Island together. ## FAQ

### What is the best cooperative game for siblings?

Outfoxed! for kids under eight, Forbidden Island for eight to twelve. Both are cheap, quick to learn, and build real teamwork.

### Do cooperative games actually reduce sibling fighting?

They reduce fighting at the table almost immediately. Carryover outside the game is slower and depends on how often you play, but most parents see more coaching and less gloating within a couple of months.

### How do you stop one kid from bossing the others in a co-op game?

Pick games that restrict talking like The Crew, give each player a secret role, and enforce a rule that the person whose turn it is makes the final decision.

### Can siblings play cooperative games without a parent?

Yes, and they should once they know the rules. Play the first few games together, then step back and let them handle the planning and the arguing themselves.
