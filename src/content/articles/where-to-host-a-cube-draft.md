Where to host a draft is the question that sinks more cube groups than the cube does. A cube sells nothing, so most game stores will not schedule it. A home fits eight and not sixteen. A bar is loud. A library closes at eight. Cube Commonwealth drafted weekly in Indianapolis for about three years with 12 to 16 players, and we moved more than once. Each venue type taught us something, and the lessons generalize to any group that needs a table every week.

Here is each option, what it costs, and what it does to the group.

## What does a cube night need from a venue?

Before comparing places, be clear about the requirements, because they are more specific than "a room."

- **Two tables of eight, or one of sixteen.** Drafts need everyone within passing distance. Long tables or pushed-together squares. Booths do not work.
- **Three and a half hours.** Forty-five minutes to draft, twenty to build, three rounds. A venue that wants the room back in two hours is a venue for a different format.
- **The same night every week.** A floating venue is a floating night, and we covered why that kills attendance in [how to get eight players to show up](/articles/how-to-get-eight-players-for-a-draft/).
- **Light and quiet enough to read cards and talk across a table.** Not silence. A conversation level.
- **A reason for the venue to want you back.** This is the one people forget. Sixteen people for three hours costs the venue something. If they get nothing, you are a guest, and guests get asked to leave eventually.

## How do game stores work for a cube group?

Stores are the obvious choice and the least likely to say yes. A store's event calendar exists to sell product: packs for draft, singles for Commander. A cube night brings in sixteen people who buy nothing. Some owners understand that those sixteen people buy sleeves, drinks, and the occasional booster box over a year. Many do not.

**When it works:** a store with a large play space and an off night. Monday or Tuesday, when the room sits empty. Offer the store something concrete: a flat table fee per player, a norm that everyone buys something, or a promise to run the store's own draft once a month. <!-- EDITOR: add detail -->

**What it costs:** often nothing, sometimes a small per-player fee. The real cost is dependence. Store policy changes, the owner sells, a Pokémon league takes your night. If a store is your home, have a second venue in your back pocket.

**What it does to the group:** store crowds are Magic crowds. You will pick up walk-ins who already play, which is good for numbers and less good for the first-timer who was promised a gentle table.

In Indianapolis, the stores that run Magic are listed in our guide to [where to play Magic in Indianapolis](/articles/where-to-play-magic-indianapolis/). Almost none run cube, which is the gap we exist in.

## How do breweries and bars work?

Our best stretch was at a brewery, and we would start there again. <!-- EDITOR: confirm -->

**When it works:** a taproom with a slow weeknight and a side room or a long back table. Breweries want bodies on a Tuesday. Sixteen adults who each buy two beers over three hours is a good Tuesday, and most owners will give you a standing reservation for it.

**What it costs:** the drinks. Make the norm explicit in the weekly post. "Buy something, this is how we keep the table." Members who do not drink buy a soda or food. The venue gets its revenue, and the group gets a reliable room.

**What it does to the group:** it makes the night feel like a night out instead of a club meeting, which brings in partners, friends, and people who would never walk into a game store. It also sets a ceiling on how young the group can be, and a floor on noise. Live music night is not your night. Ask what the calendar looks like before you commit.

The failure mode is the venue getting busier. The slow Tuesday you were offered becomes trivia night, and now you are competing for tables. Keep the owner's number and have a conversation the moment you notice it.

## How do libraries and community rooms work?

Underrated, and the right answer for a group that wants to be open to everyone, including kids and people who do not drink.

**When it works:** a branch library with a bookable meeting room, or a community center, church hall, or coworking space with evening availability. Many library systems let a community group reserve a room at no charge on a recurring basis. Some require a short application; some ask that the event be open to the public, which for a cube night is a feature.

**What it costs:** usually nothing, sometimes a small deposit. The real cost is hours. Libraries close early, often by eight or nine, and a cube night that starts at six and must be out by nine runs two rounds, not three. Weekend afternoons solve that if your group can do weekends.

**What it does to the group:** it signals that this is a community thing, not a bar thing. Parents come. Teenagers come with a parent's blessing. New players who were nervous about a store or a bar find a library easy to walk into. The energy is quieter, which some groups love and some find flat.

Bring everything. There is no food, often no vending, and the room is a room. Playmats, a speaker at low volume, and a cooler change the feel more than you would expect.

## How does hosting at home work?

Where almost every cube group starts, and where ours did.

**When it works:** eight players or fewer, a host who likes hosting, and a group that already knows each other. A dining table seats a pod. The kitchen is right there. Nobody is watching the clock on the room.

**What it costs:** the host's evening, every week. Setup, cleanup, snacks, the dog. It also costs the group its growth, because a home has a hard cap and an unspoken invitation list. New people do not show up to a stranger's house from a Discord post, and they should not be expected to.

**What it does to the group:** it makes the night intimate and makes it the host's. That is fine for a friends' group. It is the thing you have to leave behind to become a community. When we outgrew the kitchen, the move to a public venue was the moment Cube Commonwealth stopped being one person's game night.

## Which venue is right for your group?

| | Game store | Brewery or bar | Library or community room | Home |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cost to group | Free to small fee | Drinks per person | Free, maybe a deposit | Host's time |
| Capacity | 16+ | 16 with a side room | 16+ in a meeting room | 8 |
| Hours | Until close, often late | Until late | Early close | Whenever |
| New-player friendliness | Medium | High for adults | High for everyone | Low |
| Risk of losing the night | Medium | Medium, rises as venue grows | Low | Low, but capped |
| Best for | Established Magic crowd | Growing adult group | Open, all-ages group | Starting out |

If you are starting, start at home. If you have a core of six and want to grow, go to a brewery and make the drink norm explicit. If you want to be open to anyone, find a library and accept the early close. Return to a store only if a specific owner gets what you are.

## How do you keep a venue once you have it?

- **Be the easiest group they host.** Arrive on time, leave the room cleaner than you found it, stack the chairs.
- **Spend visibly.** If it is a bar, people buy drinks. If it is a store, people buy sleeves. If it is a library, people check out books and say thank you to the staff by name.
- **Give the venue a point of contact who is not the whole group.** One person who the manager can text.
- **Tell the venue what you are.** A weekly group of sixteen regulars, three years running, is an asset. Say so. Most places have never been told.

## FAQ

### Can you host a cube draft at a game store?

Sometimes. Stores prefer events that sell product, so offer something concrete: an off night, a small table fee, or a norm that everyone buys something.

### Is a brewery a good place for a Magic draft?

Yes, on a slow weeknight, if the group buys drinks and the venue has a side room or long table. Check the venue's event calendar for trivia or music nights first.

### Can a community group reserve a library room for a weekly game night?

Many library systems allow recurring reservations for community groups at no cost, sometimes with a requirement that the event be open to the public. Ask your branch.

### How many people can you host for a draft at home?

Eight, comfortably, at a dining table. Beyond that, move to a public venue.
