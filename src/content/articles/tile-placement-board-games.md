Tile placement board games share one simple idea: on your turn, you take a piece of cardboard and put it down somewhere that matters. A road meets a road. A forest grows next to a forest. A blue tile slots into the one gap in your wall that scores. The map or pattern you build is both the board and the score, so by the end of the game everyone can see the story of their choices laid out in front of them.

**The quick answer:** tile placement (also called tile laying) means building a map or pattern one tile at a time, scoring for how well the pieces fit. The big variants are a shared map everyone builds together ([Carcassonne](https://link.amazon/B012Rr5ed)), a personal area each player builds alone ([Cascadia](https://link.amazon/B0g1HYFt4), [Kingdomino](https://link.amazon/B08l49zvA)), and pattern puzzles ([Azul](https://link.amazon/B0bGRYHex), [Patchwork](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FPXTLZ21?tag=cubecommonw0a-20)). Most of the best tile laying games are easy to teach and play in under an hour.

## What is a tile placement game?

In a tile placement game, the main thing you do each turn is choose a tile and place it next to other tiles under some rule about how they must fit. Tiles might be squares, hexagons, dominoes or odd puzzle shapes. The placement rule might be matching edges, matching colors, or simply staying inside a grid.

What makes the mechanic work is that every tile changes what the next tile can do. Placing a city edge on the shared map opens a new city for someone to claim. Placing a river in your own landscape sets up where your salmon can go. Each choice is small, but the choices add up into something you built.

Tile placement pairs well with other mechanics. Many games add a way to **choose** tiles (drafting, auctions, a market) on top of the way you **place** them. Knowing which half you are looking at helps you pick games your group will enjoy.

## What are the main kinds of tile laying games?

### Shared map: one board, everybody builds it

Everyone adds tiles to the same growing map, and players compete to claim the features they help create. This is the most social version, because every placement can help or hurt someone else. **Carcassonne** is the classic: draw a tile, add it to the countryside so the edges match, and maybe place a follower to claim a road, city or monastery. [Tsuro](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GH1MJKND?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) works the same way with paths instead of cities.

### Personal tableau: your own little world

Each player builds their own area, but they choose tiles from a shared pool. There is less direct conflict and more puzzle. **Cascadia** and **Kingdomino** both work this way, and so do [Calico](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08B53WGWX?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) and [Isle of Skye](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B012HND8S8?tag=cubecommonw0a-20). You watch what your neighbors need, but the main fight is with your own layout.

### Tile drafting games: the choice is the game

Many personal tableau games are really tile drafting games. The interesting decision is less about where a tile goes and more about which tile to take before someone else does. Kingdomino ties your pick to turn order: take a strong tile and you choose last next round. Cascadia pairs each tile with an animal token, so you often take a tile you want with a token you can live with. If you like that tension, our guide to [card drafting](/articles/card-drafting-explained/) covers the same idea with cards.

### Pattern building: fill the shape

Here the goal is a pattern rather than a map. **Azul** has players draft colored tiles from small displays and fill rows to move tiles onto a wall that scores by adjacency. **Patchwork** uses odd-shaped pieces to fill a personal quilt grid, where empty squares cost you at the end. These games feel like a puzzle with a clock running.

### Tiles plus an engine

Heavier games use tile placement as one part of a larger machine. In **[The Castles of Burgundy](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07R5ZH6FQ?tag=cubecommonw0a-20)**, your dice decide which hex tiles you can take and where they can go, and each tile you place triggers its own effect. The placement still matters, but now it feeds an economy, the way [worker placement](/articles/worker-placement-explained/) or [deck building](/articles/deck-building-games-explained/) feed theirs.

## What are the best tile placement games to play?

Ten tile laying games worth a spot on the shelf, from five minutes to teach to a full evening.

### 1. Carcassonne

Carcassonne is the shared-map classic, designed by Klaus-Jürgen Wrede and first published in 2000 by Hans im Glück. It won the 2001 Spiel des Jahres. Draw a tile, add it to the countryside so the edges match, then decide whether to claim a feature with one of your followers. For 2 to 5 players. Learn it in our free [Carcassonne course](/academy/carcassonne/), then read our [Carcassonne strategy](/articles/carcassonne-strategy/) guide.

### 2. Kingdomino

Kingdomino by Bruno Cathala won the 2017 Spiel des Jahres. Players draft domino-shaped tiles and build a kingdom no bigger than 5 by 5, scoring each connected terrain by its size times its crowns. For 2 to 4 players in about 15 to 20 minutes, it is one of the easiest tile placement games to teach. The [Kingdomino course](/academy/kingdomino/) covers the draft and the crown math.

### 3. Cascadia

Cascadia by Randy Flynn, published by Flatout Games in 2021, won the 2022 Spiel des Jahres. You build a Pacific Northwest landscape from hex tiles and place wildlife tokens that score in patterns: bears in pairs, salmon in runs, hawks kept apart. For 1 to 4 players in about 30 to 45 minutes. Start with our [Cascadia course](/academy/cascadia/), and see how it compares in [Wingspan vs Everdell vs Cascadia](/articles/wingspan-vs-everdell-vs-cascadia/).

### 4. Azul

Azul by Michael Kiesling, published by Plan B Games in 2017, won the 2018 Spiel des Jahres. Take all the tiles of one color from a display, fill a row on your board, and move finished rows onto a wall that rewards connected tiles. Leftovers you cannot use cost points. For 2 to 4 players in about 30 to 45 minutes. Our [Azul course](/academy/azul/) teaches it, and [Azul strategy](/articles/azul-strategy/) goes deeper.

### 5. Patchwork

Patchwork by Uwe Rosenberg (2014) is a two-player quilt puzzle. Buy odd-shaped patches with buttons, fit them on your 9 by 9 board, and move along a time track; whoever is behind on time goes next. Empty squares cost points at the end. About 30 minutes, and a strong pick for couples.

### 6. Calico

Calico by Kevin Russ (Flatout Games, 2020) asks you to sew a quilt from hex tiles, scoring for color and pattern goals and for attracting cats who like specific patterns. It is a tighter, thinkier puzzle than its cozy look suggests. For 1 to 4 players.

### 7. Isle of Skye

Isle of Skye by Alexander Pfister and Andreas Pelikan won the 2016 Kennerspiel des Jahres. Each round you set prices on your own tiles, then everyone gets one chance to buy from someone else. Whatever nobody buys, you pay for yourself. Landscapes must match along edges, and the scoring goals change every game. For 2 to 5 players.

### 8. The Castles of Burgundy

The Castles of Burgundy by Stefan Feld, first published in 2011 by alea and Ravensburger, is the heavier pick. Two dice each turn decide which hex tiles you can take and where on your estate they can go, and every tile type does something different. Plan for about 90 minutes. The [Castles of Burgundy course](/academy/castles-of-burgundy/) walks through a first game.

### 9. Alhambra

[Alhambra](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07Z575QLT?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) by Dirk Henn won the 2003 Spiel des Jahres. Players buy building tiles with four currencies and add them to their own palace, scoring for majorities in each building type and for the longest outer wall. Paying exact change earns an extra turn, which is the heart of the game. For 2 to 6 players.

### 10. Tsuro

Tsuro by Tom McMurchie is the shortest game here. Each player has a stone on a shared board. Place a path tile in front of your stone and follow the path wherever it leads. Get pushed off the board or into another stone and you are out. For 2 to 8 players in about 15 to 20 minutes, it is a great opener for a big group.

## How do you choose a tile placement game for your group?

- **New players or kids:** Kingdomino or Tsuro. Five minutes to teach, short enough to play twice.
- **A table that likes a little conflict:** Carcassonne. You will steal cities and share farms.
- **A table that prefers to puzzle quietly:** Cascadia, Calico or Azul. Interaction comes through what you take, not what you attack.
- **Two players:** Patchwork, or Carcassonne and Azul at their two-player counts.
- **A group ready for more weight:** Isle of Skye, then The Castles of Burgundy.

If you are not sure what your group likes, the [Card Catalogue](/card-catalogue/) sorts games by how they feel, not just by mechanic.

## Why do tile placement board games work so well at the table?

Three reasons. First, they are visual: you can see the state of the game at a glance, which makes them easy to teach and easy to join. Second, turns are short, so nobody waits long and the table keeps talking. Third, the finished map or pattern is a small thing you made, and people like to look at it together at the end. That last part is easy to underrate. A lot of tile laying games end with everyone leaning over each other's boards, pointing out the clever bits.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between tile placement and tile laying?

Nothing. The two names describe the same mechanic: placing tiles to build a map or pattern. Some players use "tile laying" more for shared-map games like Carcassonne, but the terms are used interchangeably.

### What is a tile drafting game?

A tile drafting game is one where players pick tiles from a shared set, and what you leave behind is what others get. Kingdomino, Cascadia and Azul all use tile drafting. The draft is usually where the hardest decisions are.

### What is the best tile placement game for beginners?

Kingdomino is the easiest to teach and plays in about 15 to 20 minutes. Carcassonne is the classic next step, and Cascadia is a great pick for groups that like calm, puzzly games.

### Is Azul a tile placement game?

Yes. Azul is a pattern building tile placement game: you draft colored tiles, then place them on your board to complete rows and score on your wall. It also has a strong drafting element.

### Are there good two-player tile placement games?

Patchwork is built for exactly two players. Carcassonne, Kingdomino, Azul, Cascadia and The Castles of Burgundy all play well with two as well.
