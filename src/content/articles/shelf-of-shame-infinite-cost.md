Every gamer has one. Maybe it is a single box, still in shrink wrap, that seemed like a great idea at the convention. Maybe it is an entire bookcase. The shelf of shame is the collection of board games you own and have never played, and in the arithmetic of this hobby it is the most expensive thing in your house.

This article is part of The Math of Game Night, a Ludarium series on what game night really costs. The whole series rests on one formula, [cost per play](/articles/cost-per-play-explained/): price ÷ plays ÷ players ÷ hours. The shelf of shame is what happens when the second number is zero.

## Why is an unplayed game's cost per hour infinite?

Take a $70 game. Divide by the hours it has delivered: zero. You cannot divide by zero, and in practice the result is as large as you want it to be. The game has cost you $70 and returned nothing, which is a worse deal than any game you have ever played, including the ones you did not like.

Compare it to a $40 game your group has played 20 times, 4 players, an hour a session. That works out to about fifty cents per person per hour. Now set the two boxes side by side. The cheaper box has produced 80 hours of people's time together. The pricier box has produced some shelf weight.

This is the uncomfortable truth the formula surfaces: price is not where the money goes. Plays are. If you want to lower what your hobby costs, the single most effective move is not to buy cheaper games. It is to stop owning games you do not play.

## Why do board games go unplayed?

The shelf of shame is not a character flaw. It is usually the result of one of a handful of predictable mismatches, and naming them is the first step toward fixing them.

### It is too heavy for the group

You bought a game at your weight, not your group's. Something like Twilight Imperium or Spirit Island is a wonderful experience for a table that wants it and a wall for a table that does not. If your regular group leans toward Codenames and Ticket to Ride, a four-hour epic will keep losing the vote, no matter how good it is.

### It is the wrong player count

The box says two to five. Your group is six. Or the game is best at exactly two and you never play at two. Player count is the quietest reason games stall, because the game is not bad and the group is not wrong. They just do not overlap on the nights you meet. [Matching games to your actual group](/articles/match-games-to-your-group/), not the group you imagine, prevents most of these.

### You bought it on hype

A glowing review, a convention crowd, a Kickstarter campaign with beautiful renders. Hype makes you estimate plays generously and then the box arrives and the moment has passed. This is the purchase that [playing before you pay](/articles/play-before-you-pay/) is designed to prevent, and the one that is hardest to admit to.

### Nobody wants to teach it

Someone has to read the rulebook, learn the game, and then teach it to a table of people who would rather be playing already. If that person is not you, and nobody else volunteers, the game stays sealed. Heavy games with long rulebooks pay a teaching tax every session, and a game that nobody will teach is effectively a game nobody owns.

## How to run an honest shelf audit

Set aside twenty minutes and go through every game you own. For each one, answer three questions and write the answers down. The writing matters; it is harder to lie to a list.

1. **When did this last hit the table?** Not approximately. The actual last time. If you cannot remember, that is an answer.
2. **Who in my group would ask to play it?** Name the person. If you cannot name one, the game has no advocate, and games without advocates do not get played.
3. **What is stopping it?** Pick from the list above: too heavy, wrong count, hype, nobody will teach it. Most games have one clear reason.

Sort the results into three piles: played in the last year, played ever but not recently, and never played. The never-played pile is your shelf of shame. The not-recently pile is on its way there.

If your group shares a shelf across several homes, do this together. A [group shelf audit](/articles/audit-your-group-shelf/) catches duplicates and reveals that the game you were about to give up on is one someone else has been wanting to try. A game that is dead on your shelf may be alive on the shared one, and that is one of the strongest arguments for [The One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/) in the first place: one copy in the group's library is a game with eight potential advocates instead of one.

## The three options for every unplayed game

Once you know why a game sits, there are exactly three things you can do about it. Do one of them for every box in the never-played pile. "Leave it and feel bad" is not on the list.

### Option one: schedule it

Some games are unplayed because they have never been given a real chance. If the game has an advocate and the reason is "we just never got around to it," put a date on it. Tell the group a week ahead. Have whoever is teaching read the rules in advance so the session starts with play, not a 40-minute rules read.

One scheduled play usually settles things. Either the game earns a second play on its own, or the table's verdict tells you it belongs in option two. Both outcomes are fine. The only bad outcome is another year of not knowing.

### Option two: trade it or sell it

If the game is a mismatch for your group, it is a match for someone else's. Hobby gamers trade constantly, and a sealed or lightly played copy holds its value well. Trading a game that will never get played for a game that will is the cheapest purchase you will ever make, since you recover most of your sunk cost and the new game starts with real plays instead of a pile of hope. [Trading and selling games you don't play](/articles/trading-and-selling-games/) walks through how to do it without losing money.

A small test before listing it: would someone in your group pay you for it? If so, it is not a shelf-of-shame game. It is a game that has been living in the wrong house.

### Option three: admit it

A few games are keepers that will not be played. A beautiful production you want to own. A game tied to a memory. A campaign you fully intend to finish when the kids are older. That is allowed. The formula divides by hours, not by sentiment, and it does not get the final word on your shelf.

The one thing option three asks is that you say it out loud. "I own this and I am not going to play it." Once it is admitted, the game stops being a nagging obligation and becomes a possession, like a book you love and will not reread. It also stops being a reason to buy more games just like it.

## The no-shame framing

We use the name because everyone else does, but the shame part is not helpful. A shelf of unplayed games is just a list of estimates that turned out wrong. You guessed the group would want it, or you guessed you would find the time, and the guess missed. That is information, not failure.

Here is what the information is for. Look at your never-played pile and find the pattern. If three of five are heavy games, your group plays lighter than you shop. If most are five-player games and you play with three, you have found your real player count. If most came from a convention or a crowdfunding campaign, you have found your weak spot at checkout. [Tracking your plays changes what you buy](/articles/tracking-plays-changes-what-you-buy/) for exactly this reason: the shelf of shame is the clearest signal in the hobby about what your next purchase should not be.

And the fix going forward is simple. Before the next box, ask the question that would have saved the last one: who will play this, how often, and who will teach it? If you can answer all three, buy it. If you cannot, play it somewhere first. The game is the reason. The people are the point, and a game with no people around it was never going to work, however good it was.

## FAQ

### What is a shelf of shame in board games?

The shelf of shame is the gamer's term for board games you own but have never played, often still in shrink wrap. Because cost per play divides price by plays, a game with zero plays has an effectively infinite cost per hour, making it the most expensive part of any collection regardless of what it cost.

### Why do I keep buying board games I never play?

The common reasons are buying games heavier than your group likes, buying for a player count you rarely have, buying on hype or at conventions, and buying games nobody in the group wants to teach. An honest audit of your unplayed games usually reveals one pattern, which tells you what to stop buying.

### What should I do with board games I never play?

You have three options: schedule a specific play date and give the game one real chance, trade or sell it to someone whose group will play it, or consciously decide to keep it as a possession rather than an obligation. Any of the three beats leaving it on the shelf and feeling guilty about it.

### Is it worth selling unplayed board games?

Usually yes. A sealed or lightly played game holds much of its value, and recovering that money toward a game your group will actually play is close to a free purchase. Trading within the hobby is common and lets you swap a mismatch for a match without cash changing hands.

### How do I stop my board game backlog from growing?

Before buying, name who in your group will play the game, how often, and who will teach it. If you cannot answer all three, play it at a game night, café, or friend's table first. Checking whether someone in your group already owns it also prevents the duplicates that pile up fastest.
