So, what is a tabletop RPG? It is a game where a group of people sit around a table and make up a story together, with one person describing the world and the others each playing a single character in it. Dice settle the moments where the outcome is in doubt. Dungeons & Dragons is the famous one, but it is one of hundreds.

If your only exposure is the kids in the basement in Stranger Things, you already have the shape of it right: a group of friends, a table, a lot of yelling, and a monster called a Demogorgon that one of them is narrating. This article fills in the rest.

## What does "tabletop RPG" actually stand for?

RPG means role-playing game. "Tabletop" distinguishes it from video game RPGs like Skyrim or Final Fantasy, which borrowed the term from the table and not the other way around. You will also see TTRPG, "pen-and-paper RPG," or just "roleplaying game." They all mean the same thing.

The "role" part is literal. Each player takes on the role of one character and decides what that character says and does. The "game" part is the set of rules that turns those decisions into outcomes. Without the rules it is improv. Without the roles it is a board game. The combination is the thing.

## How does a tabletop RPG work?

Every tabletop RPG has the same basic structure, with different names for the parts.

**One person runs the game.** In D&D this person is the Dungeon Master, or DM. Other systems say Game Master (GM), Keeper, Referee, or Storyteller. This person describes the scene, voices every non-player character (shopkeepers, villains, the talking sword), decides what happens when players try things, and plays the monsters during fights.

**Everyone else plays one character.** You get a character sheet, a piece of paper listing who your character is, what they are good at, and what equipment and abilities they have. A fighter is tough and hits hard. A wizard is fragile and bends reality. A rogue picks locks and lies well.

**Play is a conversation.** The GM describes. The players say what they do. The GM describes the result. Repeat for three hours. There is no board in the usual sense, though many groups use a map and miniatures for fights.

**Dice resolve uncertainty.** When a player tries something that might fail, the GM asks for a roll. In D&D that means rolling a twenty-sided die and adding a bonus from your sheet. High numbers succeed. Other systems use different dice, or pools of dice, or cards, but the idea is the same: the rules introduce chance so the story is not just whoever talks loudest.

That is the whole machine. Everything you see in a thick rulebook is elaboration on those four parts.

## What is the point if nobody wins?

This is the question board game players ask, and it is a fair one. There is no victory condition. Nobody beats the GM. A session ends when the group reaches a good stopping point, the way a chapter ends.

The point is the story you did not know you were going to tell. In a novel or a video game, the plot is fixed. In a tabletop RPG, the GM prepares a situation (a village is missing its children; a vault needs robbing) and then has no idea what the players will do about it. Neither do the players, until the dice land.

The memorable moments are nearly always unplanned. The wizard who rolled a critical failure trying to open a jar. The player who talked a dragon out of a fight. The time the group spent an hour adopting a goblin instead of fighting it. These become the stories friends retell for years, the same way [game night memories tend to last](/articles/why-game-night-memories-last/).

## Is it all dragons and dungeons?

No. Fantasy is the biggest slice because D&D got there first, but there is a tabletop RPG for almost any genre you would watch a show about.

| Genre | Well-known systems | What a session feels like |
|---|---|---|
| Fantasy | Dungeons & Dragons, Pathfinder | Heroes, monsters, magic, treasure |
| Horror | Call of Cthulhu, Dread | Investigation, dread, characters who probably do not survive |
| Science fiction | Traveller, Starfinder, Mothership | Spaceships, salvage, things that should not be aboard |
| Heists and crime | Blades in the Dark | A crew of scoundrels planning and botching a job |
| Everyday life and drama | Fiasco, Good Society | Bad decisions, Austen-style romance, no combat at all |
| Kids and whimsy | Kids on Bikes, No Thank You Evil | Small-town mysteries, Stranger Things in reverse |

Some of these are as rules-light as a single page. We ranked the easiest ones in [RPGs that aren't D&D](/articles/easy-tabletop-rpgs-for-beginners/). If dragons leave you cold, that is not a reason to skip the hobby. It is a reason to pick a different book.

## Why do grown adults do this?

Because it is one of the few activities left where a group of adults sits in a room for three hours with their phones face down, paying full attention to each other.

There is a reason people describe their weekly game as the thing they protect on the calendar. A tabletop RPG asks for sustained, shared attention. It rewards listening, because the best move is often built on what another player just said. It gives permission to be silly without irony. And it produces stories that belong only to the people who were there.

There is a small but growing body of research on tabletop RPGs and wellbeing, including their use by therapists working with teens and adults on social skills and confidence; a [2025 systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12242152/) of their use with neurodivergent players found promising social benefits but only a handful of small studies, so we treat specific claims carefully. We go deeper on that in [why grown adults play pretend](/articles/benefits-of-tabletop-rpgs/). The short version is that the hobby looks like escapism from the outside and looks like social practice from the inside.

## What do you need to start?

Less than people assume.

- **A group of three to five people**, one of whom is willing to run the game. Four players plus a GM is a comfortable size.
- **A system.** D&D's free basic rules cover a first session. Many lighter games are free or cost less than a movie ticket.
- **Dice.** For D&D, the standard seven-die polyhedral set. For many other systems, a handful of ordinary six-sided dice will do.
- **Paper and pencils.** Character sheets are usually free printables.
- **An adventure.** For a first game, use a published "one-shot," a short self-contained story built to finish in one evening. Writing your own comes later.
- **A table and three hours.** Kitchen tables work. Game lounges work better, which is a big part of why we are building one.

Total cost for a first evening can be zero if someone at the table already owns dice.

## How is a tabletop RPG different from a board game?

Three differences matter.

First, there is no fixed list of moves. In Catan you can build, trade, or play a card. In an RPG you can do anything you can describe, and the GM decides how to resolve it. This freedom is the main source of first-session paralysis, and it fades fast.

Second, there is a human referee. The rules are interpreted by a person at the table, not enforced by cardboard. This makes RPGs more flexible and, occasionally, more arguable. Good tables agree up front that the GM makes the call and moves on.

Third, time. A board game is a complete experience in an evening. Many RPG groups play a continuing story, called a campaign, over months or years. You do not have to commit to that. One-shots exist for exactly this reason, and plenty of people only ever play those.

## What about the Stranger Things version?

The show gets the spirit right. Four kids, one of them narrating a fight with a monster, dice hitting the table, everyone shouting advice. That is a real D&D session in 1983, down to the specific monster names, which come from the actual Monster Manual of the time.

What the show compresses is the pacing. Real sessions have slow stretches: planning, shopping for supplies, arguing about which corridor to take. Those stretches are where the friendships live. The fights are the highlight reel. The rest is the reason you keep coming back. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### Is D&D the same thing as a tabletop RPG?

D&D is one tabletop RPG, the most popular one. Tabletop RPG is the category; D&D is a brand within it, alongside Pathfinder, Call of Cthulhu, and hundreds of others.

### Do you need to be good at acting or improv?

No. Many players describe their character's actions in plain third person and never perform a voice. The game works fine either way.

### How long does a tabletop RPG take to play?

A single session usually runs two to four hours. One-shots finish in one session; campaigns continue across many sessions for as long as the group wants.

### Can you play a tabletop RPG with two people?

Yes. One GM and one player is a valid and intimate format, though most published adventures assume three to five players.
