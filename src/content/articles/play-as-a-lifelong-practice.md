Lifelong play is the idea that play is something you keep doing on purpose, across your whole life, the way you keep sleeping and eating, rather than something children do and adults grow out of. Most adults have absorbed the opposite assumption without ever examining it. Play was for before. Now there is work, and family, and later, maybe, rest.

This article argues the assumption is wrong, that the research on adult play and aging supports treating it as a practice, and that the practical difference between a phase and a practice comes down to a few habits anyone can build.

## Why do most adults stop playing?

Not because they decide to. Because the structures that made play automatic disappear, and nothing replaces them.

A child's day is built around play. School has recess. The neighborhood has other kids. Then the structures fall away one at a time. By the late twenties, play has become something that has to be scheduled, and most people never learn to schedule it. The same thing happens to friendship, for the same reason, which is why we treat them as the same problem.

There is also a story adults tell themselves: that play is unproductive, and adult time has to be productive. Stuart Brown, the psychiatrist who founded the National Institute for Play, spent decades collecting what he called play histories, [some 6,000 of them](https://dumbofeather.com/conversations/dr-stuart-brown-believes-we-were-born-to-play), and has argued, in his book Play and [elsewhere](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/395065944), that the opposite of play is not work but depression. He describes humans as designed to play across a whole lifetime, not just childhood, and has warned that adults who suppress it lose something in mood and flexibility.

So the phase model is not a description of human nature. It is a description of what happens when nobody tends the practice.

## What does the research say about adults who keep playing?

The evidence is still mostly correlational, and we will say so where it matters, but the direction is consistent.

**Playfulness and health.** Studies of playfulness in adults, including [work by René Proyer](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01440/pdf) at Martin Luther University Halle-Wittenberg, have linked a playful disposition to a more active lifestyle and, for some facets of playfulness, better self-reported mental health. The associations are modest and the designs are mostly cross-sectional. The research suggests, roughly, that people who play more also handle hard things more flexibly.

**Games and thinking in later life.** The [Lothian Birth Cohort in Scotland](https://www.ed.ac.uk/research-innovation/latest-research-news/games-can-protect-thinking-skills-in-older-age) found that people who played more non-digital games like cards and board games in their 70s scored better on memory and thinking tests, and that those who played more over the decade showed less decline, particularly in memory and thinking speed. The researchers described the association as small and did not claim cause. We cover the fuller picture in [cognitive reserve and games](/articles/cognitive-reserve-and-games/).

**Play as social connection.** This is the strongest leg. The [Harvard Study of Adult Development](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2023/02/work-out-daily-ok-but-how-socially-fit-are-you), running since 1938, has reported that satisfaction in close relationships in midlife predicted health and happiness at 80 better than cholesterol or blood pressure at 50. Julianne Holt-Lunstad's [2010 meta-analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316), which pooled 148 studies covering more than 300,000 people, found that those with stronger social ties had about a 50 percent greater likelihood of survival over the study periods, an effect the authors described as comparable to quitting smoking. Most adult play is social play. A game night is one of the most reliable ways adults put the same people in the same room every week.

Put together: play is associated with better mood, better thinking, and more connection, and connection is associated with living longer. None of this is proof that a Thursday game night adds years. It is a reason to treat the Thursday as something more than a hobby.

## What is the difference between a phase and a practice?

A phase is something you do when conditions allow. A practice is something you arrange conditions to protect. The difference shows up in a few concrete places.

| | Play as a phase | Play as a practice |
|---|---|---|
| When it happens | When there is spare time | On a schedule, like exercise |
| Who it happens with | Whoever is around | A group you keep over years |
| What happens when life gets busy | It stops | It gets smaller, not cancelled |
| How it is judged | By whether it was fun | By whether you kept showing up |
| What it builds | Memories | Relationships, skill, identity |

The middle row is the one that matters. In a phase, a new baby or a new job ends play for a decade. In a practice, it shrinks the game from [Gloomhaven](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01LZXVN4P?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) to a 20-minute round of The Crew and keeps the people. Our piece on games for new parents is a case study in exactly that.

## How do you build a play practice that lasts?

Treat it the way you would treat any habit you intend to keep for fifty years.

1. **Pick a standing time.** Weekly is the floor. The same night with the same people is the whole engine. If you don't have a group, [how to start a game night group](/articles/how-to-start-a-game-night-group/) covers the steps.
2. **Choose a form that scales down.** A practice has to survive your worst month. Keep a shelf of short games and a two-player option so a bad week shrinks the session instead of skipping it.
3. **Let the game change with your life.** The game you play at 30 is not the game at 60 or 90. [Catan](https://link.amazon/B0h8BDGR2) gives way to cribbage. That is not decline. That is the practice continuing in a new form.
4. **Keep an open chair.** Groups that admit new people survive moves, deaths, and divorces. Closed groups end when one person leaves.
5. **Count it as health.** Put it on the same mental list as sleep and movement. When you are deciding whether to skip it because you're tired, remember that the tired weeks are when connection does the most good.
6. **Start before you need it.** The people who do well through retirement, illness, or loss are the ones who already had a Thursday.

## What does lifelong play look like across the decades?

A sketch, not a prescription.

**Twenties and thirties.** Building the group. Lots of games, lots of people, finding the four or five who stick. The main risk is drift when people move or have kids.

**Forties and fifties.** Protecting the group. Fewer new games, more depth. This is when a standing night most often dies and most needs defending. The payoff is that a group that reaches 20 years together has something no new group can buy.

**Sixties and seventies.** Expanding the group. Retirement opens time and closes the workplace, which for many people was their only social structure. Play is the easiest way to rebuild it. [Why friendships fade with age](/articles/why-friendships-fade-with-age/) covers that transition.

**Eighties and nineties.** Adapting the table. Large-print cards, afternoon light, slower turns, and the same people, or their children. We describe that table in detail elsewhere on the site.

The game changes in every decade. The practice does not. That is the whole idea.

## FAQ

### What does lifelong play mean?

Lifelong play means treating play as an ongoing practice you maintain across adulthood and old age, scheduled and protected like other health habits, rather than a childhood phase you outgrow.

### Is play good for adults?

The research suggests so. Playfulness in adults has been associated with better self-reported health and coping, non-digital game play in later life with better memory and thinking scores, and the social connection most play provides with lower mortality risk. The evidence is largely correlational.

### How do I start playing again as an adult?

Pick one standing weekly time, invite two or three people, and choose a game that takes under an hour and teaches in five minutes. Consistency matters more than the game.

### Does the kind of play matter?

Social, in-person play appears to carry the most benefit because it combines mental engagement with connection. Solo play has value too, but the research on longevity points most strongly to play with other people.
