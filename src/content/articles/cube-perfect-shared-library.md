If you want to see the One Copy Rule taken to its limit, watch a Magic Cube draft. One person brings one box. Eight people sit down, build decks from it, play for three hours, and put every card back. Nobody buys anything. The same box comes out next week for a different eight people, and the week after that. One copy, unlimited players, unlimited plays.

This is the seventh article in The Shared Shelf series from The Ludarium. You do not need to know anything about Magic: The Gathering to follow it. We will explain what a Cube is in plain terms, show why it is the purest example of a shared library we know of, and pull out three lessons any board game group can borrow, whether or not they ever touch a Magic card.

If you are new to the series, the rule the Cube proves is laid out in [The One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/).

## What is a Magic Cube, for someone who has never played Magic?

Magic: The Gathering is a card game where each player builds a deck of cards and plays it against someone else's deck. In the normal version of the hobby, every player buys their own cards and builds their own decks. That gets expensive, and it rewards whoever spends the most.

A Cube flips that. A Cube is a curated pool of cards, usually a few hundred, that one person assembles and owns. There is exactly one copy of each card in the pool. The cards are chosen to work well together, cover a range of styles, and produce interesting choices.

On Cube night, the owner shuffles the pool into packs and deals them out to eight players. Each player opens a pack, takes one card, passes the rest to the left, and repeats until the packs are gone. That process is called drafting. When it is over, each player has a stack of forty-five cards they picked, builds a deck from them, and plays against the other drafters.

At the end of the night, every card goes back into the box. Next time, the same box produces eight completely different decks, because the draft went differently. The pool is the same; the games are never the same.

That is the whole thing. One owner, one box, eight players, infinite variety.

## Why is a Cube the purest Shared Shelf?

Think back to cost per play: price ÷ plays ÷ players ÷ hours. A Cube stretches every term in that formula as far as it will go.

- **One price.** The owner builds the Cube once. It might be built from cards they already had, from inexpensive reprints, or over years of careful additions. Either way, there is one collection, not eight.
- **Unlimited plays.** A Cube does not get used up. The same pool drafts differently every single time, so it never goes stale the way a board game with a fixed setup can.
- **Eight players, every time.** Every draft night divides the owner's cost across eight people. Over a year of weekly drafts, that is hundreds of player-sessions from one box.
- **Hours, not minutes.** A draft plus three rounds of games is a full evening.

Compare that to a four-person board game group sharing a copy of Catan. That is a great deal, about fifty cents per player per play by the illustration in the pillar. A Cube is the same idea with twice the players and no ceiling on plays.

And the social structure is the Shared Shelf exactly. One person owns it. Everyone plays from it. Nobody at the table needs to own anything, and the owner is not giving anything up, because a Cube with one player is not a Cube at all. The seven other drafters are not freeloaders; they are the reason the Cube exists.

## What does Cube Commonwealth look like in practice?

[Cube Commonwealth](/articles/cube-draft-indianapolis/), our Magic Cube community in Indianapolis, drafted weekly for about three years and is relaunching as part of The Ludarium. On a Cube night the room is full of people who show up with nothing but a drink and leave with nothing but a good evening, because the library is on the table and the library is enough.

What strikes first-timers is how little buying there is. Most of the hobby's energy around Magic is about acquiring cards. A Cube room has almost none of that. The conversation is about what you drafted, what you should have drafted, and what the owner should swap in next month. The library is the shared object, and everyone has opinions about it, and that is what makes it a community rather than a store.

## What can a board game group borrow from the Cube model?

You do not need a Cube to learn from one. Three habits make a Cube work, and all three transfer directly to a Shared Shelf of board games.

### Lesson one: curation beats accumulation

A Cube is not every good card. It is a few hundred cards chosen to work together. The owner cuts as often as they add. When a new card goes in, an old one comes out, because the pool has a fixed size and every slot has to earn its place.

A board game Shared Shelf benefits from the same discipline. Twenty games that cover every lane and player count beat sixty games where forty never get played. The Shelf of Shame is what happens when accumulation wins. A collection cap, where you buy what fits the collection rather than what you merely want, is the board game version of a Cube's fixed size. [One In, One Out: Living With a Board Game Shelf Cap](/articles/one-in-one-out-shelf-cap/) goes deeper on that habit.

### Lesson two: one caretaker per library

Every Cube has an owner, and the owner is also the curator. They know every card, they track what is working and what is not, and they make the changes. Eight people drafting a Cube would be chaos if eight people were also editing it.

On a Shared Shelf, the equivalent is one caretaker per lane. The strategy owner knows the strategy games, keeps them in good condition, brings the right one on the right night, and decides what gets added. That is exactly what [Claim a Lane](/articles/claim-a-lane/) sets up. The rest of the group gets to have opinions, and good lane owners listen to them, but someone has to hold the box.

### Lesson three: rotation keeps one copy fresh

A Cube's secret is that the same cards produce different games every time, because the draft reshuffles everything. Board games do not reshuffle themselves, so a Shared Shelf has to build rotation in on purpose.

A few ways to do it:

- **Rotate which lane leads each night.** Strategy night, then party night, then co-op night. The same twenty games feel like sixty.
- **Rotate hosts, and let the host's shelf set the menu.** Games get played where they live.
- **Swap the shelf seasonally.** The Cube owner swaps cards in and out; a lane owner can "retire" two games to the closet for six months and bring them back as new.
- **Track plays.** A Cube owner knows which cards never get drafted and cuts them. A play group that tracks which games actually hit the table learns the same thing about its shelf. [Tracking Your Plays Changes What You Buy](/articles/tracking-plays-changes-what-you-buy/) covers why that matters.

## Do you need a Cube to run a Shared Shelf?

No. The point of this article is not that every group should start drafting Magic. It is that the Cube proves something about shared libraries that is easy to doubt in the abstract: one well-curated copy, cared for by one person and played by many, is not a compromise. It is the better version of the hobby.

Eight people at a Cube table are not making do with one box. They are having a better night than eight people with eight collections would, because the pool was built for the table, by someone who loves it, and everyone at that table is playing from the same shelf.

That is what a Shared Shelf of board games is reaching for. The Cube just gets there first.

The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### What is a Magic: The Gathering Cube?

A Cube is a curated pool of Magic cards, usually a few hundred, that one person owns and maintains. On Cube night, the owner deals the pool out as packs, eight players draft cards from them, build decks, and play. At the end, every card goes back in the box, ready for a completely different draft next time.

### How many people play in a Cube draft?

A standard Cube draft seats eight players. The pool is dealt into packs, players pick one card and pass the rest around the table until the packs are empty, then each player builds a deck and plays. A group with sixteen regulars would run two pods and usually needs a second Cube, which is one of the few real exceptions to the One Copy Rule.

### Do you need to buy Magic cards to play Cube?

No. The Cube owner supplies every card, and players draft from that pool and return everything at the end of the night. That is what makes Cube the purest shared library in tabletop gaming: one person's collection serves eight players, week after week, with no purchases required from anyone else.

### What can a board game group learn from a Magic Cube?

Three things. Curate instead of accumulate, so every game on the shelf earns its slot. Give each part of the library one caretaker who knows it and keeps it in shape. And build in rotation, so the same shelf feels fresh: rotate which genre leads each night, rotate hosts, retire and return games, and track what gets played.
