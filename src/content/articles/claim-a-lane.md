Once a play group agrees on the One Copy Rule, a new question shows up: if we are only buying one copy of each game, who buys what? Claim a Lane is the answer. Each person in the group collects a different genre or role, so four people buying in four directions cover far more ground than four people chasing the same hits.

This is the third article in The Shared Shelf series from The Ludarium. It takes the idea from [The One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/) and turns it into a buying plan. By the end you will have six lanes, a worked example of four friends covering more than twenty games, and a way to assign and rebalance lanes without anyone feeling boxed in.

## What is Claim a Lane?

Claim a Lane means each member of a play group takes responsibility for one slice of the shared library. One person collects strategy games. Another collects party games. Another collects co-ops. Nobody is forbidden from buying outside their lane, but when the group needs a new game in a lane, the lane owner is the one who buys it.

The lanes we use are:

- **Strategy / euro:** the meatier games for the long evening. [Catan](https://link.amazon/B0h8BDGR2), [Wingspan](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07YQ641NQ?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [7 Wonders](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08F65MX4L?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Terraforming Mars](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01GSYA4K2?tag=cubecommonw0a-20).
- **Party:** six or more players, loud, easy to teach. [Codenames](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B014Q1XX9S?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Just One](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07GJ7NDZQ?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Wavelength](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07T446163?tag=cubecommonw0a-20).
- **Co-op:** everyone on the same side. Pandemic, [The Crew](https://link.amazon/B099S9RKf), [Spirit Island](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01MUHP51S?tag=cubecommonw0a-20).
- **2-player:** for the night only two people show up. Patchwork, [Jaipur](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07SRMXRZB?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Hive](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000EN5ZLW?tag=cubecommonw0a-20).
- **Campaign / legacy:** one game played across many sessions. [Gloomhaven](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01LZXVN4P?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Sherlock Holmes Consulting Detective](https://www.amazon.com/dp/2370990074?tag=cubecommonw0a-20).
- **Filler:** under twenty minutes, pulled out while people arrive or as a closer. [Sushi Go!](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00J57VU44?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Love Letter](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00AGJ4HC2?tag=cubecommonw0a-20).

These lanes line up with the roles on a starter shelf: gateway, filler, two-player, party, co-op, stretch. The difference is that a starter shelf is one person covering all the roles with five or six games. Claim a Lane is four people covering all the roles with twenty.

## Why do lanes beat everyone buying their favorites?

Without lanes, each person buys what they like most, and what people like most tends to overlap. A group of four strategy fans ends up with four shelves of strategy games and no party game for the night a cousin comes to town. The library is deep in one place and empty everywhere else.

With lanes, the group's buying is spread out by design. Every purchase lands in a role, and every role has someone responsible for it. The library grows wide as well as deep. It also makes the "who buys the next one?" conversation almost automatic: the group wants a new co-op, the co-op person buys it, and the game lives at their place.

There is a side effect worth naming. When you own a lane, you pay attention to it. The party-lane owner starts noticing new party games and learns which ones teach fastest. The co-op owner knows which co-op scales to five. Each person becomes the group's expert in one corner, and the whole group benefits from four experts instead of four generalists.

## A worked example: four friends, five games each, 20+ games

Here is an illustration of how it plays out. Four friends, call them A, B, C, and D, each buy five games over a year or two. That is twenty purchases. With lanes assigned, the shelf might look like this.

| Lane | Owner | Games (illustration) | Player counts covered |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strategy / euro | A | Catan, Wingspan, 7 Wonders, Terraforming Mars, [Root](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07F454YF3?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) | 2 to 7 |
| Party | B | Codenames, Just One, Wavelength, plus two more party titles | 4 to 12+ |
| Co-op | C | [Pandemic](https://link.amazon/B0eHP5GCg), The Crew, Spirit Island, plus two more co-ops | 1 to 5 |
| 2-player and filler | D | [Patchwork](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FPXTLZ21?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), Jaipur, Hive, Sushi Go!, Love Letter | 2 to 8 |
| Campaign / legacy | Shared or rotating | Gloomhaven (bought as a group gift) | 1 to 4 |

Twenty-one games, every player count from two to a dozen covered, and not a single duplicate. Compare that to the uncoordinated version where each of those four friends buys Catan, Codenames, Pandemic, and two other hits: maybe eight distinct games and a dozen repeats, for the same money. We run that comparison in full in [Two Groups, Two Years, Same Money](/articles/two-groups-two-years/).

Notice that D took two lanes. Two-player games and fillers are both small, cheap, and portable, so pairing them is a natural fit. Campaign games are a special case we will come back to below.

## How do you assign lanes?

Lanes should follow two things: taste and hosting habits.

**Taste first.** Ask each person which kind of night they enjoy most. The person who always lobbies for the three-hour game is your strategy owner. The one who brings their partner and two coworkers is your party owner. The one who hates player elimination and loves a shared puzzle is your co-op owner. People buy more carefully in a lane they care about.

**Then hosting.** The person who hosts most often should own the lane with the biggest boxes, because the big boxes want to live where the games get played. Strategy and campaign games are heavy and come out at the host's table. Fillers and two-player games are small and travel well, so they can belong to the person who rarely hosts and usually arrives with a backpack.

A few practical rules for the conversation:

1. Everyone names a first and second choice.
2. Resolve conflicts by hosting: the more frequent host takes the bigger lane.
3. Pair small lanes (2-player with filler) so nobody is stuck with only tiny purchases.
4. Treat campaign and legacy games as a group buy or a rotating lane, since one copy serves one campaign and the box usually stays wherever the campaign is being played.
5. Write the assignments down. The audit template in [How to Audit Your Group's Game Shelf](/articles/audit-your-group-shelf/) has a column for it.

## What about games that do not fit a lane?

Some games straddle lanes. Is The Crew a co-op or a filler? Is [Azul](https://link.amazon/B0bGRYHex) a strategy game or a gateway? Do not overthink it. Put it in whichever lane's owner wants it most, and move on. The lanes are there to spread purchases out, not to classify the universe.

Expansions follow the base game. If the strategy owner has Wingspan and the group wants an expansion, the expansion goes to the strategy owner, ideally as a gift from the rest of the group. That is the whole idea behind [Gifting as Library Strategy](/articles/gifting-as-library-strategy/).

## How do you rebalance lanes over time?

Lanes are not permanent. Groups change, tastes change, and a lane that was exciting two years ago can go stale. Rebalance when any of these happen:

- **Someone joins.** A fifth member can take a lane that was shared or split a crowded lane in two.
- **Someone leaves.** Their lane and its games leave with them. The group decides whether to reassign the lane or let it go quiet for a while. See [The Shared Shelf Social Contract](/articles/shared-shelf-social-contract/) for the departure conversation.
- **A lane is overfull.** If the strategy shelf has twelve games and the group only plays four of them, the strategy owner should stop buying and the group should steer the next purchases to the thinnest lane.
- **A lane is underplayed.** If the campaign game stalled at session three, the group may not need a campaign lane right now. That is fine. Empty lanes cost nothing.

A good habit is to glance at the pooled shelf twice a year and ask one question: which lane would we reach for and come up empty? That lane gets the next purchase. Everything else can wait.

## Why Claim a Lane is really about trust

Claim a Lane works because it asks each person to buy for the group rather than for themselves. The party owner is buying games they will enjoy, but also games the group needs on the night ten people show up. That is a small act of trust in both directions: the owner trusts the group will play the games, and the group trusts the owner to choose well.

Over a few years, that trust builds a library no single person could have assembled. Twenty games, every player count, every mood, and four people who each know their corner of it cold.

The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### How do friends share a board game collection?

Treat the group's games as one library spread across several homes. Agree on the One Copy Rule so nobody buys duplicates, then assign each person a lane (strategy, party, co-op, two-player, campaign, filler) so purchases spread across genres and player counts. Keep a pooled list in the group chat and check it before buying.

### How many board games does a play group need?

Five or six well-chosen games cover most occasions for one person. A group of four following Claim a Lane can comfortably reach twenty or more distinct games over a couple of years without duplicates, covering everything from two-player nights to twelve-person parties. More than that is a bonus, not a requirement.

### What are the main categories of board games for a group collection?

The six lanes we use are strategy or euro games, party games for six or more, cooperative games, two-player games, campaign or legacy games, and fillers under twenty minutes. Every game night falls into one of those, so a shared shelf with something in each lane is ready for any night and any headcount.

### Who should buy the big board games in a play group?

The person who hosts most often. Big boxes want to live where the games get played, and strategy and campaign games tend to come out at the host's table. Smaller, portable games such as fillers and two-player titles suit the people who rarely host and usually arrive carrying a bag.
