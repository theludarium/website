Are board games an expensive hobby? Ask someone who just paid $80 for a big box and they will say yes. Ask them again a year later, after that box has carried a dozen game nights, and they will give you a different answer. The honest way to settle it is to measure board game cost per hour the same way you would measure any other evening out, and then put the numbers next to each other.

This is the third article in The Math of Game Night, a Ludarium series built on one formula: [cost per play](/articles/cost-per-play-explained/), which is price ÷ plays ÷ players ÷ hours. Here we take that formula out of the game room and point it at everything else you might do on a Saturday night.

## How much does a ballgame cost per hour?

Start with the one number we can state with confidence. A ballgame for a family of three runs about $130 to $135 once tickets, parking, and a round of concessions are in. The game itself lasts roughly three hours. That works out to about $45 per hour for the family, or about $15 per person per hour.

That is not a knock on ballgames. Three hours in the stands with your kids is a fine way to spend an afternoon, and nobody at the park is doing this math. But it gives us a yardstick. Fifteen dollars per person per hour is what a mainstream, in-person, out-of-the-house family activity costs. Hold that number.

## How does a board game compare?

Now run the same family of three through the formula with a board game. Take a game at about $40, which is where most family and strategy titles land in the US. Say the family plays it 20 times over a couple of years, an hour a session, and the same three people sit down each time. That is an illustration, not a survey, but the inputs are ordinary.

- $40 ÷ 20 plays = $2 per play
- $2 ÷ 3 players = about $0.67 per person per play
- $0.67 ÷ 1 hour = **about $0.67 per person per hour**

The ballgame cost about $15 per person per hour. The board game, in this illustration, costs under a dollar. And the board game's number keeps falling every time it comes back to the table, while the ballgame's number is fixed the moment you walk out of the stadium.

That is the difference between an experience you rent and one you own. The rented evening is paid in full each time. The owned evening is paid once and then amortized across every future play, which is why the [÷ plays and ÷ players steps](/articles/cost-per-play-explained/) matter so much more than the shelf price.

Two things jump out.

First, in-person events cluster between about $10 and $35 per person per hour. That is the price of a ticket to somewhere. Board games and streaming sit well under a dollar. That is the price of owning the entertainment and reusing it.

Second, streaming looks competitive with board games, and in pure dollars per hour it often is. The difference is in the people column. A streaming hour is usually one or two people, each half-watching, and nobody has to show up for it. A board game hour is a table of people who chose to be in the same room, facing each other, with phones down. We would argue that is a different product, but the table is honest enough to show that cheap-per-hour is not unique to cardboard.

## The $1 per hour hobbyist

Here is a real back-of-envelope figure from inside the hobby. One gamer tallied about $300 spent in a year on games and about 300 hours played. That is about $1 per hour, before dividing by anyone else at the table.

Think about what that number means. Three hundred hours is roughly six hours a week, every week, for a year. If that hobbyist's regular group is four people and the games are shared, the per-person figure drops toward a quarter. Compared to the ballgame's $15 per person per hour, the gap is somewhere between fifteen-fold and sixty-fold, depending on how you count.

And the $300 was not a stretch. Across the hobby, only about 6% of players spend under $100 a year on games, and about 22% spend over $1,000, so $300 is a middling budget. Even the heavy spenders at $1,000 a year would need to play fewer than 70 hours annually to match the ballgame's per-person rate, and most people who spend that much on games play far more than that. [How Much Do Board Gamers Actually Spend?](/articles/how-much-do-gamers-spend/) goes deeper on the bands.

## What makes the board game number so low?

Three things, and only one of them is the price tag.

- **Reuse.** A game is played again and again. Every event you buy a ticket for is consumed once.
- **Sharing.** The fun from one copy is distributed to everyone at the table at the same time. This is the whole premise of [The One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/): a group needs one copy, not one per person, because the ÷ players step is already doing the work. Four friends who pool their games under a [shared shelf](/articles/claim-a-lane/) each get a library four times the size of what they paid for.
- **Low ceiling on price.** Most new games cost $15 to $60, with family and strategy titles around $25 to $40. A single concert ticket can cost more than three board games, and the board games are still there next month.

Put those together and board games are among the cheapest in-person, social entertainment available, per hour, by a wide margin. The Magic: The Gathering Cube is the extreme case: one curated card pool, drafted by eight players, week after week, for years. Its cost per seat per hour becomes almost too small to bother computing.

## The catch: only if you play

Now the part that keeps this article honest. Every number above assumes the plays happen. A $40 game played 20 times costs about 67 cents per person per hour. The same game played zero times costs $40 for nothing, which is infinitely more per hour than the ballgame, the concert, and the streaming subscription combined.

This is the [Shelf of Shame](/articles/shelf-of-shame-infinite-cost/), and it is the only way to lose the comparison. A ticket to a ballgame at least guarantees the three hours. A board game guarantees nothing until someone opens the box, sets it up, and teaches it to a table of people who showed up.

So the board game's advantage is conditional. It is cheap per hour if, and only if, you have the people and the habit to turn plays into hours. Which means the real comparison is not board games versus ballgames at all. It is a play group versus no play group. The group is the asset. The games are just what the group does.

If you want the low number, the recipe is not complicated. Buy fewer games and play the ones you have. Share copies across the group instead of duplicating them. Pick games that fit the people you have, at the counts you have them, and that someone is willing to teach. Do that, and the hobby costs about what a cup of coffee costs, per person, per hour, with far better conversation. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### Are board games an expensive hobby?

Not per hour. A $40 game played 20 times by three people for an hour each costs about 67 cents per person per hour, and the figure falls with every additional play. One hobbyist's tally of about $300 spent and about 300 hours played works out to about $1 per hour before sharing with anyone else at the table.

### How much does a board game cost per hour?

It depends on plays, players, and session length, not just price. Divide the price by plays, then by players, then by hours. A $40 game played 20 times by 4 players for an hour each is about 50 cents per person per hour. A game played zero times has an infinite cost per hour.

### How does a board game compare to a ballgame in cost?

A ballgame for a family of three runs about $130 to $135 for roughly three hours, about $15 per person per hour. A $40 board game played 20 times by that same family costs under a dollar per person per hour, and the gap widens with every additional play because the game is reused rather than consumed.

### Is streaming cheaper than board games per hour?

In pure dollars per hour, streaming can be comparable or cheaper, since a monthly subscription spread over many hours costs well under a dollar an hour. The difference is social: a board game hour is a table of people together in one room, while a streaming hour is usually one or two people watching passively.

### What is the cheapest way to play board games?

Play before you pay, at game nights, cafés, or friends' tables. Then share one copy of each game across your play group instead of everyone buying their own, and buy only games your group will play at the player counts you actually have. The lowest cost per hour comes from plays, not from bargain prices.
