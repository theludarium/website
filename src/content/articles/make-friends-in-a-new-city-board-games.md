If you are trying to figure out how to make friends in a new city, the fastest route runs through a table with a board on it. Find the nearest game store or open game night, show up on the same night three weeks in a row, and say yes to whatever is being taught. Within a month you will know a dozen names. Within three, a few of them will be friends.

That is a strong claim. Here is why it holds up better than the usual advice about joining a gym or saying yes to things.

## Why is making friends in a new city so hard as an adult?

Sociologists who study friendship tend to point to three conditions, which Rebecca Adams of UNC Greensboro summarized for [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2012/07/15/fashion/the-challenge-of-making-friends-as-an-adult.html) as proximity, repeated unplanned interactions, and a setting that encourages people to let their guard down. School had all three. So did a first job with a big cohort. A move at 32 has none of them. You work from a laptop, your neighbors wave from their driveways, and every social contact has to be scheduled.

The numbers on how long friendship takes are sobering. One frequently cited [estimate from the University of Kansas](https://news.ku.edu/2018/03/06/study-reveals-number-hours-it-takes-make-friend) puts the threshold for a casual friend at roughly 50 hours of shared time, and a close friend at somewhere past 200. Nobody accumulates 200 hours over coffee dates. You need a structure that produces hours without each one being negotiated.

Most newcomer advice fails on this point. "Join a running club" works if you run. "Go to meetups" produces a room of people giving each other elevator pitches. "Say yes to everything" burns you out in six weeks. What you need is something that is repeated, low-pressure, and already full of people who want more people.

## Why board games are the fastest way to build a local circle

A game table solves the adult friendship problem almost by accident. Look at what it gives you.

- **A reason to be there.** You are not at a bar hoping someone talks to you. You are at a table because the game needs a fourth player. The awkward part is already handled.
- **A script.** Rules, turns, a goal. For two hours you know exactly what to do and say. Conversation happens in the gaps, which is where it happens best.
- **Repetition built in.** Game stores run the same open night every week. Groups meet on a schedule. You get the repeated contact without arranging it.
- **Low guard.** Something about a shared problem, whether it is a [Pandemic](https://link.amazon/B0eHP5GCg) outbreak or a bad [Catan](https://link.amazon/B0h8BDGR2) trade, drops the performance. People show you who they are when they lose a close one.
- **A gentle filter.** The people at a game night self-selected for sitting in a room with strangers for two hours. They are, almost by definition, open to new people.

Compare that to a networking event or a pickup basketball game. Those favor the loud and the skilled. A game table rewards showing up, paying attention, and being pleasant. Those are things anyone can do on their first week in town.

## Where to find a game group in a new city

Start with the places that already exist. In most American cities of any size there are more than you think.

1. **The friendly local game store.** Nearly every store runs an open board game night and a Magic: The Gathering or D&D night. Ask the person at the counter which night is best for someone new. They will tell you, and they will probably introduce you.
2. **Library game nights.** Many public libraries host monthly tabletop events. They skew family and all-ages, which is useful if you have moved with kids.
3. **Board game cafes and lounges.** A growing number of cities have a dedicated space with a library of games and staff who teach. Go alone, tell them you are new, and ask to join a table.
4. **Online listings for in-person groups.** Meetup, BoardGameGeek's local forums, and city subreddits all have standing groups. Pick one with a weekly cadence and recent activity.
5. **Your own apartment building or block.** A sign in the mailroom, "Game night Thursday, 7pm, unit 3B, all welcome," is braver than it sounds and works more often than you would guess.

A note on what to look for. Favor the group that meets weekly over the one that meets monthly. Favor open tables over closed campaigns. A D&D group that has been playing the same story for two years is a hard room to join. A [Codenames](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B014Q1XX9S?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) table is not.

## The 90-day plan for turning a game table into friends

Showing up once produces nothing. Here is a schedule that produces a circle.

**Weeks 1 to 3: Be the reliable stranger.** Pick one night and go every week. Same night, same place. Introduce yourself to whoever is running it and say you are new in town. Play whatever is being taught. Do not try to make friends yet. Try to become a face people expect.

**Weeks 4 to 6: Learn names and use them.** By now you recognize the regulars. Start greeting them by name. Ask one small follow-up each week: how was the trip, did the job thing work out. Bring one game you know well and offer to teach it. Teaching is the fastest way to become someone people want at the table.

**Weeks 7 to 9: Move one step off the schedule.** Invite two or three of the people you click with to something outside the group night. A different game store's event, a two-player game at a coffee shop, a bigger game at your place on a Saturday. This is the moment a group acquaintance becomes a personal one.

**Weeks 10 to 13: Start your own table.** You now know enough people to host. A standing night at your place, same time every week, does for your new circle what the store night did for you. There is a [whole playbook for running one that lasts](/articles/how-to-start-a-game-night-group/), and it works even better when the hosts are the ones who know what it felt like to be new.

By day 90 you have something most people do not have two years after a move: a weekly event, six or eight people who expect you there, and two or three you would call if something went wrong.

## Which games work best for meeting strangers?

Not every game is a good first-contact game. You want short teaches, lots of table talk, and no way for a new player to get crushed and humiliated.

| Goal | Games that do it well |
|---|---|
| Get a big group laughing fast | Codenames, [Wavelength](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07T446163?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Just One](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07GJ7NDZQ?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Telestrations](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0F699VTB2?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) |
| Learn how people think | [The Crew](https://link.amazon/B099S9RKf), Pandemic, [Forbidden Island](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B003D7F4YY?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) (cooperative) |
| Give quiet people a way in | [Azul](https://link.amazon/B0bGRYHex), [Cascadia](https://link.amazon/B0g1HYFt4), [Ticket to Ride](https://link.amazon/B0jh8RB7f) (low talk pressure, easy to learn) |
| Signal you are a regular | Bring [Wingspan](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07YQ641NQ?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) or Catan, know the rules cold, offer to teach |

Avoid bringing anything with a forty-minute rules explanation to an open night. Save [Gloomhaven](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01LZXVN4P?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) for month four. Avoid pure social deduction games like Werewolf as your very first play, since lying to strangers before they know your name is a strange foundation. Codenames gets you the talk without the deceit.

Cooperative games deserve a special mention for newcomers. Losing a game of Pandemic together is one of the fastest known routes from stranger to acquaintance. Shared failure bonds faster than shared victory, and nobody has to feel bad about beating the new person. It is also where [the stories a group retells for years](/articles/why-game-night-memories-last/) tend to start.

## What if you are shy, or not a "gamer"?

Good. Most people at a game table are not hobbyists either. They are nurses and teachers and engineers who like having a reason to sit with other people once a week.

Shyness is less of a problem at a table than almost anywhere else. The game gives you something to do with your hands and your attention. You can be quiet for twenty minutes and nobody notices, because everyone is looking at the board. When you do speak, it is about the game, which is easy. The personal stuff arrives on its own, usually around the third week, usually while someone is shuffling.

If the idea of walking into a room alone is the hard part, go on a teaching night and arrive five minutes early. Tell the organizer you are new and ask where to sit. Organizers live for this. You will be at a table with a name tag and a rules explanation before you have time to feel awkward.

## FAQ

### How long does it take to make friends in a new city?

With a weekly structure like a game night, expect familiar faces in three weeks, real acquaintances in six, and a couple of people you would call friends somewhere around three months.

### Is it weird to go to a board game night alone?

No. Most people at open game nights arrived alone at some point, and organizers are used to seating newcomers. Arrive a few minutes early and say you are new.

### What is the best board game for meeting new people?

Codenames. It handles any group size, teaches in two minutes, gets everyone talking, and has no way for a new player to be singled out for losing.

### How do I find board game groups near me?

Ask at the nearest game store, check the public library's events calendar, and search Meetup or BoardGameGeek's regional forums for groups that meet weekly.
