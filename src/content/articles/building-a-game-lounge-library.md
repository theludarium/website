A game lounge library is not a collection. It is a menu. The difference is that a collection grows by what the owner likes, and a menu is designed around who is going to sit down and what they will want in the first five minutes. We are planning The Ludarium's opening library at about 100 games, chosen by job rather than by hype, and this article lays out how we are choosing them. The specific titles will shift before we open. The structure will not.

## Why only 100 games?

Because a shelf of 600 games is a wall of noise to the person we are building for.

The hobbyist with 400 games at home is welcome, but they are not the design target. The target is the adult who has not played since college and walked in because a friend dragged them. That person needs a host to say, "You will like this one," and the host needs a library small enough to know every game cold.

A hundred well-chosen games covers every table we expect to seat: a couple on a date, a family with a nine-year-old, six coworkers, four strangers at public game night, and a campaign group that brings its own box. We wrote about the broader principle in [what makes a good board game cafe library](/articles/board-game-cafe-library/). The opening list is the smallest version that does every job.

We will add games after opening, slowly, and mostly by replacing something that never gets pulled off the shelf. A library should get better, not bigger.

Those numbers add to 100 and will drift. Notice what is heavy and what is light: a third of the library is games you can teach to anyone in under five minutes. That is deliberate. The first game of the night decides whether there is a second.

## Which games will we buy two copies of?

The ones that are always out. In every venue we have visited, the same handful of games are never on the shelf on a Friday night, and the host is apologizing for it.

Our current list for duplicates: Codenames, Ticket to Ride, Azul, The Crew, Wavelength, and Catan. <!-- AFFILIATE --> Possibly Wingspan. We would rather own two copies of a game that gets played every night than one copy of ten games that get played twice a year.

Duplicates also let us run learn-to-play nights where three tables play the same game at once, which is the single best way we know to turn walk-ins into regulars.

## What will we not stock, and why?

A few categories are out on purpose.

**Games that take 45 minutes to teach.** If a game cannot be taught in under 15 minutes by a good host, it does not belong in a walk-in library, however good it is. The heavy shelf is the exception, and those are reservation-only with a host who knows the game.

**Games with a hundred fiddly pieces that walk away.** A library copy of a game with tiny unique components is a library copy with pieces missing within a month. We are leaning toward games that survive public use, and buying component bags and inserts for the ones that do not. The accessories question is worth its own article.

**Games that only one of us loves.** This is the hardest rule. Every one of us has a favorite nobody else wants to play. Those stay at home. The library is for the members.

**Trading card games as library stock.** Members can and will bring their own decks and run cube drafts, and we will give them tables and a cabinet. But we are not stocking retail product, which brings us to a rule that matters more than any title: we do not sell games. That gets its own article in [why we won't sell games](/articles/why-game-lounges-dont-sell-games/).

## How do we decide what goes on the shelf and what gets cut?

By watching the shelf.

We plan to track two things from day one: how often each game gets pulled, and how often a table finishes it. A game that gets pulled and abandoned is worse than a game that never gets pulled, because it cost a table its evening. After six months, the bottom of the list gets replaced.

We also plan to ask members directly, with a board on the wall and a line in the newsletter. "What do you want us to add?" and "What have you never seen anyone play?" Members are better at curating a library than owners, because they are the ones using it.

Finally, the hosts get a vote that counts double. A host who can teach a game with real enthusiasm will get it played ten times more than a better game they teach with a shrug. Hire the teacher, then stock what they love to teach.

## What does it cost to build a 100-game library?

We have not bought it yet, so we will not pretend to a number. At the retail prices on this list, which run from about $15 for a small card game to $100 and up for the big boxes, 100 games plus duplicates and inserts would land in the low five figures at the time of writing, but publisher pricing, used copies, and donations change that a lot. <!-- EDITOR: add detail once purchased -->

What we can say is that the library is one of the smallest costs of opening a lounge. The furniture costs more. The lease costs far more. The library gets the attention because it is the fun part, and because it is the thing every visitor sees first. But a modest library with a great host beats a huge library with nobody to teach it, every night of the week.

## Will we take donations?

Yes, with a filter. Plenty of people have games they do not play and would rather see used. We will take donations of games on our target list or close to it, in good condition, with all pieces. We will decline, kindly, the box of 1990s trivia games from the garage. The library is a menu, and a menu has to say no.

## FAQ

### How many games does a game cafe need?

Enough to cover every kind of table it seats, which for most venues is 100 to 300. A smaller, well-taught library serves newcomers better than a large one nobody can navigate.

### What are the best games for a game lounge library?

Games that teach in minutes and play in under an hour do most of the work: Codenames, Ticket to Ride, Azul, The Crew, and Wavelength are the kind of titles that never stay on the shelf. Deeper games serve regulars but should be a minority.

### Should a game cafe buy duplicate copies?

Yes, for the handful of games that are always in use. Two copies of a game played nightly are worth more than ten games played twice a year.

### Can I bring my own games to The Ludarium?

Yes. Members can bring their own games and, at higher tiers, store a campaign box or deck on site. The library is there for the nights you do not.
