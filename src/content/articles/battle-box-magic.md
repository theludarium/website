A battle box is a single shuffled stack of spells that two players both draw from, plus a set of lands that sits outside the game. There is no draft and no deck. You sit down, each take your ten lands, draw four cards from the shared pile, and play. Setup is under a minute, which makes battle box the fastest Magic format there is and one of the most underrated things a cube owner can build.

The format's best-known champion is Brian DeMars, who named his own box the Danger Room and wrote about it for [Star City Games in June 2014](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/the-danger-room/), noting that Hall of Famer Ben Stark had praised the format and said he preferred it to cube drafting. Those articles are still the starting point. This one covers the rules, then the construction decisions that separate a box that gets played for years from one that gets played twice.

## How does a battle box work?

The rules fit on an index card, and most groups adjust one or two of them:

- **Shared library.** All the nonland cards are shuffled into one pile, usually 100 to 200 cards. Both players draw from it. Your graveyard is your own.
- **Land stack.** Each player has an identical set of ten lands kept outside the game. DeMars's original is five basics and five tapped allied duals (the Invasion-era Coastal Tower cycle); other boxes use the enemy pairs instead. You may put one land from your stack onto the battlefield each turn, as your land drop.
- **Opening hand.** Four cards, no mulligans, because every hand has lands. Some boxes use five. The player going first does not draw on turn one.
- **Life.** Twenty, standard.

That is it. Because land drops are guaranteed, nobody floods or screws, and every game reaches turn five with both players casting spells. Because the library is shared, the box's design is your deck's design, and that is where the interesting work is.

The [Draftsim overview](https://draftsim.com/battle-box-mtg/) collects several public lists and their sizes: DeMars's own has grown past 650 cards, Anthony Mattox's sits at 146, and the multiplayer variants it lists run about 200. For a first box, 150 is plenty and fits in a single deck box with the lands.

## What makes a card good in a battle box?

The format changes card evaluation more than any cube does, because of two facts: every player has perfect mana, and every card you draw was chosen for the whole pool rather than for a deck.

**Perfect mana rewards the five-color card.** A player always has access to five colors by turn five, with the tapped duals and basics in the stack. That makes off-color kicker cards (the Invasion Battlemages, Agonizing Demise with its red kicker) and modal spells (the Commands, the Charms, split cards like Fire // Ice) the best cards in the box. They are never dead, and the decision of which mode to pick is the game.

**No deck means no synergy packages.** A Spirit lord in a battle box is a bear with a line of text that triggers once a month. Cards must stand alone. Good battle box cards are rate-plus-decision: a 3/3 for three with a relevant choice attached, not a 2/2 that is a 5/5 in the right deck.

**Shared library breaks several mechanics.** Tutors find a card for you from a pile that is also your opponent's deck, which is strange but mostly fine. Mill is pointless. Scry and surveil, in quantity, let one player sculpt both players' draws. Land search finds nothing. Most boxes cut all of it and keep perhaps one or two scry effects at low density.

DeMars's stated target is that most cards sit at the power of an uncommon you would happily first-pick in a booster draft. A box built out of your cube's strongest 150 cards will be a worse battle box than one built from the uncommon layer of a peasant cube.

## How do you build a 150-card battle box?

Start with the land stack, because it decides your color commitments. Ten lands per player: five basics plus five duals is the default. If you run ally-color duals (the gain lands, or the Guildgates, both cheap), you have made the box slightly easier to play two allied colors than two enemy colors, and your spell choices should accept that. | Tricks and modal spells | 15 to 20 | Charms, Commands, kicker spells, combat tricks |
| Card advantage | 8 to 12 | Divination-rate draw, small value creatures |
| Artifacts and Equipment | 5 to 8 | A Bonesplitter, a mana rock or two, no bombs |
| Sweepers and bombs | 2 to 4 | One per color at most; several boxes run zero sweepers |

Color balance should be even or close to it: 25 to 30 cards per color plus a small gold section. Gold cards are fine here because mana is perfect; DeMars notes they make for swingy, memorable turns. Keep them to the ten two-color pairs rather than three-color cards, which still take until turn six or seven to cast.

Card examples that earn a slot in almost any box: Kitchen Finks, Flametongue Kavu, Man-o'-War, Putrid Leech, Boros Charm, Kolaghan's Command, Lightning Helix, Mulldrifter, and Shriekmaw (evoke is a mode). Each of those is a decision as well as a body. Cards that look similar but fail: Sakura-Tribe Elder (land search finds nothing) and Mortician Beetle (needs a deck around it).

## What should you keep out?

The battle box community has converged on a list of things that make games worse, and nearly all of them come from the perfect-mana rule:

- **Mana acceleration and land destruction.** Both break the one-land-per-turn promise, which is the format's whole balance mechanism. Zero ramp, zero Stone Rain.
- **Cheap unconditional removal in quantity.** With no deck to protect, a box full of Swords to Plowshares becomes a game of trading one-for-one until someone draws a bomb. Conditional removal (Last Gasp, Electrolyze, Journey to Nowhere) keeps creatures mattering.
- **Token swarms.** A Hordeling Outburst is fine; a Secure the Wastes box stalls. Keep token makers light.
- **Bombs that end the game alone.** One Grave Titan in 150 cards means one game in forty is decided by who drew it. A box with none is more fun than a box with four.
- **Tutors, mill, scry, and surveil.** All interact badly with the shared library.

The opposite instinct, "put in only fair cards," has a failure mode too: a box of vanilla creatures and Shocks is boring in a week. The fix is decisions, not power. A card like Fire // Ice is weak by cube standards and one of the best cards in any battle box.

## How is a battle box different from a cube?

A cube is a draft environment; the drafting and the building are most of the game. A battle box deletes both and keeps only the play. That is a trade, and for some nights it is the right one:

- Two players, twenty minutes, no table space for a draft: battle box.
- A new player who can follow combat but not a 45-card pick sequence: battle box.
- Eight friends with an evening: cube, and we have written about [running one](/articles/how-to-run-a-cube-draft/).

The two can live in the same box. A 150-card battle box pulls easily from the commons and uncommons a cube cut last year, and a Winston draft of a battle box works fine when two players want a little deckbuilding back.

## FAQ

### How many cards do you need for a battle box?

Between 100 and 200 nonland cards is typical, plus ten lands per player. 150 is a comfortable first build.

### How many lands do you use in battle box?

Ten per player, kept outside the game: usually five basics and five dual lands, one played per turn as a land drop.

### Can you play battle box with more than two players?

Yes. Multiplayer boxes run about 200 cards and often add Commander-set cards for free-for-all or Battlebond cards for Two-Headed Giant. Draws from a shared pile get thinner with four players, so build bigger.

### Is battle box good for new players?

It is one of the best ways to teach Magic, because mana problems are gone and every hand is playable. The player learns combat and sequencing without needing to build a deck first.

## One pile, one minute, play

A battle box is a cube owner's second box: the one that lives in a backpack and comes out when the draft falls through. The game is the reason. The people are the point.
