The best board games for 5 year olds give the child a real decision every turn and give the adult something to think about besides waiting. Outfoxed!, Dragomino, Rhino Hero, My First Castle Panic, and Animal Upon Animal all do that. They play in fifteen to twenty minutes, nobody needs to read, and a parent on the third play of the evening is still choosing rather than going through the motions.

That last point is the whole article. There are hundreds of games a five-year-old can play. There are far fewer a five-year-old and a tired adult can both enjoy, and those are the ones that get played enough to matter.

## What can a 5-year-old actually handle in a board game?

More than Candy Land assumes, less than Catan Junior requires. Here is a realistic picture of a typical five-year-old at a table, with the usual caveat that children vary a lot at this age.

- **Reading:** Assume none. Games should run on pictures, colors, and shapes.
- **Counting:** Reliable to about ten. Dice with pips work; dice with numerals mostly work; adding two dice is a stretch.
- **Turn-taking:** Fine, as long as turns are short. A minute is long. Two is an eternity.
- **Decisions:** One real choice per turn is ideal. "Which of these two tiles do you want?" works. "Plan three turns ahead" does not.
- **Attention:** Fifteen to twenty minutes of focused play. After that the game becomes a toy, which is fine, but the game is over.
- **Losing:** Hard. This is the age when it is hardest, and it gets better with practice. We cover it in [how to teach kids to lose gracefully](/articles/teaching-kids-to-lose-at-games/).

Also assume they will want to touch everything. Pick games with pieces that survive being handled, dropped, and occasionally chewed.

## Why do most preschool games bore adults?

Because they remove decisions to remove frustration. Candy Land and Chutes and Ladders have zero choices. You draw a card or spin, and you move. The child cannot make a mistake, which was the design goal, but the adult is a spectator to a random process.

The better design, which a German publisher called HABA has leaned on for decades, is to keep the decision and shrink its consequences. In Animal Upon Animal, you choose which wooden animal to stack and where. If it falls, you take a couple of animals back. The choice is real. The penalty is small. A five-year-old feels agency and a parent feels engaged.

Cooperative games solve the problem a different way. In Outfoxed!, everyone works together to catch the fox before it escapes, so the adult can talk through the reasoning out loud without it being "helping the kid win." You are just playing.

## How do you teach a board game to a 5-year-old?

Do not explain the rules. Play the first turn out loud.

Set the game up, take the first turn yourself, and narrate what you are doing and why in one sentence. "I'm putting the pig on top because it has a flat back." Then hand them the pieces. Children at this age learn by imitation far faster than by instruction, and they will copy you almost exactly.

A few more things that help:

- **Skip the scoring the first time.** Play Dragomino once just matching tiles. Add the egg scoring on the second play.
- **Let them handle the setup.** Sorting tiles and placing pieces is half the fun at five and buys you two minutes to look at the rules.
- **Ask, do not tell.** "Which one do you want?" beats "You should take the blue one." Even when the blue one is clearly right.
- **End on a high.** When they say "again!" play again. When they wander off mid-game, let the game end. No pursuit.

## Should you let a 5-year-old win?

Sometimes, and never obviously. Most games on this list have enough luck that a five-year-old wins a fair share without help. In Outfoxed! and the other cooperative games, the question does not come up.

In a pure skill game, a parent who plays at full strength will win every time, and a child who loses every time stops asking to play. The fix is not to throw games. It is to pick games with enough luck that the outcome is open, and to play the dexterity games with your off hand once in a while. Nobody has to know.

Where it gets harder is the child who needs to win every single time and melts down otherwise. That is a separate situation with its own approach, which we cover in [how to play games with a kid who has to win](/articles/kid-who-hates-losing/).

## What should you skip?

- **Candy Land, Chutes and Ladders, Hi Ho! Cherry-O.** No decisions. If you already own them, fine. Do not buy them.
- **Anything with "Junior" on a game you have not played.** Some are excellent (Catan Junior, Ticket to Ride: First Journey). Some are a license on a spinner. Check that there is a choice on each turn.
- **Trivia games for kids.** A five-year-old's knowledge is not the point of play and a quiz turns the table into a test.
- **Long games.** Even a great forty-minute game is a bad fit at five. Wait a year or two.

## FAQ

### What is the best first board game for a 5-year-old?

Outfoxed! is the safest first pick. It is cooperative, so nobody loses alone, and the deduction is interesting enough for the adult.

### Can a 5-year-old play Ticket to Ride?

Not the full game, but Ticket to Ride: First Journey is designed for roughly ages six and up and many five-year-olds manage it with a parent next to them.

### How long should a board game be for a 5-year-old?

Ten to twenty minutes. Short enough to play twice, short enough to stop before anyone melts down.

### Are cooperative games better for young kids?

They are easier, especially for the first year of playing. Mix in competitive games with lots of luck so losing becomes normal and small.
