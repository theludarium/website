Purpose in retirement is usually discussed as a problem to solve with a project: a second career, a nonprofit board, a book. Those work for some people. For many, they replace one kind of obligation with another and miss what was actually lost. What most people lose at retirement is not a mission. It is a place to be expected, people who notice when they are gone, and a role that is theirs.

This article makes the case that play, done as a practice with the same people, supplies those things, and that the research on purpose and aging supports taking it seriously. It is not an argument against the second career. It is an argument that the Tuesday table counts too.

## What does "purpose" actually mean in the research?

Less than the word suggests, and that is useful.

Researchers who study purpose in later life generally measure it with questions about whether a person feels their life has direction, whether they have things they look forward to, and whether their daily activities feel worthwhile. Nothing in those questions requires a grand cause. A person who looks forward to Thursday euchre and would be missed if they skipped it scores well.

The findings that get cited most come from large cohort studies. A [2019 analysis](https://hrs.isr.umich.edu/bibcite/reference/14582) of the Health and Retirement Study, published in JAMA Network Open, followed nearly 7,000 American adults over 50 and found that those with the lowest sense of purpose had more than twice the mortality risk over about four years compared with those with the highest. [Patrick Hill and Nicholas Turiano](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/having-a-sense-of-purpose-in-life-may-add-years-to-your-life.html) found that purpose predicted lower mortality over 14 years in more than 6,000 adults of all ages, and [Eric Kim and colleagues](https://hrs.isr.umich.edu/bibcite/reference/11201) have associated higher purpose with lower odds of heart attack and stroke and more use of preventive health care. These are associations, and the researchers say so. People with purpose may also be healthier for other reasons. But the pattern is consistent enough that purpose is now treated as a legitimate health variable.

A second line of work gives a mechanism. Purpose seems to work partly through behavior. People who have a reason to get up tend to get up, move, eat, sleep on a schedule, and keep appointments. The purpose does not need to be large. It needs to be regular.

## Why does retirement hit purpose so hard?

Because work quietly supplied four things at once, and retirement removes all four on the same day.

| What work provided | What usually replaces it | What play can replace it with |
|---|---|---|
| A schedule | Nothing, at first | A standing weekly game |
| People who expect you | Family, if nearby | A group that saves your seat |
| A role and competence | Hobbies done alone | Being the one who knows the rules |
| Something to look forward to | Travel, occasionally | Next Thursday |

Notice that the losses are structural, not emotional. Most retirees are not grieving their job. They are missing the scaffolding it built around their week. Advice to "find your passion" does not rebuild scaffolding. A standing commitment does.

Friendship compounds the problem. For many adults, especially men, the workplace was the last setting in which they saw the same people repeatedly. Retirement ends that, and the friendships fade with it. [Why friendships fade with age](/articles/why-friendships-fade-with-age/) covers that mechanism. [Friendship after retirement](/articles/friendship-after-retirement/) covers the rebuild.

## How does play supply purpose?

By giving back the four things in the table, in a form that is low-stakes enough to actually keep.

**A schedule.** The weekly game is a fixed point. People organize the rest of the week around it: the doctor's appointment on Wednesday so Thursday is clear.

**People who expect you.** A group of four notices an empty chair immediately. Being missed is one of the most underrated forms of mattering. The [Harvard Study of Adult Development](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2023/02/work-out-daily-ok-but-how-socially-fit-are-you), running since 1938, found that satisfaction in close relationships in midlife predicted health and happiness at 80 better than cholesterol or blood pressure at 50, and its directors [argue](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/1180213004) that those relationships have to be actively tended to last.

**A role.** This is where games are better than most alternatives. A game group needs someone to keep the scorebook, someone to teach newcomers, someone to host, someone to remember the obscure rule. Those roles are real, they are earned, and they last. The retired engineer who runs the Tuesday bridge table has a job again, and it is one he chose.

**Something to look forward to.** A good game produces anticipation. Who will show up, whether the new strategy works, whether the standings change. That forward pull is exactly what purpose researchers measure.

There is a fifth thing play adds that work rarely did: it is fun. Purpose that is a burden does not last. Purpose that is also a pleasure does.

## What does purposeful play look like in practice?

It looks like a few deliberate choices, not a reinvention.

1. **Join or start one standing group.** Weekly, same people, same game or rotation. Everything else is extra. If you need the steps, [how to start a game night group](/articles/how-to-start-a-game-night-group/) has them.
2. **Take a role in it.** Keep the scorebook. Be the one who teaches. Host once a month. Purpose comes from being needed, and groups need people.
3. **Teach someone younger.** A grandchild, a neighbor's kid, a new member at the lounge. Transmitting a game is a form of legacy that costs nothing and lands immediately.
4. **Build toward something.** A league with standings, a tournament in the spring, a campaign game like Pandemic Legacy that takes a year to finish. Long arcs give the weeks direction.
5. **Keep learning.** Add one new game a season. The [Lothian Birth Cohort in Scotland](https://www.ed.ac.uk/research-innovation/latest-research-news/games-can-protect-thinking-skills-in-older-age) found that people who increased their game playing in their 70s showed less cognitive decline over the decade, though the researchers called the association small and did not claim cause. Novelty appears to matter.
6. **Hold the open chair.** A group with a role for newcomers has a mission built in: finding the next person who needs a Thursday.

None of this excludes the second career or the nonprofit board. It sits under them. People who have a table to come back to tend to do the big projects better, because the big projects are not carrying the whole weight of mattering.

## What if play feels unserious?

Many people raised to equate worth with productivity feel this, and it is worth answering directly.

The research does not distinguish between purpose that looks impressive and purpose that does not. It measures whether your days feel worthwhile and whether you have things to look forward to. A standing game with people who would miss you meets both tests. So does a garden, a choir, a volunteer shift. The common thread is regularity, people, and a role. Play simply happens to be the version with the lowest barrier and the most built-in structure.

And if the sense of pointlessness after retirement is heavy, lasts for months, or comes with changes in sleep, appetite, or interest in everything, that is worth a conversation with a doctor. Low purpose and depression overlap, and the second one is treatable. A game is a good habit to have alongside that help. It is not a substitute for it.

The game is the reason. The people are the point. In the second half of life, that sentence is also a decent definition of purpose.

## FAQ

### How do you find purpose in retirement?

Rebuild what work provided: a schedule, people who expect you, a role, and something to look forward to. A standing weekly commitment with the same group, where you have a job to do, supplies all four.

### Is a sense of purpose linked to living longer?

Large cohort studies have associated higher purpose in adults over 50 with lower mortality and lower cardiovascular risk. The research is observational, but the pattern is consistent across studies.

### Can hobbies give you purpose after retirement?

Yes, especially social hobbies done regularly with the same people. Solitary hobbies help with enjoyment; group hobbies with a role in them do more for the sense of being needed.

### What activities give retirees a sense of purpose?

Volunteering, mentoring, teaching, caring for grandchildren, and running or belonging to a regular group such as a game night, choir, or club. The regularity and the people matter more than the activity.
