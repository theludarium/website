The cube primer archetypes section is the part drafters actually use. They skim the introduction, nod at the house rules, and then read the archetype list twice: once before their first draft, and again after they lose with a deck that was not a deck. If this section is good, your cube teaches itself. If it is bad, the regulars teach it, badly, by word of mouth.

The format we use is one paragraph per lane, built from four pieces: the plan in one sentence, two or three signpost cards, the curve or count that makes it work, and what beats it. This article explains each piece, shows worked examples, and lists the mistakes that turn the section into a wall.

## What is a lane, and how many should the section list?

A lane is a deck the cube is built to support, not a deck that happened once. The test: could a drafter who committed to it at pick three expect to end with a playable deck in most eight-player drafts? If yes, it is a lane and it gets a paragraph. If it needs two specific cards to work, it is a combo, and it gets a line in the FAQ instead.

Most 540-card cubes support the ten two-color pairs plus two to four lanes that cross colors or sit inside one: mono-red aggro, five-color green, a Reanimator plan that is mostly black with a splash. That is twelve to fourteen paragraphs, and it is the ceiling. A 360-card cube supports fewer, and the section should say so rather than pretend. The reasoning on how many lanes a given size can carry is in [archetype density](/articles/archetype-density/).

Do not list a lane you wish were there. If white-blue in your cube is a pile of good cards with no plan, write "white-blue: good-stuff control, no dedicated support" and move on. Honesty here saves a drafter a bad night.

## What goes in each paragraph?

Four pieces, in this order, in four to six sentences.

**The plan.** One sentence that tells the drafter what the deck does to win. "Curve out with cheap creatures and finish with burn." "Make two or three tokens a turn and go wide." "Stall the ground, draw extra cards, and win with one big threat." If the plan takes two sentences, the lane may be two lanes.

**The signposts.** Two or three cards that are both strong in the deck and weak outside it. A signpost is a card whose presence in a pack tells the drafter the lane is open. Gold cards do this naturally; a cheap two-color uncommon that only one deck wants is the clearest signal in the cube. Name them by exact card name, so a drafter can search the list.

**The number that matters.** Every lane has one count a drafter should hit. For aggro it is one-drops and two-drops, often twelve or more creatures at one and two mana in a 23-spell deck. For a tokens deck it is the ratio of makers to payoffs. For control it is removal plus card draw, and the number of finishers, usually two or three. Give the number and say it is a target, not a law.

**The weakness.** One sentence on what beats this deck in your cube. Aggro loses to lifegain and sweepers. Tokens loses to a single well-timed sweeper. Control loses to a fast clock plus a counterspell on its one big spell. This line is the most useful one in the paragraph for the regular who keeps losing with the deck and cannot see why.

## Worked examples

Here are three paragraphs in the format, written for a generic unpowered 540-card cube. The cards named are real and common in cubes at that power; swap them for your own signposts.

**Red-white aggro.** Curve out with one- and two-mana creatures, attack every turn, and burn the last points. Signposts: Figure of Destiny, Boros Charm, and any creature with haste at two mana. Aim for at least twelve creatures at one and two mana and no more than two cards above three. It loses to repeatable lifegain, a sweeper on turn four, and your own hand of four lands and three one-drops on turn seven.

**Blue-black reanimator.** Put a huge creature in the graveyard early and return it for less than it costs. Signposts: Animate Dead, Entomb if your cube runs it, and Griselbrand as the usual target. You need three to four reanimation spells, three to four targets, and six or more ways to discard or mill a card you control. It loses to graveyard hate, a counterspell on the one reanimation spell you drew, and drawing the targets in your hand with nothing to do about it.

**Green-white tokens.** Make more creatures than the opponent can block, then pump them all at once. Signposts: Intangible Virtue, Call of the Herd, and the overrun effects your list runs. Count ten or more token makers and two or three anthem or overrun payoffs. It loses to a single sweeper with nothing held back, and to an opponent who ignores the board and races in the air.

Each is under a hundred words. A drafter can read all twelve in five minutes, which is the point.

## How do you order the lanes?

Three options, and the choice depends on the reader.

1. **By color pair in WUBRG order.** White-blue, white-black, white-red, white-green, blue-black, and so on. Standard, searchable, and matches how CubeCobra sorts. Best for a general cube.
2. **By speed.** Aggro lanes first, midrange, then control, then combo. Best for a cube with a strong tempo identity, because a first-timer can read the top three and have a plan.
3. **By how supported they are.** The deepest lanes first, the thin ones last, with the last ones flagged. Best for a cube with uneven support, because it tells the truth about which lanes are safe for a new drafter.

Whatever you choose, use the same card-name capitalization and the same four-piece order in every paragraph, so a reader who learns the shape of one can skim the rest.

## What mistakes make the section unreadable?

- **Listing every playable card per lane.** Three signposts, not thirty. The list is on CubeCobra; link to a tag or filter if you want to show the whole lane.
- **Writing a pick order.** Pick orders are context-dependent and go stale with every update. Signposts and the number that matters do the same job without expiring.
- **Describing decks that are not supported.** Every "sometimes you can" paragraph dilutes the ones that are true.
- **Flavor over function.** "A grinding, attrition-based deck that rewards patience" tells a drafter less than "trade one-for-one, then win with a planeswalker."
- **No weakness line.** A lane with no stated weakness reads as the owner's pet deck, and often is.

## How does the section change with cube size and theme?

A 360-card cube should list eight to ten lanes and say outright that two or three of the color pairs are thin. A 720-card cube might list sixteen, and the section becomes long enough to need a table of contents at the top. A themed cube often has lanes that are not color pairs at all, such as "artifact aggro" or "spells matter," and those get the same four-piece paragraph with the theme in the plan sentence.

Pauper and peasant cubes deserve one extra line per lane: the one card at the rarity that makes it work, because readers coming from higher-power cubes will not recognize the engine. ## FAQ

### How many archetypes should a cube primer list?

As many as the cube supports, usually ten to fourteen for 540 cards and eight to ten for 360. Do not list lanes that need two specific cards to work.

### What is a signpost card in a cube primer?

A card that is strong in one lane and weak outside it, so seeing it in a pack tells a drafter the lane is open. Two or three per archetype is enough.

### Should the archetype section include a pick order?

No. It goes stale with every update and teaches memorization rather than reading the table. Give signposts and a target count instead.

### How long should each archetype description be?

Four to six sentences, under a hundred words: the plan, the signposts, the number that matters, and what beats it.

## Twelve paragraphs, read twice

A drafter who reads your archetype section before their first draft and again after their first loss has learned the cube faster than a season of play would teach them. That is what the section is for. The game is the reason. The people are the point.
