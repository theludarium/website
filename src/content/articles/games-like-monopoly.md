The best games like [Monopoly](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00CV5PN2W?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) keep what people love about it, buying property, collecting rent, and making deals, and drop the part where the game never ends. For the same feel in 15 minutes, play [Monopoly Deal](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CPXCDG65?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [For Sale](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B001PQLNY0?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), or [Machi Koro](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07HKZ4BH6?tag=cubecommonw0a-20). For the trading and table talk, play [Bohnanza](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000MLZ516?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Catan](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DYK1ZH2D?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), or [Sheriff of Nottingham](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B007EZMABG?tag=cubecommonw0a-20). For the property and money, done with more choices, play [Chinatown](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00IVF4ZH0?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Acquire](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0D7MSX6PR?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), or [Modern Art](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0716P8GKX?tag=cubecommonw0a-20). And for a family ready for a bigger evening, [Splendor](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00IZEUFIA?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) and [Power Grid](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07HM7WZT9?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) turn money into a real engine.

**The quick answer:** for most families, start with **Monopoly Deal** (the same sets, rent, and steals in a small card box) or **Machi Koro** (roll dice, buy buildings, collect income, done in about half an hour). For a family that loves the wheeling and dealing, **Bohnanza** puts a trade on every turn.

Below are 11 picks, sorted by what you like about Monopoly, with a comparison table, a "which one first" guide, and a plain note on how each one differs.

## Why Monopoly drags (and it is not all Monopoly's fault)

Monopoly is not a bad game to love. It is how millions of people first learned that a board game could have money, deals, and a winner who earned it. It comes from Lizzie Magie's The Landlord's Game, patented in 1904, and Parker Brothers first published Monopoly in 1935. It has been on family shelves ever since.

So why do so many games of it end with a shrug and the box going back on the shelf half finished? A lot of it comes from house rules that most families play without knowing they are house rules:

1. **Free Parking money.** Many families put taxes and fees in the middle and pay them out to whoever lands on Free Parking. The official rules do not do this. Free Parking is just a free space. The jackpot keeps putting cash back into the game, so nobody goes broke and the game keeps going.
2. **Skipping the auction.** In the official rules, if you land on a property and decide not to buy it, the bank auctions it to everyone. Most families skip this. Without auctions, properties sell slowly and it takes much longer for anyone to build a monopoly.
3. **Player elimination.** Even with the official rules, Monopoly ends when everyone but one player is bankrupt. Whoever goes out first spends the rest of the evening watching. Our piece on [catch-up mechanics](/articles/catch-up-mechanics-board-games/) explains why that slow, visible collapse feels so rough, and the guide to [the kid who hates losing](/articles/kid-who-hates-losing/) has tips for younger players.

Try the official rules once and you may like Monopoly more. But if your family wants the same feel with a clear finish, the games below are built for it.

## What to look for in a game like Monopoly

When people say they want "something like Monopoly," they usually mean one or more of these:

- **Owning things that pay you.** A property that earns rent every time someone lands on it is a small engine. Our guide to [engine building games](/articles/engine-building-games/) traces that idea from Monopoly to modern games.
- **Money that matters.** Cash you count, spend, and save, with a real choice about when to use it.
- **Deals.** Trades, offers, and "I'll give you this if you give me that."
- **A clear end.** This is the part Monopoly is missing. Every game on this list ends at a set point, so the last half hour is not just waiting.

## If you want Monopoly, but quick

These three keep the feeling closest to Monopoly and fit in a short evening, or before dinner.

### Monopoly Deal

Monopoly Deal is a card game made under Hasbro's Monopoly license and first released in 2008. It plays 2 to 5 in about 15 minutes. [Monopoly Deal](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CPXCDG65?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) uses the same color sets and property names as the board game, plus money cards and action cards for rent, deals, and steals. The first player with three complete property sets wins.

**How it differs:** there is no board and no dice, and nobody goes bankrupt. You still charge rent and you still steal a property right out from under someone, but the race to three sets keeps the game short. It is the easiest pick for a family that already knows Monopoly, since the colors and the names are familiar.

### For Sale

Designed by Stefan Dorra and first published in 1997, For Sale plays 3 to 6 in about 20 to 30 minutes. In [For Sale](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B001PQLNY0?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), the first half is an auction for houses, from a cardboard box to a space station. In the second half, everyone sells their houses for checks, using the houses as their bids.

**How it differs:** it is the real estate part of Monopoly, boiled down to two quick rounds of bidding. It teaches in two minutes and works for kids who can count. We cover it as a tool for teaching kids the value of a deal in our guide to [negotiation board games](/articles/negotiation-board-games/).

### Machi Koro

Designed by Masao Suganuma and first published in Japan in 2012, Machi Koro plays 2 to 4 in about 30 minutes in the current Pandasaurus edition. In [Machi Koro](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07HKZ4BH6?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), you roll dice and buy buildings for your little city. When the dice show a number that matches one of your buildings, it pays out, sometimes even on other players' turns.

**How it differs:** this is the closest feeling to Monopoly's "roll, land, get paid," but you are building your own town instead of fighting over one board. The game ends when someone builds all of their landmarks, so there is a clear finish line and nobody is knocked out early. It is a fine step for kids who have outgrown roll-and-move games.

## If you love the dealing

For many families, the best part of Monopoly is the trade that makes the whole table groan. These games put that moment at the center.

### Bohnanza

Designed by Uwe Rosenberg and first published by Amigo in 1997, Bohnanza plays 2 to 7 in 30 to 60 minutes. In [Bohnanza](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000MLZ516?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), you plant bean cards in your fields and harvest them for coins. You have to play cards in the order they sit in your hand, so you trade away the beans you cannot use, and anyone can trade with the active player.

**How it differs:** there is no board and no property, but every single turn is a trading round. If your favorite Monopoly moment is "I'll give you Boardwalk if you give me both railroads," Bohnanza is that, all game long. It also plays up to seven, which helps at a holiday table.

### Catan

Designed by Klaus Teuber and first published in 1995, [Catan](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DYK1ZH2D?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) plays 3 to 4 in about 60 to 120 minutes, with an extension for 5 and 6. You collect wood, brick, sheep, wheat, and ore from dice rolls, trade them with each other, and spend them on roads, settlements, and cities. The first player to 10 points wins.

**How it differs:** Catan is the game most people play after Monopoly, and for good reason: it keeps the dice and the trading but nobody is eliminated, and the game ends at 10 points. Our list of [games like Catan](/articles/games-like-catan/) picks up where this one leaves off. If you want to get good, the free [Catan course](/academy/catan/) starts with your first settlement, and our comparison of [Catan vs Ticket to Ride](/articles/catan-vs-ticket-to-ride/) helps if you are choosing between the two classic next steps.

### Sheriff of Nottingham

Designed by Sergio Halaban, André Zatz, and Bryan Pope and published by Arcane Wonders in 2014, Sheriff of Nottingham plays 3 to 5 in about 60 minutes. In [Sheriff of Nottingham](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B007EZMABG?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), each round one player is the Sheriff, and everyone else tries to bring goods, and maybe some contraband, through the gate. You can bribe the Sheriff with coins, make promises, and bluff.

**How it differs:** the money is for bribes, not rent. It is louder and sillier than Monopoly, and it suits families with teenagers who like a good bluff. Lying is allowed and expected, so set the tone before you start.

## If you love the property and the money

These three are for families who liked owning the board and counting the cash, and want more real choices along the way.

### Chinatown

Designed by Karsten Hartwig and first published by alea in 1999, Chinatown plays 3 to 5 in about 60 minutes. In [Chinatown](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00IVF4ZH0?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), you get lots on city blocks and business tiles each round. Businesses pay more when they are bigger and connected, but your lots and tiles rarely line up, so you trade lots, tiles, and money with everyone else.

**How it differs:** it is the closest thing to "Monopoly, but every trade is a real decision." There are no dice to move you around and nobody goes broke. The game ends after a set number of rounds, and the richest player wins.

### Acquire

Designed by Sid Sackson and first published by 3M in 1964, Acquire plays 2 to 6 in about 90 minutes. In [Acquire](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0D7MSX6PR?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), players place tiles on a grid to found hotel chains, buy stock in them, and profit when a bigger chain swallows a smaller one. The biggest shareholders in the merged chain get cash bonuses.

**How it differs:** you do not own the hotels, you own shares in them, so you can profit from a chain someone else founded. It is older than most of the games here and still holds up. Our guide to [area majority vs area control](/articles/area-majority-vs-area-control/) explains why "own the most shares" plays so differently from owning a space outright.

### Modern Art

Designed by Reiner Knizia and first published by Hans im Glück in 1992, Modern Art plays 3 to 5 in about 45 minutes. In [Modern Art](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0716P8GKX?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), you are art dealers. You auction paintings to each other using several kinds of auction, then the most popular artists pay out at the end of each round.

**How it differs:** it is all money, and every coin you pay goes to another player, not the bank. That makes every bid a small dilemma. Our guide to [Reiner Knizia games](/articles/reiner-knizia-games/) covers it alongside his other bidding classics.

## If your family is ready for the next step

These two take Monopoly's idea, money that earns more money, and make it the whole game.

### Splendor

Designed by Marc André and published by Space Cowboys in 2014, Splendor plays 2 to 4 in about 30 minutes. In [Splendor](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00IZEUFIA?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), you take gem chips and spend them on cards. Every card you own works as a permanent discount, so your buying power grows each turn, just like a row of properties starts paying for itself.

**How it differs:** there is no trading and no board, just a clean money engine that ends when someone reaches 15 points. It plays well with two. Learn it with [how to play Splendor](/academy/splendor/how-to-play-splendor/).

### Power Grid

Designed by Friedemann Friese and first published in 2004, Power Grid plays 2 to 6 in about 2 hours. In [Power Grid](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07HM7WZT9?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), you bid on power plants, buy fuel from a shared market, and connect cities on the map to your network. The more cities you power, the more money you make.

**How it differs:** it is the grown-up version of Monopoly's money game, with real auctions and a market where prices rise as everyone buys. It is heavier and longer, so save it for teens and adults who already like strategy games. Learn it with [how to play Power Grid](/academy/power-grid/how-to-play-power-grid/), then read the lesson on [Power Grid auction strategy](/academy/power-grid/power-grid-auction-strategy/).

## FAQ

### What is the closest game to Monopoly?

Monopoly Deal, since it uses the same property sets and rent with a much shorter game. For a board game with dice and buying, Machi Koro is the closest feel.

### What is a good game like Monopoly for kids?

For Sale, Machi Koro, and Monopoly Deal all work for younger players who can count and read a little. For more ideas by age, see our list of the [best family board games](/articles/best-family-board-games/).

### Why does Monopoly take so long?

Mostly house rules. Free Parking money keeps cash flowing so nobody goes broke, and skipping the auction slows down how fast properties sell. The official rules are shorter, but the game still ends by elimination.

### Is there a Monopoly game without player elimination?

Monopoly Deal ends when someone collects three property sets, so nobody is knocked out. Every other game on this list also ends at a set point instead.

### What should we play after Monopoly?

Catan or Ticket to Ride are the usual next steps. Our list of [gateway board games](/articles/gateway-board-games/) has more first picks, and our [family game night ideas](/articles/family-game-night-ideas/) help you build an evening around them.
