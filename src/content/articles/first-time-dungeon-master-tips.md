The most useful first time Dungeon Master tips are the ones about what not to do. Do not write a novel's worth of lore. Do not memorize the rulebook. Do not build a world. You are running one evening for a few friends who have never played, and the bar is lower and the job is simpler than the internet makes it look.

This is the article for the person who said "I guess I could run it" and is now mildly panicking. Here is what to prepare, what to ignore, and how the night actually goes.

## What does a Dungeon Master actually do?

Three jobs, in rough order of how much of the evening they take.

**Describe the scene.** You tell the players where they are and what they notice. "The hallway is narrow and the torches are out. You hear water dripping somewhere ahead." Two or three sentences per scene is plenty. Then stop and ask, "What do you do?"

**Play everyone who is not a player.** The innkeeper, the guard, the goblin, the dragon. You do not need voices. You need a one-line attitude for each: the innkeeper is tired, the guard is bored, the goblin is scared. Attitude is enough to make a character feel real.

**Decide what happens.** When a player tries something uncertain, you ask for a roll, pick a target number, and narrate the result. When a player tries something the adventure did not anticipate, you decide on the spot. This is the actual skill of running a game, and it is a skill of judgment, not memory.

Notice what is not on the list. Winning. The DM does not play against the players. Your job is to make the world push back enough that choices matter, and then to be delighted when the players find a way through.

## How much do you need to prepare?

Less than you fear. For a first session with new players, here is the full list.

- **A published beginner adventure.** Do not write your own. Many starter sets include an adventure designed for exactly this situation, and we explain the format in [the one-shot article](/articles/dnd-one-shot-for-beginners/). Read it twice. Make a one-page outline of the scenes in order.
- **Pre-made characters.** One per player plus one spare. Hand them out at the table. Do not let anyone build from scratch on night one.
- **The three rules you will use most.** How to make a basic check (roll the twenty-sided die, add the bonus, beat a target number). How a turn works in combat. How damage and hit points work. Everything else you can look up when it comes up, and you should say so out loud.
- **A list of ten names.** For the characters you did not plan. Players will always talk to the one guard you did not name.
- **Dice, pencils, scratch paper, snacks.**

That is one evening of preparation for one evening of play. If you find yourself three hours deep in writing the history of a kingdom, stop. Your players will spend the whole session in one village and never ask.

## What are the five habits of a good first session?

These five will carry you through the night better than any amount of rules knowledge.

**1. Say yes, then make them roll.** When a player wants to swing from the chandelier, do not check whether the rules allow it. Say "sure, give me an Acrobatics check," pick a number between 10 and 15, and see what happens. Players remember the DM who let them try things, not the one who cited page numbers.

**2. Ask "what do you do?" and then wait.** Silence after that question feels long to you and short to the players. Let them think. If one player always answers, turn to a quieter one by name: "Sam, your character is closest to the door. What's she doing?"

**3. Fail forward.** A failed roll should not stop the story. The rogue fails to pick the lock? The lock opens, but it makes a noise and something hears it. A failure that creates a new problem keeps the night moving. A failure that creates a dead end makes everyone stare at you.

**4. Keep rulings fast.** When you are not sure of a rule, make a reasonable call in five seconds, say "we'll look it up after," and move on. Nobody at a first session cares whether you got the grappling rules right. They care whether the game kept going. For the inevitable disagreement, [our rules disputes article](/articles/board-game-rules-argument/) applies here too.

**5. Pull your punches, invisibly.** New players should feel danger without a character dying in their first evening. If a fight is going badly, the monster can flee, the reinforcements can arrive late, the hit points behind your screen can be a little lower than written. Nobody needs to know.

## How do you run combat without it dragging?

Combat is the part of D&D with the most rules, and it is where first-time DMs lose the room. A few tricks keep it moving.

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Players do not know what to do on their turn | Offer two options: "You can attack the goblin or move to help Dana." |
| Looking up rules mid-fight | Make a ruling, note it, look it up after the session |
| Long gaps between a player's turns | Call the next player's name before the current one finishes, so they are thinking |
| Fights going too long | Have the enemies break and run after they lose half their number |
| Nobody can picture the scene | Sketch the room on paper in thirty seconds; it does not need to be good |

A combat round that takes more than a few minutes per player is too slow for beginners. The goal is a fight that feels dangerous and resolves in twenty to thirty minutes. If it is longer than that, start ending it.

## What do you do when the players go off the map?

They will. You prepared a haunted mill and they decided to open a bakery. This is the moment every new DM dreads and it is also the moment the game becomes theirs.

Three moves cover almost every case.

**Move the content.** The interesting thing you prepared in the mill can be in the abandoned bakery instead. The players will never know. Everything you prepared is portable.

**Ask them what they are hoping for.** Out of character, briefly: "What are you guys looking to do tonight?" Players will tell you, and you can steer toward it.

**Let the world respond.** If they ignore the plot, the plot comes to them. The missing children's parents show up at the bakery. The thing in the mill gets hungry and leaves the mill. A world that reacts to being ignored is more fun than one that waits politely.

What you should not do is force it. "You can't go that way" is the fastest way to teach players that their choices do not matter, and once they learn that, they stop making them.

## What does a first session feel like from behind the screen?

Faster than you expect, and messier. You will forget a rule. You will forget a character's name. You will narrate a dramatic reveal and a player will be in the bathroom. All of this is normal, and none of it is visible to the players the way it is to you.

What the players see is a friend who put in an evening of work so they could have an evening of fun. They see someone willing to be the one who makes it happen. That is rarer than good rules knowledge, and it is what they will remember. Experienced DMs will tell you that the first session they ran was a mess and that three of the players are still at their table ten years later. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## What should you do after the session?

Ask one question: "What was your favorite moment?" Each answer tells you what to do more of. Then ask if anyone wants to play again. You will usually be surprised by the yeses.

Write three lines of notes while it is fresh: what happened, what the players seemed to want, and what you would do differently. That is the entire post-session workload. You are now a Dungeon Master, which is a title you earn by doing it once and keep by doing it again.

## FAQ

### How long should a first-time DM prepare for a session?

Two to three hours is plenty for a first session: read a published adventure twice, make a one-page outline, and skim the basic rules. More preparation than that tends to go unused.

### Do I need to know all the D&D rules to be a Dungeon Master?

No. Know how a basic check works, how a combat turn works, and how hit points work. Look up everything else when it comes up and make quick rulings in the meantime.

### Should a first-time DM write their own adventure?

Not for the first session. A published beginner adventure handles pacing and structure so you can focus on running the table. Write your own once you have run a few.

### What if my players do something I did not plan for?

Move the content you prepared to wherever they went, ask them what they are hoping for, or let the world react to their choice. Never tell them they cannot go somewhere.
