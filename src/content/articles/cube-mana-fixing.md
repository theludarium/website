Cube mana fixing is the set of nonbasic lands, mana rocks, and mana creatures that let a drafted deck cast its spells on time. The amount you include decides more about your cube than almost any card choice: light fixing pushes drafters into two colors and rewards discipline, heavy fixing lets three-color good-stuff decks form and rewards taking the best card in every pack. Neither is wrong. You have to pick.

This article covers how many fixing slots a cube needs, which land cycles play best, and where the fixing should sit in your list. It assumes you know the game and are building or tuning a cube. For lane design, read [cube archetypes explained](/articles/cube-archetypes-explained/) alongside this.

## How much fixing does a cube need?

The common baseline for a 360-card cube is one land cycle per color pair, meaning 10 dual lands, plus a handful of utility and five-color lands. Many cubes run two or three cycles, which is 20 to 30 duals. Some run four or five.

Here is what each level does to the table:

| Fixing level | Duals in 360 | What gets drafted |
|---|---|---|
| Light | 10 | Strict two-color decks, splashes are rare and risky |
| Medium | 20 | Two colors plus an easy one-card splash |
| Heavy | 30 | Three-color decks are normal, gold cards get taken early |
| Very heavy | 40 or more | Four- and five-color piles, color commitment stops mattering |

Which level is right depends on what you want the draft to feel like. Light fixing makes reading signals matter, because a drafter who switches colors in pack three cannot fix their way out of it. Heavy fixing makes raw card quality matter and makes gold cards reliably playable, which is good for a cube with a lot of them.

Our own list sits around two cycles, and we lean toward medium for a weekly group with a mix of veterans and new drafters. <!-- EDITOR: add detail --> New players handle two-color decks well. Three-color mana bases are where their games fall apart.

A rule that holds at any level: fixing lands count against your land slots, not your color slots. If you add 10 duals, cut 10 cards from somewhere else in the cube, and make sure it is not all from one color.

## Which land cycles play best in cube?

Not all fixing is equal. The way a land enters the battlefield and the colors it produces shape what decks can use it.

**Fetch lands (Flooded Strand, Bloodstained Mire).** Search for a land with a basic type and put it onto the battlefield. On their own they fix one color pair. Combined with original duals or shock lands, each fetch becomes a four-color land. They are the best fixing in the game and the most expensive. Proxies are common here.

**Original dual lands (Tundra, Badlands).** Two basic land types, no drawback. Fetchable. The gold standard, and priced like it; most cubes use the Reserved List alternatives below.

**Shock lands (Hallowed Fountain, Blood Crypt).** Enter tapped unless you pay 2 life. Have basic types, so they are fetchable. The best budget approximation of true duals, and the 2 life is a real cost for the control deck and almost free for aggro.

**Pain lands (Adarkar Wastes, Sulfurous Springs).** Enter untapped, deal 1 damage when tapped for colored mana. Great for aggro, fine for everyone. Not fetchable.

**Check lands (Glacial Fortress, Dragonskull Summit).** Enter untapped if you control a land with a matching basic type. Reliable in two-color decks, awkward in three.

**Fast lands (Seachrome Coast, Blackcleave Cliffs).** Untapped on turns one through three, tapped after. Perfect for aggro, mediocre for control.

**Slow lands, pathways, triomes, bond lands.** All usable. Triomes (Raugrin Triome and friends) produce three colors, have basic types, and enter tapped, which makes them a strong pick for cubes that want three-color decks to exist without being free.

**Enters-tapped duals with no upside (guildgates, Coastal Tower).** Fine in pauper cubes, where they are the best option available. Weak anywhere else.

## Where does fixing go: lands, rocks, or creatures?

Lands are the cleanest fixing because they do not cost a spell slot. But cube also fixes through artifacts and creatures, and each has a different effect on the draft.

**Mana rocks.** Signets (Azorius Signet and the rest), Talismans, Fellwar Stone, Prismatic Lens, Coalition Relic. A two-mana rock both ramps and fixes, which means the slow decks get the fixing and the fast decks do not. If your midrange and control decks are already too strong, rocks are the wrong place to add fixing. Signets in particular push a cube toward turn-three four-drops across the table.

**Mana creatures.** Birds of Paradise, Noble Hierarch, Deathrite Shaman, Ignoble Hierarch. These give green decks the best fixing in the cube and reward the ramp lanes. They also die to every removal spell, which keeps them honest.

**Fixing spells.** Rampant Growth, Farseek, Nature's Lore, Cultivate. Green only. Farseek and Nature's Lore can fetch a shock land or triome, which makes them stronger in cubes with typed duals.

**Five-color lands.** City of Brass, Mana Confluence, Gemstone Mine, Exotic Orchard. One or two per cube. They let a drafter rescue a mana base late in pack three. More than that and color commitment stops meaning anything.

A balanced split for a medium-fixing 360 cube might be 20 dual lands, 2 five-color lands, 5 to 8 rocks, 6 to 8 mana creatures, and 3 to 5 green fixing spells. Count what you have and look at who it helps.

## How does fixing change what drafters do?

More than the designer usually intends.

With light fixing, the correct draft strategy is to settle into two colors by the middle of pack one and take the best card in those colors from then on. Gold cards get taken late because they are risky. Aggro does well because its mana is simple and its opponents are stumbling.

With heavy fixing, the correct strategy is to take the best card in every pack for most of pack one, take lands aggressively in pack two, and end up in whatever three colors the cards point to. Gold cards get taken early. Control and midrange do well because they always have their colors, and the drafter who disciplined themselves into Boros is facing Esper with Baleful Strix and Lightning Helix in the same deck.

Here is the practical takeaway: if your drafters complain that "signals do not matter," you probably have too much fixing. If they complain that "I never get to play my gold cards," you probably have too little. Adjust by one cycle at a time and watch for a month.

One more effect. Fetch lands in a cube with shock lands or true duals make every fetch a high pick, because it is a four-color land. That is fine if you want it, but it means the first picks of pack one are sometimes lands, which new players find deflating. A cube can run fetches without typed duals; then they are plain two-color fixing and get drafted like it.

## How do you draft lands well?

A short guide for drafters, since the designer's choices are only half of it:

1. **Take a dual over a marginal playable in your colors.** The dual makes every spell in your deck better. The twenty-third playable usually does not make the cut.
2. **Take fixing for your splash, not your main colors.** A Dimir deck splashing Lightning Helix wants Watery Grave less than it wants Blood Crypt and Steam Vents.
3. **Do not take lands over real cards in pack one.** Commit first, then fix. The exception is a fetch in a cube with typed duals, where it is close to the best card in the pack regardless.
4. **Count your sources.** Eight sources for a main color, three or four for a splash of one card. Fewer than that and the land is not fixing anything.

## FAQ

### How many dual lands should be in a 360 cube?

One cycle of ten is a floor. Two to three cycles, 20 to 30 duals, is the most common range. Pick based on whether you want two-color or three-color decks to be the norm.

### What is the best budget fixing for a cube?

Shock lands plus pain lands, or triomes plus check lands. Pauper cubes use guildgates and the Kaldheim snow duals. Proxied fetch lands are common and generally accepted in casual cubes.

### Should a cube include Signets?

Many do. Be aware that two-mana rocks mostly help slow decks and accelerate the whole table toward turn-three four-drops. If your aggro decks are struggling, cut rocks before cutting lands.

### Do mana creatures count as fixing?

Yes, for green decks. Birds of Paradise and Noble Hierarch are among the strongest fixing in any cube that runs them, and they reward drafting green as a base.

## Get the mana right first

Nobody remembers the night their mana worked. Everyone remembers the night it did not. Fixing is the least glamorous part of a cube list and the part that most decides whether people get to play the decks they drafted. Count it, tune it, and let the games happen. The game is the reason. The people are the point.
