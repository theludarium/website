The best board games for grandparents are the ones that get taken out of the box after you leave. That rules out most of what sells well. It leaves games with familiar bones, large components, short rounds, and a reason to call the grandkids over.

This guide is built from what we have seen work at tables with players in their seventies, eighties, and nineties. Prices are approximate and were checked at publisher or major retailer pages at the time of writing.

## What do grandparents actually want from a game?

When we ask older players what keeps a game on the kitchen table, the answers are consistent and practical.

- **Pieces they can see and hold.** Big tiles, thick cards, high-contrast colors. Tiny text and gray-on-beige boards go back in the closet.
- **Rules that feel like something they already know.** Card games built on rummy or trick-taking get played. Games that invent a new vocabulary do not.
- **Rounds under thirty minutes.** Energy and attention are real constraints, and so is a schedule built around meals and medication.
- **Something to do with visitors.** The game is often a reason for the grandkids to sit down. A game that only works for two adults misses this.

A game that satisfies all four is a gift that keeps paying off in phone calls that start with "we played your game again."

## The best board games for grandparents

### Games with familiar bones

**Rummikub** <!-- AFFILIATE --> (about $15 to $20; [Walmart lists the classic edition at $14.34, down from $19.97](https://www.walmart.com/ip/Original-Rummikub-Game/882604), at the time of writing). Rummy with chunky number tiles instead of cards. If they have ever played gin rummy, they already know this game. The tiles are easy to grip and read, and the racks keep hands private without anyone holding fanned cards.

**Qwirkle** <!-- AFFILIATE --> (about $25; [$24.99 at MindWare](https://www.mindware.orientaltrading.com/qwirkle-tile-matching-game-award-winning-strategy-game-for-family-game-night-a2-32016.fltr) and at [Walmart](https://www.walmart.com/ip/Qwirkle-Qwirkle-Board-Game/17058288) at the time of writing). Wooden tiles with six colors and six shapes. Make lines that match on one attribute. It plays like Scrabble with no spelling, which puts a grandparent and a grandchild on even ground. No text anywhere in the game.

**Skip-Bo** or **Phase 10** <!-- AFFILIATE --> (under $10 each; Mattel's own store lists [Skip-Bo at $8.49](https://shop.mattel.com/products/skip-bo-card-game-42050) and [Phase 10 at about $7](https://shop.mattel.com/products/phase-10-card-game-w4729) at the time of writing). Mass-market card games built on familiar sequencing and set-collection rules. Not sophisticated, and that is the point. They are the games most likely to already be in rotation at a senior center, which means they have a track record.

### Games that are better than the ones they have

**Azul** <!-- AFFILIATE --> (about $32; [Target lists it at $31.99](https://www.target.com/s/azul)). Draft colored tiles and arrange them in rows. The tiles are large, bright, and pleasant to handle. The rules take five minutes. It is the game we recommend when a grandparent already owns Rummikub and is ready for something new that still feels approachable.

**Patchwork** <!-- AFFILIATE --> (about $30; [$29.99 at the Asmodee store](https://store.asmodee.com/collections/lookout-games/products/patchwork-revised-edition)). A two-player game of buying fabric pieces to fill a quilt board. For a grandparent who lives with a spouse or has one regular visitor. Calm, tactile, and twenty minutes long.

**Sagrada** <!-- AFFILIATE --> (about $40; [$39.95 at Floodgate Games](https://floodgate.games/products/sagrada-classic) at the time of writing). Place colorful translucent dice into a stained-glass window pattern. The dice are big, the game is quiet, and it looks beautiful on a table. Good for someone who liked jigsaw puzzles before their eyes made them harder.

### Games to play with the grandkids

**Sushi Go!** <!-- AFFILIATE --> (about $15; [Noble Knight lists the tin at $15.49](https://www.nobleknight.com/P/2147556632)). Pass cards, collect sets. Works for a seven-year-old and a seventy-year-old at the same time, and the whole game takes fifteen minutes. The cards are smaller than ideal, so check the "large print" note below.

**Ticket to Ride: First Journey** <!-- AFFILIATE --> (about $20 to $39 depending on sale; Tom's Guide tracked it at [$19 on Black Friday 2025 against a $39 list price](https://www.tomsguide.com/gaming/black-friday-2025-board-games-card-games-deals)). A simplified Ticket to Ride with a smaller map and shorter routes, aimed at kids six and up. It is also, as it happens, the right version of the game for a grandparent who finds the full map busy.

**Codenames** <!-- AFFILIATE --> (about $20 to $25; Target lists it at $24.99 per [Board Game Oracle](https://www.boardgameoracle.com/boardgame/price/9tB3SAHmND/codenames)). Word association across generations is a joy to watch. Grandma's clue of "Sinatra" for three cards is a story the grandkids will tell. The word cards are small; the [XXL edition](https://store.czechgames.com/products/codenames-xxl-2025-pre-order) exists for this reason and costs about $10 more ($34.99 at the publisher's store at the time of writing, against $24.99 for the standard box).

**Hues and Cues** <!-- AFFILIATE --> (about $26; [$25.95 at The Op](https://theop.games/products/hues-and-cues) at the time of writing). Guess a color from a one-word clue. No reading, no rules to remember between turns, and it seats ten, which covers a holiday table. ## Which game for which grandparent?

| If they... | Give | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Play cards already | Rummikub | Same rules, better pieces |
| Have trouble with small print | Qwirkle or Azul | No text, big components |
| Live with a spouse | Patchwork | True two-player, 20 minutes |
| Loved jigsaw puzzles | Sagrada or Azul | Pattern-building, quiet |
| Host the grandkids often | Hues and Cues or Codenames | Seats a crowd, all ages |
| Say they "don't play games" | Sushi Go! | Fifteen minutes, hard to refuse |

## What about large print and accessibility?

A few practical notes from playing with older relatives.

- **Card games can be upgraded.** Standard card sleeves do not help eyesight, but many publishers sell large-format editions. Codenames XXL is the best known. For games without one, a cheap lighted magnifier next to the table solves more than you would expect.
- **Tiles beat cards.** Rummikub, Qwirkle, and Azul all use tiles that can be read at arm's length and picked up with stiff fingers. Shuffling a deck is harder than it looks at eighty.
- **Cooperative games remove the sting.** If a grandparent is self-conscious about keeping up, a game where everyone wins or loses together, like The Crew, can be easier to say yes to.
- **Dice trays are a kindness.** Dice roll off tables. A folding faux-leather or felt tray costs about $7 to $16 at the time of writing ([a basic folding tray at Walmart](https://www.walmart.com/ip/294564045) was under $10; [Ultra Pro's magnetic folding tray at Miniature Market](https://www.miniaturemarket.com/vivid-dice-tray-magnetic-foldable-black-upr16332.html) was $15.99) and keeps the game on the table and off the floor.

## Is there a health reason to give games?

There is a reasonable one, with hedges. Regular social contact and mentally engaging activity are both associated with better cognitive and emotional health in older adults; the [U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory on loneliness](https://www.hhs.gov/surgeongeneral/reports-and-publications/connection/index.html) summarized the social side of that research, including the link between isolation in older adults and higher dementia risk. A board game is not medicine, and anyone worried about a relative's memory or mood should talk to a doctor. But a game is a reason to sit down together, and that is the part the research keeps pointing at.

We wrote more about this in our piece on [board games and dementia](/articles/board-games-and-dementia/), including what the evidence does and does not support.

## The real gift is the second visit

A game given to a grandparent works best when you play it with them the day they open it. Teach it once, lose gracefully, and leave it on the table rather than back in the box. The next time you visit, it will be set up before you get your coat off.

The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### What is the best board game for elderly parents?

Rummikub, if they already play cards; Qwirkle or Azul if small print is a problem. All three have large pieces, familiar rules, and games under thirty minutes.

### What board games can grandparents play with grandchildren?

Sushi Go!, Ticket to Ride: First Journey, Qwirkle, and Hues and Cues all play fairly across a sixty-year age gap because they do not depend on speed or trivia.

### Are there large print board games?

Yes. Codenames XXL is the best known. Tile games like Rummikub, Qwirkle, and Azul are naturally large-format and need no special edition.

### How much should I spend on a board game for a grandparent?

Most of the games here are $7 to $40 at the time of writing. The expensive part is the visit to teach it, and that is the part that matters.
