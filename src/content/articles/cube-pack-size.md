Cube pack size is the quiet variable behind every draft. The standard is fifteen cards per pack, three packs, forty-five picks per player, because that is what retail boosters were when cube was invented and it is what eight drafters times 360 cards divides into. Change the pack size and you change how many playables a deck sees, how loud signals are, how long the draft takes, and how much of the cube gets opened. None of those are side effects. They are the draft.

This article gives the arithmetic for any pod and cube size, then the design reasons to depart from fifteen.

## Why is 15 cards and 45 picks the standard?

Two reasons, one historical and one structural.

The historical one: boosters had fifteen cards for most of Magic's life (fourteen playables and a basic or token, depending on the era), so a cube draft that used fifteen-card packs felt like a draft. The Play Boosters introduced with Murders at Karlov Manor [contain fourteen cards, one of them non-playable](https://wpn.wizards.com/news/introducing-play-boosters-the-best-of-two-worlds-combined), and the change has not altered cube practice.

The structural one: forty-five picks is about the right number for a 40-card deck. A deck runs 23 spells and 17 lands, with most lands coming from the basic box. Forty-five picks gives a drafter 23 playables plus 22 cards that are fixing, sideboard, hate picks, or mistakes. That ratio, about two picks per spell in the final deck, produces decks that are coherent but not perfect, which is what makes drafting a skill.

Drop to 36 picks (three packs of twelve) and decks get noticeably worse: 23 spells from 36 picks leaves no room for misreads. Raise it to 54 (three packs of eighteen) and decks get better and more similar, because everyone has enough playables to fill a curve.

## The arithmetic for any pod

The formula is simple. Cards opened = players × packs × pack size. Compare that to your cube size to see how much gets drafted.

| Players | 3 × 15 | 3 × 12 | 3 × 18 | 4 × 11 | 2 × 15 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 90 | 72 | 108 | 88 | 60 |
| 4 | 180 | 144 | 216 | 176 | 120 |
| 6 | 270 | 216 | 324 | 264 | 180 |
| 8 | 360 | 288 | 432 | 352 | 240 |
| 10 | 450 | 360 | 540 | 440 | 300 |
| 12 | 540 | 432 | 648 | 528 | 360 |

A few things the table shows at a glance.

A 360 cube seats eight at fifteen, or ten at twelve, or twelve at ten-card packs. If an extra pair shows up, shrinking the pack is the quick fix, at the cost of weaker decks. Our pod logistics piece covers how we handle the odd-numbered night.

A 540 cube with eight players and eighteen-card packs opens 432, which is most of the cube and produces strong decks. Some groups use this when they want a 540's variety and a 360's deck quality on the same night.

Two players drafting fifteen-card packs through Winston or grid need far fewer cards than this table suggests, because those formats do not open full packs. Our two-player formats guide has those numbers.

## What changes when packs get smaller?

Smaller packs (eleven to thirteen cards) do four things.

**Decks get weaker.** Fewer picks, same 23-spell requirement. Expect more filler in main decks and shorter sideboards.

**Signals get quieter.** In a fifteen-card pack, your first pick wheels as the ninth card someone else sees. In a twelve-card pack, cards go around faster and there is less late-pack information. Reading the table gets harder.

**Drafts get shorter.** A fifteen-card pack with eight players takes about ten to twelve minutes with a pick timer; twelve-card packs save three to four minutes per pack, which is ten minutes a draft. See [pick timers and pace](/articles/draft-pick-timers/) for how to keep any draft under 45 minutes.

**More of the cube stays in the box.** For a 540 this increases variety. For a 360 it means cards that never get opened, which is a flaw in a cube designed to be fully visible.

Smaller packs make sense when you have more players than the cube supports at fifteen, when you are short on time, or when the cube is a chaos or retail-emulation cube where weaker decks are part of the fun.

## What changes when packs get bigger?

Bigger packs (sixteen to twenty cards) are rarer and more interesting.

**Decks get stronger and more homogeneous.** With 54 or 60 picks, everyone fills their curve and the difference between a well-drafted deck and a poorly drafted one shrinks. Good for a casual night, bad for a format that wants drafting skill to matter.

**Late picks become real.** In a twenty-card pack, picks fifteen through twenty are cards nobody wanted, and in a tight cube that is still a playable or a dual. Drafters who count cards can plan to wheel specific things. Some owners like this; it rewards attention.

**Packs take longer.** Twenty-card packs with eight players is a 90-minute draft without a timer. Budget the time or drop to two packs.

**The cube needs to be bigger or the pod smaller.** 8 × 3 × 18 = 432, so a 360 cannot do it. A 540 can.

Bigger packs make sense when a group wants the "everyone has a good deck" feel, when the cube is unusually tight and the extra picks are all playable, or in two-pack formats (2 × 20 = 40 picks) that want a faster night with a normal pick count.

## How does pack size interact with cube size?

Cube size and pack size trade off against each other for the same resource: how much of the cube appears per draft. The combinations fall into three families.

- **Fully opened.** 360 at 8 × 3 × 15, or 180 at 4 × 3 × 15. Every card appears every draft. Mastery and planning matter.
- **Mostly opened.** 540 at 8 × 3 × 18 (432) or 450 at 8 × 3 × 15 (360 of 450). Some variety, strong decks.
- **Partly opened.** 540 at 8 × 3 × 15 (360 of 540) or 720 at 8 × 3 × 15 (half). Variety is the point; archetypes need redundancy.

If you want a specific feel, you can get there from either side. A 540 owner who wants a fully-opened draft can run 8 × 3 × 22 or two pods of six at 3 × 15, and a 360 owner who wants variety can run 3 × 12 and leave 72 cards in the box. Deciding which lever to pull is the real design choice; see [360 vs 540](/articles/360-vs-540-cube/) for the cube size side of the trade.

## A note on pack construction

The default is random: shuffle the whole cube, deal packs of fifteen. Two alternatives change the draft without changing the count.

**Seeded packs.** Each pack gets a guaranteed composition, for example one land, one gold card, and thirteen others. It flattens variance between packs and guarantees fixing appears evenly. The cost is setup time and a less surprising draft. Some owners seed only lands, which is the cheapest version.

**Asymmetric packs.** Pack one is seventeen cards and packs two and three are fourteen, or the first pack is a larger "Rochester" pack. Rare, but used by cubes that want pack one to set lanes more strongly than later packs. Worth knowing, not worth starting with.

Whatever you choose, write it in the primer. A drafter who assumes random packs and gets seeded ones will misread signals for a whole draft.

## Quick reference

- Standard: 3 packs × 15 cards = 45 picks. Cube size needed = 45 × players.
- Short on cards or long on players: 3 × 12 = 36 picks. Decks weaker, draft faster.
- Want stronger decks from a 540: 3 × 18 = 54 picks, opens 432 with eight.
- Short on time: 2 × 20 = 40 picks, two rounds instead of three.
- Any format: count the cards you will open before you shuffle.

The game is the reason. The people are the point. The pack size is just how many choices you hand each of them before the game starts.

## FAQ

### How many cards are in a cube pack?

Fifteen is standard, giving 45 picks over three packs. Twelve- and eighteen-card packs are the common alternatives when a group has more players or wants stronger decks.

### How many picks do you get in a cube draft?

Forty-five in the standard format. A 40-card deck uses about 23 of them as spells; the rest are lands, sideboard, and hate picks.

### Can you cube draft with 12-card packs?

Yes. Twelve-card packs let a 360 cube seat ten players, shorten the draft, and produce slightly weaker decks with quieter signals.

### Does pack size matter in cube?

It decides how much of the cube is opened, how strong decks are, how readable signals are, and how long the draft takes. It is one of the two main levers alongside cube size.
