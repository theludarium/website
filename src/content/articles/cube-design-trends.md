Cube design trends move slower than Standard and faster than most owners notice. A cube built from the best public lists in 2014 and left alone would feel strange today: too many planeswalkers, too few one-drops, a storm deck nobody can assemble, a gold section twice the size it needs to be, and almost no fixing for aggressive decks. None of those were mistakes at the time. They were the consensus.

This is a look at what shifted, why, and which of the shifts a paper cube owner should take seriously. It is written from the public record, mostly the MTGO Vintage Cube updates and the lists on CubeCobra, and from a decade of running our own. Where we state a specific change from memory, it is tagged for the editor to confirm.

## What did a typical cube look like around 2014?

The reference points then were the MTGO Vintage Cube, a handful of famous personal lists with forum primers, and the cube subreddit. The consensus 540 looked roughly like this:

- Planeswalkers everywhere. Each color had four to six, and a lot of games were decided by whoever resolved Jace, the Mind Sculptor or Elspeth, Knight-Errant first.
- A big gold section, often 60 to 80 cards, because gold was where the "cool" cards were.
- Thin aggro. White had its one-drops but red and black often did not, and the aggressive decks were understood as the thing you drafted when you got cut.
- Combo as a tolerated guest: a storm package, a Sneak and Show package, maybe Splinter Twin, each with enough dedicated cards to be real and enough to tax everything else.
- Fixing designed for control and midrange: fetches, duals, and shocks, but few of the fast lands or painlands that let a two-color aggro deck curve out.
- Blue on top. Not as a complaint, as a fact of design. Control was the "skill" deck and blue was the control color.

If you drafted a cube in that era, you remember the texture: long games, planeswalker standoffs, and a general sense that the aggro player was there to make the midrange player feel good.

## What changed, and roughly when?

A handful of shifts, each with a cause.

**Aggro got real support.** Cube writers were arguing for [critical-mass aggro support as early as 2010](https://luckypaper.co/articles/the-history-of-the-cube-format/), and over the following decade the public lists increasingly treated aggressive decks as a design goal rather than a byproduct: more one-drops in every color, more cheap burn. Magic Online's own [Vintage Cube notes](https://www.mtgo.com/news/holidayvintagecube-122022) tell drafters that aggro is stronger than they think, and [SCG cube coverage](https://articles.starcitygames.com/magic-the-gathering/mtgo-vintage-cube-what-worked-what-didnt-and-next-steps/) has pushed for still more mono-color aggro support.

**Planeswalker counts fell.** In the lists we follow, owners have trimmed planeswalkers over the years, keeping the ones that are either cheap (Teferi, Time Raveler; Wrenn and Six) or that define a lane, and cutting the five-mana value walkers that made games stall.

**Gold sections shrank.** The realization that gold cards are signposts rather than payoffs led many owners to cut gold sections to three to five cards per pair. The freed slots went to mono-colored cards that serve more decks.

**Fixing got broader.** Fast lands, pathways, and the Modern Horizons duals gave owners ways to fix for aggressive decks without the tempo loss of a tapped land. Full fetch-and-dual packages became the default in powered lists, and the question moved from "how much fixing" to "which fixing for which decks."

**Power creep arrived.** From roughly 2019 on, new sets delivered cube-worthy cards at a fast clip: Oko, Uro, Ragavan, the Modern Horizons 2 evoke elementals, and a long list of two- and three-mana cards that outclassed their predecessors. [Modern Horizons 2](https://articles.starcitygames.com/magic-the-gathering/select/my-top-10-cube-cards-from-modern-horizons-2/) alone drew talk of being the best cube set ever. Cubes that had been stable for years suddenly had a dozen swaps per set. This is its own subject, and a neighbor of this one: [power creep and cube](/articles/power-creep-and-cube/).

**Combo got cleaner or got cut.** Storm was the first casualty in many lists, replaced by combo decks that overlap with fair decks: Reanimator shares with Dimir and Rakdos, Sneak Attack shares with red ramp, Oath of Druids with Simic. The era of a twenty-card storm package that is dead in every other deck is mostly over outside dedicated powered lists.

**The free spells question.** Force of Will was always in. Then came Force of Negation, Solitude, Grief, Fury, Endurance, Subtlety, and the pitch cycle became a design problem rather than a single card. Some cubes embraced it; some cut it to Force of Will alone. Either way, it became a decision an owner had to make, which it had not been before.

## What changed in the tools?

Arguably the bigger shift. In 2014, a cube list lived in a spreadsheet, a forum post, or a text file. Testing meant eight people in a room. Today:

- **[CubeCobra](https://cubecobra.com/)** hosts the list, tracks changes with a blog and a changelog, lets owners playtest drafts against bots, and now holds [more than 100,000 cubes](https://luckypaper.co/articles/the-history-of-the-cube-format/) to compare against. The ability to see what percentage of cubes in your power band run a given card changed how swaps get argued.
- **Lucky Paper** and similar projects publish data work, from a [Cube Map](https://luckypaper.co/resources/cube-map/) that clusters tens of thousands of CubeCobra lists to [land-count analysis across 75,000-plus draft decks](https://luckypaper.co/articles/how-many-lands-should-you-include-in-your-cube/), which turned "I think this card is good in cube" into something checkable.
- **Proxies got easy and acceptable.** High-quality proxies became cheap enough that power level stopped being a budget question for most groups. The social norm shifted too; a few proxies in a casual cube is uncontroversial in most rooms now.
- **Discord replaced forums.** Cube discussion moved from long-form primers to fast conversation, which made trends move faster and primers rarer. The primer is coming back, which is its own small trend.

The net effect is that cube design is more empirical than it was. That is mostly good. The risk is that empirical design converges, and a thousand cubes tuned on the same data start to look like one.

## Which trends should a paper cube follow?

Not all of them. A short verdict on each.

| Trend | Follow it? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Real aggro support | Yes | Makes every other deck better by giving it a clock to beat. |
| Fewer planeswalkers | Yes | Games with three walkers on the table are bad games. |
| Smaller gold section | Yes | Frees slots and clarifies lanes. |
| Broader fixing | Yes | Cheapest variance reduction available. |
| Chasing every new set | No | Add two cards a set, not ten. |
| Full pitch-spell suite | Depends | Fine for a powered proving ground; rough for a mixed room. |
| Cutting combo entirely | Depends | Keep the combo that overlaps with fair decks; cut the combo that does not. |

The pattern: follow the trends that are about structure, be skeptical of the trends that are about the newest cards.

## What will the next ten years do?

Guessing, but a few things look likely from where we sit.

Cubes will keep getting smaller. The 360 seems to be regaining ground after years in which 540 was a common default, because owners have noticed that a tighter list drafts better and is easier to maintain. The 450 may end up the compromise.

Power creep will keep forcing choices, and more owners will respond by setting a hard cutoff: no cards after a certain year, or no cards above a certain power, as a way to make the list stop moving. The "frozen" cube, one that is finished on purpose, will become a recognized style rather than a sign of neglect. We touch on that tension in [a cube is never finished](/articles/cube-is-never-finished/).

And the tools will keep improving, which means the gap between a cube built on data and a cube built on taste will widen. The good cubes will be built on both.

## FAQ

### How has cube design changed since the early 2010s?

Aggro got real support, planeswalker counts fell, gold sections shrank, fixing got broader, and power creep made yearly maintenance heavier. Tools like CubeCobra made design more data-driven.

### Are 540-card cubes still the standard?

They are still common, but smaller cubes have come back into favor because they draft more consistently and are easier to maintain. The 360 and 450 are both popular again.

### Should I update my cube to match the Vintage Cube?

Use its changes as a source of candidates, not a set of instructions. A paper cube has a different room, a different power level, and should have its own point of view.

### What is the biggest trend a paper cube should adopt?

Supporting aggressive decks properly. It improves every game in the cube, not just the aggro ones.

## Know which decade your cube is from

Every cube carries the assumptions of the year it was built. Check yours against the shifts above, take the structural ones, and leave the fashion. The game is the reason. The people are the point.
