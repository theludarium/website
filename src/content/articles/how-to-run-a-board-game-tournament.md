A board game tournament is the easiest way to turn a casual group into an event. People who skip a normal game night show up for a bracket, because a bracket has stakes, a story, and an ending. The hard part is not the games. It is the format, the timing, and the three rules nobody thinks about until a tie happens at 10:40 p.m.

This guide covers the four formats that work for tabletop, when each fits, how to seed, how to break ties, and how to keep the whole thing from running two hours over. ## Which game should you run a tournament for?

The game matters more than the format. A good tournament game has four properties.

1. **Short matches.** Twenty minutes or less, including setup. Love Letter <!-- AFFILIATE -->, Hive <!-- AFFILIATE -->, Jaipur <!-- AFFILIATE -->, Patchwork <!-- AFFILIATE -->, Star Realms <!-- AFFILIATE -->, and 7 Wonders Duel <!-- AFFILIATE --> all qualify. Catan does not, unless you have all day.
2. **A clear winner.** Games that end in scores are fine. Games that end in "we sort of ran out of time" are not.
3. **A fixed player count.** Two-player games make brackets simple. Three- and four-player games work but need a scoring system rather than a knockout.
4. **Low learning cost.** If half the field is learning the game in round one, round one is a demo, not a match.

If you want a multiplayer game, pick one that plays in 30 to 45 minutes and scores points: Azul <!-- AFFILIATE -->, Splendor <!-- AFFILIATE -->, Kingdomino <!-- AFFILIATE -->, or Ticket to Ride <!-- AFFILIATE -->. Then use Swiss or round robin, not a knockout bracket.

## What are the four tournament formats?

### Single elimination

Lose once and you are out. Sixteen players, four rounds, one champion.

**When it fits:** you have more players than time. Thirty-two people and ninety minutes means single elimination or nothing. It also fits when the drama matters more than fairness; the final is a real final.

**The cost:** half your guests are done after round one. Plan a side event for them, or the night empties out. A consolation table running the same game casually keeps eliminated players in the room.

### Double elimination

You must lose twice to go out. Losers from the main bracket drop to a second bracket and fight back toward the final.

**When it fits:** a game with real luck in it, like Love Letter or anything with dice. One bad draw should not end someone's night. Double elimination also keeps everyone playing at least two matches, which is the minimum for a tournament to feel like one.

**The cost:** roughly twice as many matches as single elimination, and the bracket is confusing to run by hand. The bracket tool handles it, or a printed template does. The grand final has a quirk: the player from the losers' bracket has to beat the undefeated player twice, since the undefeated player has not yet lost. Decide in advance whether you honor that rule or play a single final. Say it out loud before round one.

### Swiss

Nobody is eliminated. Each round pairs players with the same record: winners play winners, losers play losers. After a fixed number of rounds, the best record wins.

**When it fits:** most tabletop tournaments, in our experience. Swiss is how Magic: The Gathering events run, and for good reason. Everyone plays every round, the pairings get tighter as the night goes on, and the winner has beaten the other strong players. Swiss is also the right format for multiplayer games: seat four players by record each round and award points by finish position.

**The cost:** you need to decide the round count in advance. The usual guide is the number of rounds it would take a single-elimination bracket of that size: three rounds for up to eight players, four for up to sixteen, five for up to thirty-two. Swiss also produces ties more often than any other format, so read the tiebreak section carefully.

### Round robin

Everyone plays everyone. Best record wins.

**When it fits:** small fields. Six players at a two-player game is fifteen matches, which is a long night but a fair one. Round robin is the right choice for a league that runs across weeks, where one evening's time limit does not apply. Our guide to a [board game league format](/articles/board-game-league-format/) builds on it.

**The cost:** match count grows fast. Eight players is twenty-eight matches. Past six or seven players in a single evening, switch to Swiss.

| Format | Best for | Matches (8 players) | Everyone plays all night? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single elimination | Big fields, short time, drama | 7 | No |
| Double elimination | Luck-heavy games, mid fields | 14 or 15 | Mostly |
| Swiss | Most events, any field size | 12 (3 rounds) | Yes |
| Round robin | Small fields, leagues | 28 | Yes |

## How do you seed a tournament?

Seeding means arranging the bracket so the strongest players do not meet in round one. For a casual group, this is less important than it sounds, and random seeding is fine. Pull names from a hat, in front of everyone, so nobody wonders.

Seed deliberately when two things are true: you know who the strong players are, and the format is single or double elimination. In Swiss and round robin, seeding does not matter because the pairings sort themselves out.

If you do seed, use the standard pattern: the top seed plays the bottom seed, second plays second-to-last, and so on. A bracket of eight goes 1 vs 8, 4 vs 5, 2 vs 7, 3 vs 6, so that 1 and 2 can only meet in the final.

Odd numbers get a bye: one player skips the first round. Give the bye to the top seed, or draw it randomly. In Swiss, the bye counts as a win for pairing purposes.

## How do you handle tiebreaks?

Ties happen in Swiss and round robin, and they happen in the standings that matter. Decide the tiebreak order before the tournament starts, write it on the whiteboard, and never change it mid-event.

A reasonable order for a casual tournament:

1. **Head-to-head.** If the tied players played each other, the winner of that match ranks higher.
2. **Opponents' win percentage.** Add up the records of everyone each tied player faced. The player who beat stronger opponents ranks higher. This is the standard Swiss tiebreak, and the bracket tool computes it.
3. **Point differential** (for games with scores). Total points scored minus total points conceded, across all matches.
4. **A single sudden-death match**, if time allows and the tie is for first place.

For multiplayer scoring games, assign tournament points by finish position each round (for example 4, 3, 2, 1 at a four-player table), then use total game points as the first tiebreak.

A tiebreak that works badly: a coin flip for first place. People remember it. Use the coin only for places that do not carry a prize.

## How do you keep a tournament on time?

Time is the thing that goes wrong. Three habits fix most of it.

**Set a round clock.** Each round gets a fixed length, announced and visible. When the clock ends, unfinished games are scored as they stand, or the player ahead wins. People play faster when a clock exists.

**Start each round together.** Do not let fast tables start the next match early. The round starts when the organizer says it starts, after every result is recorded.

**Collect results at a single table.** One person, one sheet or one screen. Players report to the organizer, not the other way around. Results that are shouted across a room get recorded wrong.

A realistic schedule for a 16-player Swiss event at a 20-minute game: four rounds at 30 minutes each (20 to play, 10 to record and re-pair) is two hours, plus 15 minutes at the front for rules and seating. Announce the end time, and end there.

## What should the prize be?

Small. A tournament prize should be something to hold up for a photo, not something people would compete badly for. A trophy, a copy of the game you played, a hand-lettered certificate. Our rule for a [themed bracket night](/articles/themed-game-night-ideas/) is that the prize should be slightly embarrassing.

Recognize second place and a "best sport" pick as well. The second one matters more than it sounds; it tells the room what kind of event this is.

## FAQ

### What is the best tournament format for a board game?

Swiss, for most events. Everyone plays every round, no one is eliminated early, and the winner has beaten the other strong players. Use single elimination only when you have far more players than time.

### How many rounds should a Swiss tournament have?

Three rounds for up to eight players, four for up to sixteen, five for up to thirty-two. The final standings become reliable at about that count.

### How do you run a tournament for a four-player board game?

Use Swiss with table seating by record. Each round, group players with similar records at four-player tables, award tournament points by finish position, and re-seat after each round.

### Do you need software to run a board game tournament?
