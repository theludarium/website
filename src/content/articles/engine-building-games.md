Engine building games are games where the things you acquire early make everything you do later cheaper, faster, or more rewarding. You start weak. You spend your first turns building capacity rather than scoring. Then, somewhere past the midpoint, the pieces start feeding each other and a turn that once produced one resource produces six. That moment of acceleration is the whole appeal.

This article explains what an engine is in board game terms, how the curve works, why the feeling is so compelling, where the style came from, and which games to try if you want to feel it for yourself.

## What is an "engine" in a board game?

An engine is a set of game elements that produce value without being spent. The distinction between producing and spending is the key.

In [Monopoly](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00CV5PN2W?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), a property you own produces rent every time someone lands on it. You bought it once; it pays forever. That is an engine, a crude one. In [Catan](https://link.amazon/B0h8BDGR2), a settlement on a good hex produces resources on every matching roll. Also an engine.

What modern engine building games add is interaction between the parts. In [Wingspan](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07YQ641NQ?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), a bird in your forest row might give you food every time you activate the row. A second bird in that row might let you cache food for points. A third might draw a card whenever you gain food. Now one action triggers three effects, and the three effects feed each other. That chain is the engine, and building it is the game.

The common signs of an engine builder:

- You acquire permanent things (cards, buildings, birds, gems) rather than only consuming them.
- Those things give you a repeating benefit every turn or every time something triggers.
- The benefits compound: acquiring one makes the next easier to acquire.
- Scoring is back-loaded. Early turns build; late turns cash in.

## What does the engine curve look like?

Almost every engine building game follows the same shape, and recognizing it is the first step to playing well.

**Phase one: investment.** You have few resources and each turn does little. You buy the cheapest permanent things that produce something. In [Splendor](https://link.amazon/B0fEvY6Wg), that means picking up level-one cards for their gem bonuses, not their points. It feels slow. It is supposed to.

**Phase two: acceleration.** The early purchases start paying off. You can afford mid-tier things, and those produce more. In [Dominion](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01LYLIS2U?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), this is when your deck starts drawing extra cards and playing extra actions in the same turn. Turns get longer and more satisfying.

**Phase three: conversion.** The engine is built. Now you stop building and start turning its output into points. In Wingspan, this is the fourth round, when you stop placing birds and start laying eggs. In Splendor, it is when you take the nobles and the high-point cards.

The core strategic question in every engine builder is when to switch from phase two to phase three. Switch too early and your engine is weak. Switch too late and the game ends while you are still building. Most games lost by experienced players against other experienced players come down to mistiming that switch.

## Why are engine building games so addictive?

Several things are working on you at once.

**Compounding is pleasurable.** Watching a small thing grow into a large thing by its own momentum is one of the deepest satisfactions available in play. It is the same feeling as a savings account with interest, or a garden, or a sourdough starter. Engine builders deliver it in ninety minutes.

**Progress is visible.** Your tableau of cards or birds or buildings grows in front of you. You can see what you have built, and so can everyone else.

**Every turn is better than the last.** In many games, your position gets harder as the game goes on. In an engine builder, your options expand. The game gets more fun as it proceeds, which is the opposite of how many games feel.

**The loss is instructive.** When you lose an engine builder, you can usually see why: you bought the wrong early card, you switched to points too late, you ignored a combo the winner saw. That clarity makes you want to play again immediately. "One more game" is the natural ending.

There is a downside, and it is the standard criticism of the genre: engine builders can be solitary. If your engine does not touch my engine, we are playing two games side by side. Designers address this with shared markets, limited supplies, and race conditions, but the complaint has merit for some titles. ## Where did engine building come from?

The idea is old. Monopoly's properties and Acquire's hotel chains are engines in the loose sense. But as a named style, engine building grew out of two threads in the 2000s.

The first is Dominion (2008), which made the deck itself the engine and gave the hobby the vocabulary of "building" something out of cards. Our piece on [deck building games](/articles/deck-building-games-explained/) tells that story.

The second is the European economic game tradition: [Puerto Rico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Rico_(board_game)) (2002), [Caylus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caylus_(board_game)) (2005), and [Agricola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricola_(board_game)) (2007), where buildings and upgrades produced repeating benefits. [Race for the Galaxy](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000YLAOEW?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) (2007) combined both threads into a tableau of cards that chained off each other, and it is probably the first game most hobbyists would call a pure engine builder.

Splendor (2014) stripped the idea to its bones: gems buy cards, cards give permanent gem discounts, discounts buy better cards. Wingspan (2019) wrapped a rich engine in a theme warm enough to sell to people who had never heard the term. Between them, the style became one of the most popular in the hobby.

## Which engine building games should you try first?

| Game | Engine made of | Length | Why start here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Splendor | Gem cards that discount future cards | 30 min | The purest, shortest version of the curve |
| Wingspan | Birds with row-triggered powers | 60 to 90 min | The warmest theme; the engine is visible and pretty |
| [Century: Spice Road](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B071DXCT5L?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) | Trade cards that convert cubes | 30 to 45 min | Hand of cards you cycle; teaches conversion timing |
| Dominion | Your own deck | 30 min | The deck-builder origin; endless setup variety |
| Race for the Galaxy | Tableau of planets and developments | 30 to 45 min | Deep and fast once learned; dense iconography |
| [Terraforming Mars](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01GSYA4K2?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) | Project cards and production tracks | 2 to 3 hours | The long form; the engine is a planet |

Splendor is the best teacher because there is nothing else in it. Every decision is an engine decision. Once the curve makes sense there, it makes sense everywhere.

Wingspan is the best introduction for a mixed group because the theme carries people who do not care about efficiency. Someone can play it for the birds and still feel the acceleration.

Terraforming Mars is where to go once you want the engine to be enormous. It is also where the genre's weaknesses show: long games, long turns, and the sense that the other players are mostly scenery.

## How do you get better at engine builders?

Three habits cover most of the ground.

**Buy production before points.** Early in the game, a card that produces is worth more than a card that scores, even if the scoring card has a bigger number on it. The production card will pay off every turn for the rest of the game.

**Count the turns left.** Know how many turns remain and ask what a new engine piece will produce in that time. A piece that pays back in three turns is a bad buy with two turns left.

**Look for triggers that feed each other.** The strongest engines are not the ones with the most parts. They are the ones where one action sets off several effects. A bird that gives food when you lay eggs, next to a bird that lays eggs when you gain food, is better than two unrelated birds with higher point values.

## FAQ

### What is an engine building game?

A game where you acquire permanent things that produce repeating benefits, so that your capacity grows over the game and late turns are much stronger than early ones.

### Is Wingspan an engine builder?

Yes, and probably the most popular one. Birds placed in each habitat row give powers that trigger every time you use that row.

### Is Catan an engine building game?

Loosely. Settlements produce resources every turn, which is an engine, but there is little compounding between the parts, so it is usually classified as a trading and network game.

### What is the difference between engine building and deck building?

Deck building is one way to build an engine, where the engine is your deck. Engine building is the broader category and includes tableaus, boards, and resource tracks.

## The engine and the table

The best part of an engine builder is the turn where everything finally works, and the second best part is watching someone else have that turn. Both are better with people in the room to see it. The game is the reason. The people are the point.
