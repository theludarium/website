The best games like [Catan](https://link.amazon/B0h8BDGR2) depend on what kept your group at the table. If it was the trading and the table talk, play [Bohnanza](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000MLZ516?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) or [Sheriff of Nottingham](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B007EZMABG?tag=cubecommonw0a-20). If it was building across a shared map, play [Carcassonne](https://link.amazon/B012Rr5ed), [Ticket to Ride](https://link.amazon/B0jh8RB7f), or [Cascadia](https://link.amazon/B0g1HYFt4). If it was turning resources into more resources, play [Splendor](https://link.amazon/B0fEvY6Wg), [Century: Spice Road](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B071DXCT5L?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), or [Stone Age](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00CF4G7OW?tag=cubecommonw0a-20). And if your group is ready for a longer, deeper evening, [Lords of Waterdeep](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0786959916?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Concordia](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00HFDSQP6?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), [Power Grid](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07HM7WZT9?tag=cubecommonw0a-20), and [Brass: Birmingham](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1988884047?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) are waiting.

**The quick answer:** for most tables, start with **Ticket to Ride** (easier to teach, plays up to five) or **Bohnanza** (all the trading of Catan in a small card box). For a group that has played Catan for years and wants the next step, **Concordia** is the game that feels most like a grown-up Catan.

Below are 12 picks, sorted by what you liked about Catan, with a comparison table, a "which one first" guide, and a plain note on how each one differs.

## What makes a game feel like Catan?

Catan, designed by Klaus Teuber and first published in Germany in 1995, won the 1995 Spiel des Jahres and helped start the modern board game boom. The base game plays 3 to 4 in about 60 to 120 minutes, with an extension for 5 and 6. When people say they want "something like Catan," they usually mean one or more of four things:

1. **The trading.** Catan is a game you play by talking. Offers, counteroffers, and "I'll give you two sheep for a brick" are half the fun. Our lesson on [the art of the trade](/academy/catan/art-of-the-trade/) breaks down why.
2. **The shared map.** Everyone builds on the same island. Your settlement can block my road. Space is tight and it matters where you go.
3. **The resource engine.** Dice hand out wood, brick, sheep, wheat, and ore. You spend them to build things that earn you more of them next time.
4. **About an hour of play with a clear finish.** First to 10 points wins. Simple goal, lots of ways to get there.

No game on this list hits all four the way Catan does. Pick the one that doubles down on the part your group liked most.

## If you loved the trading

For many groups, the dice and the map are just an excuse to haggle. These two put the haggling at the center.

### Bohnanza

Designed by Uwe Rosenberg and first published by Amigo in 1997, Bohnanza plays 2 to 7 in 30 to 60 minutes. You are bean farmers. You plant bean cards in your fields and harvest them for coins. The catch: you must play cards in the order they sit in your hand, so you have to trade away the beans you cannot use.

**How it differs:** there is no map and no building. Every turn is a trading round, and anyone can trade with the active player. If your favorite part of Catan was the moment someone says "anyone have wheat?", Bohnanza is that moment for the whole game. It also plays more people than Catan out of the box.

### Sheriff of Nottingham

Designed by Sergio Halaban, André Zatz, and Bryan Pope and published by Arcane Wonders in 2014, Sheriff of Nottingham plays 3 to 5 in about 60 minutes. Each round one player is the Sheriff, and everyone else tries to bring goods, and maybe some contraband, past them. You can bribe, promise, and bluff.

**How it differs:** Catan's trades are mostly honest. Here, lying is part of the game. It suits groups who loved the table talk in Catan and want more drama. Our guide to [negotiation board games](/articles/negotiation-board-games/) covers it and Bohnanza as tools for teaching people to deal.

## If you loved the shared map

These three keep the feeling of building across one board where everyone's choices bump into each other.

### Carcassonne

Designed by Klaus-Jürgen Wrede and published by Hans im Glück in 2000, Carcassonne won the 2001 Spiel des Jahres. It plays 2 to 5 in 35 to 45 minutes. Each turn you draw a tile, add it to the growing map of roads, cities, and fields, and may place one of your followers to claim a feature.

**How it differs:** there are no resources to trade. The tension comes from sharing features and sneaking into someone else's city. It plays well with two, which Catan does not. Learn it with [how to play Carcassonne](/academy/carcassonne/how-to-play-carcassonne/), then read our [Carcassonne strategy guide](/articles/carcassonne-strategy/).

### Ticket to Ride

Designed by Alan R. Moon and published by Days of Wonder in 2004, Ticket to Ride won the 2004 Spiel des Jahres. It plays 2 to 5 in 30 to 60 minutes. You collect sets of colored train cards and spend them to claim routes between cities, trying to finish the secret routes on your tickets.

**How it differs:** there is no trading, and it is easier to teach than Catan. Blocking still matters: when someone takes the route you needed, you feel it. We compared the two in detail in [Catan vs Ticket to Ride](/articles/catan-vs-ticket-to-ride/). Learn it with [how to play Ticket to Ride](/academy/ticket-to-ride/how-to-play-ticket-to-ride/) and our [Ticket to Ride strategy guide](/articles/ticket-to-ride-strategy/).

### Cascadia

Designed by Randy Flynn and published by Flatout Games and AEG in 2021, Cascadia won the 2022 Spiel des Jahres. It plays 1 to 4 in 30 to 45 minutes. Each turn you take a hex habitat tile and a wildlife token and add them to your own growing landscape, scoring for animal patterns and large habitats.

**How it differs:** if the hex tiles were what you loved about Catan's board, Cascadia is all hex tiles. But each player builds their own map, so there is almost no conflict. It is the calm pick for families and for anyone who found Catan's robber too mean. Learn it with [how to play Cascadia](/academy/cascadia/how-to-play-cascadia/).

## If you loved the resource engine

Catan's core loop is simple: get resources, spend them, get more next time. These three are built entirely on that loop.

### Splendor

Designed by Marc André and published by Space Cowboys in 2014, Splendor plays 2 to 4 in about 30 minutes. You take gem chips and spend them on development cards. Each card you own works as a permanent gem discount, so your buying power grows every turn.

**How it differs:** there is no map and no trading, just the pure engine. It is quicker than Catan and plays well with two. Learn it with [how to play Splendor](/academy/splendor/how-to-play-splendor/), then try our [Splendor strategy guide](/articles/splendor-strategy/).

### Century: Spice Road

Designed by Emerson Matsuuchi and published by Plan B Games in 2017, Century: Spice Road plays 2 to 5 in 30 to 45 minutes. You collect spice cubes and play trade cards that turn cheap spices into better ones, then cash them in for point cards.

**How it differs:** it takes Catan's "two of these for one of those" idea and turns it into your hand of cards. Your trades are with the game, not with other players, so it suits groups who liked the planning more than the haggling.

### Stone Age

Designed by Michael Tummelhofer (a pen name of Bernd Brunnhofer) and published by Hans im Glück in 2008, Stone Age plays 2 to 4 in 60 to 90 minutes. You send your tribe's workers to the forest, the quarry, the river, and the fields, then roll dice to see what they bring back.

**How it differs:** dice still decide how much you collect, just like Catan, but you choose where to roll. It also feeds your people each round, which adds a gentle squeeze. Stone Age is a classic first worker placement game; our guide to [worker placement](/articles/worker-placement-explained/) explains how that system works.

## If your group is ready for the next step

These four are longer and deeper. They are the games Catan players often reach for after a few years.

### Lords of Waterdeep

Designed by Peter Lee and Rodney Thompson and published by Wizards of the Coast in 2012, Lords of Waterdeep plays 2 to 5 in 60 to 120 minutes. You place agents around a fantasy city to recruit adventurers, shown as colored cubes, then spend them to complete quests.

**How it differs:** it swaps Catan's dice for worker placement, so there is no luck in what you collect. The goal structure, "gather these resources, cash them in for points," will feel familiar. It is a smooth step up for groups who want more control.

### Concordia

Designed by Mac Gerdts and published by PD-Verlag in 2013, Concordia plays 2 to 5 in about 90 minutes. You send colonists along roads and sea lanes across the Roman Empire, build houses in cities, and collect the goods those cities produce. Your hand of cards is how you act.

**How it differs:** of everything on this list, it feels the most like a grown-up Catan. You still spread across a shared map and build an economy. But there are no dice and no robber, and scoring is hidden until the end, so it rewards long-term planning. There is also no player-to-player trading.

### Power Grid

Designed by Friedemann Friese and first published in 2004, Power Grid plays 2 to 6 in about 2 hours. You bid on power plants, buy fuel from a shared market, and connect cities on the map to your network.

**How it differs:** it is heavier and runs longer. Instead of trading with each other, you compete through auctions and a fuel market whose prices rise as everyone buys. If your group liked the money side of Catan, Power Grid takes it seriously. Learn it with [how to play Power Grid](/academy/power-grid/how-to-play-power-grid/).

### Brass: Birmingham

Designed by Gavan Brown, Matt Tolman, and Martin Wallace and published by Roxley in 2018, Brass: Birmingham plays 2 to 4 in 60 to 120 minutes. You build canals, then railways, and industries across the English Midlands, and your network shares coal, iron, and beer with everyone else's.

**How it differs:** this is the heaviest game here. It keeps Catan's shared map and its "your build helps me too" tension, but turns them into a long economic puzzle with no dice. Save it for a group that already loves strategy games. Learn it with [how to play Brass: Birmingham](/academy/brass-birmingham/how-to-play-brass-birmingham/).

## FAQ

### What is the closest game to Catan?

Concordia is the closest for experienced groups: a shared map, an economy to build, and goods to spend. For newer players, Ticket to Ride and Carcassonne are the usual next steps. They are lighter and quicker to teach.

### What is a good game like Catan for two players?

Catan itself is built for three or more. For two, try Carcassonne, Splendor, or Cascadia. All three play well at two.

### What game is like Catan but with more trading?

Bohnanza. Trading is the whole game, and every player can join a trade on every turn. Sheriff of Nottingham adds bluffing on top.

### Is Catan a good gateway game in 2026?

Yes, for groups that enjoy talking and dealing. If your table is newer or quieter, see our list of [gateway board games](/articles/gateway-board-games/) for other first picks.

### Should I get a Catan expansion or a new game?

If your group still loves Catan, an expansion adds variety without a new teach. If the game has started to feel samey, a new box changes more. Not sure you have mastered the base game yet? Our guide on [how to win at Catan](/articles/how-to-win-at-catan/) and the lesson on [the four core strategies](/academy/catan/four-core-strategies/) are good places to start.
