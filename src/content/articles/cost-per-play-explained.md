Every board game has two prices. The first is printed on the shelf tag. The second is what the game actually costs you, and it depends on how many times it hits the table, how many people sit around it, and how long each session runs. That second number is cost per play, and once you learn to see it, the shelf tag stops meaning much on its own.

This is the pillar article for The Math of Game Night, a Ludarium series on what game night really costs and how to make every dollar do more. You do not need a spreadsheet for any of it. You need one formula and the habit of asking it a few questions before you buy.

Here is the formula:

**Cost per play = price ÷ plays ÷ players ÷ hours**

That is the whole thing. The rest of this article walks through each division, shows you what it does to a real-looking number, and explains why one of those four steps matters more than the other three combined.

## What is cost per play, in plain terms?

Cost per play is the price of a game divided by the amount of fun it produces, where "fun" is measured the only way we can measure it: in hours at the table, per person. It turns a one-time purchase into a rate, like dollars per hour, so you can compare a $15 card game to an $80 strategy box without guessing.

The point is not to reduce game night to accounting. The point is to notice that a game's price is a bad predictor of its value. A cheap game nobody plays is expensive. An expensive game your group plays every month for years is one of the best deals in your house.

Let's use one illustration the whole way through: say a $40 game, played 20 times, with 4 players, for 1 hour per session. Those numbers are made up for the walkthrough, but they are in the range of a typical family or strategy title in the US, where most new games land somewhere between $25 and $40.

## Step one: price ÷ plays

Start with the shelf tag and divide by how many times the game gets played.

- $40 ÷ 20 plays = **$2 per play**

Already the number looks different. A $40 game that gets 20 plays costs you two dollars every time you open the box. That is less than most things you would buy at a gas station on the way to game night.

This is the division that separates a game that becomes part of your life from a game that becomes decoration. It is also the step with the widest range. The same $40 game could see 2 plays or 200. Your job before buying is to estimate that number as truthfully as you can, and we will come back to how.

Notice the brutal case at this step. If plays equals zero, the math breaks. $40 ÷ 0 is undefined, which in practice means infinite. That is the [Shelf of Shame](/articles/shelf-of-shame-infinite-cost/), and it gets its own article in this series.

## Step two: ÷ players (the step that matters for groups)

Now divide by the number of people at the table. Each play of the game delivered an evening to four people, not one.

- $2 per play ÷ 4 players = **$0.50 per player per play**

Fifty cents a seat. This is where cost per play stops being a personal budgeting trick and becomes a group strategy, because here is the thing the division reveals: the game's value was already shared by everyone at the table the moment it was opened. One copy produced four people's worth of fun. A second copy of the same game in a second player's house does not produce four more people's worth of fun. It produces the same four people, playing the same game, with a redundant box.

That is the whole logic behind [The One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/): a play group needs one copy of a game, not one copy per person. If four friends each buy their own [Catan](https://link.amazon/B0h8BDGR2) at about $40, the group has spent about $160 to own a game that only ever gets played at one table at a time. Three of those boxes add roughly $120 to $150 of cost and zero plays. The ÷ players step is the proof.

The ÷ players step also tells you which games are good buys for a group. A party game that seats eight divides its price by eight. A tight two-player game divides by two. Neither is wrong, but the eight-player game has an easier path to a low number.

The most extreme example we know is a Magic: The Gathering Cube, where one person's curated card pool gets drafted by eight players, week after week. One copy. Eight seats. Dozens of sessions. The ÷ players step turns a real investment into pocket change per seat, and it is [the best proof we have that one copy serves many](/articles/cube-perfect-shared-library/).

## Step three: ÷ hours

Finally, divide by how long each session lasts. A one-hour game and a four-hour game are different products, and your evening is the thing being spent.

- $0.50 per player per play ÷ 1 hour = **$0.50 per player per hour**

For our illustration the last step does not change the number, because the session was an hour. But it matters whenever you compare games of different lengths. A $60 epic that runs four hours and a $15 filler that runs 20 minutes can land in the same place once you account for time, and you can only see that if hours are in the formula.

A big box that takes an entire evening may get played six times a year and still produce more hours of fun than a light game played every week. Hours are the honest unit. Plays are just how you get there.

Now change one input. Give the big hobby game 2 plays instead of 8, which is what happens when a game is too heavy for the group or nobody wants to teach it. The cost per player-hour jumps to more than $5. Same game, same price, five times the real cost. Plays are the lever.

## How to use cost per play before you buy

The formula is most valuable before money leaves your account. You cannot know the final numbers, but you can estimate them, and even a rough estimate catches the worst purchases.

1. **Estimate plays truthfully.** Ask: who in my group will want this, and how often do we meet? If you play twice a month and this game would get the table one night in four, that is six plays a year. Be conservative. Hype inflates this number more than anything else.
2. **Count the players you actually have.** Not the number on the box. If your regular group is three people, a game that shines at five will divide by three at best and may not get played at all. The [match-games-to-your-group](/articles/match-games-to-your-group/) test applies here.
3. **Be realistic about hours.** A three-hour game needs a three-hour evening. If your sessions run 90 minutes, it will get set up less often than you hope.
4. **Check the shared shelf first.** Does someone in the group already own it? If so, the cost per play of a second copy is almost always infinite, because it will create no plays that would not already happen. That is the [second-copy test](/articles/when-a-second-copy-is-worth-it/).
5. **Compare to the $0 option.** Can you play it at a game night, a café, or a friend's table first? [Play before you pay](/articles/play-before-you-pay/) turns your estimate of plays from a guess into an observation.

If the estimate comes out under about a dollar per player-hour, you are in healthy territory. If it comes out higher, that is not a veto. It is a prompt to ask why, and whether a cheaper game would do the same job.

## How to use cost per play after you buy

After the purchase, the formula becomes a mirror. Track your plays, even roughly, and once a year look at what the numbers say about your shelf.

You do not need software for this. A note on your phone with a game name and a tally mark works. Some groups keep a shared note that everyone can add to, which fits naturally with the idea of a [shared shelf](/articles/audit-your-group-shelf/) spread across several homes. The Ludarium app, in development, will let subscribers track the games they play and like, but a pencil does the job today.

What you will find, almost every time:

- A handful of games carry the whole collection. They get played constantly and their cost per play is pennies.
- A larger middle group gets played occasionally and sits in the fine-to-good range.
- A few games have not been played in a year or more. These are your real expenses, and [tracking your plays changes what you buy](/articles/tracking-plays-changes-what-you-buy/) more than any review ever will.

The mirror is there to make the next purchase better. If every game that got fewer than three plays last year has something in common, you have just learned something about your group that no box can tell you.

## Why ÷ players is the secret to a cheap hobby

It is worth saying once more, because it is the step people skip. The price of a game is paid once. The fun is distributed to everyone at the table, every time. That means the single best thing you can do for your cost per play is not to buy cheaper games. It is to put more of the right people at the table, more often, with one copy between you.

This is why a play group is a financial strategy and not just a social one. Four friends who each buy different games and bring them to the same table have built a library four times larger than any one of them paid for, and every game in it divides its price by four seats. When each person [claims a lane](/articles/claim-a-lane/) and collects a different genre, the overlap disappears and the library covers more ground for the same money.

The result is a hobby that can run at about a dollar an hour, per person, or less. One hobbyist's back-of-envelope works out to about $300 spent in a year against about 300 hours played, which is a dollar an hour before you even divide by the friends at the table. We compare that to other ways of spending an evening in [Board Games vs. Everything Else](/articles/board-games-vs-everything-else/).

## What cost per play does not capture

Now the honest caveat. The formula measures hours, not joy, and those are not the same thing.

A game you played once at a friend's house on the night you all stayed up until two in the morning laughing has a terrible cost per play and may be the most valuable box you own. A long campaign game like [Gloomhaven](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01LZXVN4P?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) can take a year to finish and produce memories a tally mark cannot hold. A two-player game you play with your spouse on quiet Tuesday nights divides by only two, and it would be silly to let the arithmetic tell you it is a worse purchase than a party game.

So use the formula for what it is good at: catching the purchases that will never get played, choosing between two games that would fill the same role, and seeing that the group, not the shelf, is where value comes from. Then set it down. The formula divides by players because players are the thing that makes a game worth anything. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### What is cost per play for board games?

Cost per play is a game's price divided by the number of times it is played, then by the number of players, then by hours per session. It converts a one-time purchase into a rate, such as dollars per player per hour, so you can compare games of different prices, lengths, and player counts on equal footing.

### How do you calculate the cost per play of a board game?

Take the price and divide by plays, then by players, then by hours. For example, a $40 game played 20 times by 4 players for 1 hour each works out to $40 ÷ 20 ÷ 4 ÷ 1, which is about $0.50 per player per hour. Estimate the inputs before buying and track them afterward.

### What is a good cost per play for a board game?

There is no official threshold, but many hobbyists treat about a dollar per player-hour or less as healthy. One common back-of-envelope figure is about $300 spent per year against about 300 hours played, which is roughly $1 per hour before dividing by the number of friends at the table.

### Why does dividing by players matter so much?

Because a single copy delivers its fun to everyone at the table at once. A second copy in a second player's home does not add plays or players; it only adds price. That is why a play group needs one copy of a game, not one per person, and why duplicates are the most common wasted dollars in the hobby.

### Does cost per play mean cheaper games are always better?

No. Cheap games that never get played cost more per hour than expensive games played monthly. Cost per play rewards games that fit your group and get to the table, regardless of price. It also ignores things that matter, like memorable sessions and the people you play with, so treat it as a tool, not a verdict.
