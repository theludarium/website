The board game lounge business has a model problem. For forty years, the place you went to play games was the hobby shop, and the hobby shop made its money selling boxes. The tables in the back were a cost. Coworking, which arrived around the same time as the modern board game boom, made the opposite bet: the room is the product, and people will pay monthly to belong to it. This article looks at what coworking understood, where game stores went wrong, and what a lounge built on the better model looks like.

## How does the traditional game store make money?

A friendly local game store is a retailer. Revenue comes from selling board games, Magic: The Gathering product, miniatures, paint, and dice. Play space exists to drive sales: you come for Friday Night Magic, you buy a booster box.

That model has three structural weaknesses.

**Margins are thin and getting thinner.** Online retailers sell the same box cheaper. The store cannot compete on price, so it competes on service and community, but it does not charge for either.

**The best customers cost the most.** The regular who plays every night uses the tables, the bathroom, and the staff's attention, and may buy one sleeve pack a month. The store needs them for atmosphere and cannot afford them.

**The space is designed for stock, not people.** Folding tables, fluorescent light, metal chairs, and a wall of product. It works for a tournament. It does not work for a date, a family, or an adult who has not played since college.

None of this is a knock on store owners, who are often the most generous people in the hobby. It is a knock on a model that asks them to give away the thing people value most.

## What did coworking understand about rooms?

Coworking started from a simple observation: freelancers and remote workers were already sitting in coffee shops for eight hours and buying one latte. They wanted a desk, Wi-Fi, and other humans. What they were missing was permission to stay.

Coworking sold the permission. A monthly membership bought you a seat, a key, a community, and a reason to come back every day. The desk was not the product. The room full of people like you was.

Whatever you think of the companies that scaled it, the core insight held, and it transfers directly to the [game lounge membership](/articles/game-lounge-membership/) model:

- Charge for belonging, not for transactions.
- Design the space for the regular, not the walk-in.
- Make the community the amenity.
- Keep revenue predictable so you can invest in the room.

The game store sells boxes and gives away the room. Coworking sells the room and does not care about boxes. One of those is a better business when the room is what people came for.

## Why does space design matter so much for a board game lounge business?

Because the room is what you are selling, and most rooms where people play games were not designed for it.

Walk into a hobby shop and a coworking space back to back and the difference is physical. The coworking space has good light, acoustic control, chairs you can sit in for six hours, a kitchen, and a quiet area. The hobby shop has a tournament hall.

A lounge that wants adults to pay monthly needs the coworking standard applied to play:

| Element | Game store default | Lounge standard |
|---|---|---|
| Tables | Folding, 30 inches, wobbly | Solid, correct height, room for elbows and boards |
| Chairs | Metal stacking | Padded, back support, four-hour comfort |
| Light | Overhead fluorescent | Warm, bright on the table, soft on the room |
| Sound | Hard surfaces, echo | Soft materials, zones for loud and quiet |
| Food and drink | Vending machine | Coffee, beer, food you can eat with one hand |
| Layout | Rows for events | Mixed: two-tops, six-tops, a campaign room |

## What did coworking get wrong that a lounge should avoid?

Coworking's biggest failures were about scale and leases, not about the idea. The lessons are worth stating plainly.

**Do not sign leases your membership cannot cover.** The companies that collapsed took long, expensive leases against short, flexible memberships. A lounge should grow into space, not ahead of it.

**Do not confuse amenities with community.** Free beer taps and neon signs did not make anyone friends. A weekly table with the same six people does. Spend on the host, not the decor.

**Do not pretend to be a tech company.** A lounge is a hospitality business with a membership layer. Its economics look like a gym or a club, not a software platform.

**Do not forget the walk-in.** Coworking's best spaces still sold day passes, because the day pass is the trial. A lounge needs a [public game night](/articles/board-game-night-near-me/) that strangers can join, or the membership funnel has no top.

## What does a lounge built on the coworking model look like?

Take the membership idea, apply the space standards, keep the hobby shop's generosity, and you get something like this.

- **Daytime:** quiet, coffee, a few two-player tables, remote workers who break for a game at lunch. Close to coworking in feel.
- **Evening:** the room fills. Public game night on one side, a reserved campaign table on the other, a cube draft in the back room.
- **Weekends:** families in the morning, groups in the afternoon, a louder crowd at night.
- **Revenue:** memberships first, then food and drink, then events, then a small, curated retail shelf of games the staff love and will teach.
- **Staff:** at least one person on every shift whose only job is to teach games and remember names.

This is the shape The Ludarium is building toward in Indianapolis. Coworking, but the work is playing together. The near-term version is content, public events, and a corporate tabletop events service for teams. The long-term version is the room.

## Can the game store and the lounge coexist?

Yes, and they should. The store sells you Wingspan. The lounge is where you play it every Thursday with people who also own it. A city with both is better off than a city with one.

The mistake is asking one business to do both jobs. Retail wants square footage for stock and fast table turns for events. Belonging wants comfortable chairs, long evenings, and regulars who buy nothing but keep coming. Those pull in opposite directions, and the store has been losing the tug-of-war for decades.

## Why does this matter beyond the business?

Because adults need these rooms. Sociologist [Ray Oldenburg](https://www.pps.org/article/roldenburg) called them third places, the spots that are neither home nor work where people are known by name, and he argued that without them life shrinks to a home-to-work-and-back shuttle. The [U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory on loneliness](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf) made the health case: the mortality impact of social disconnection is on par with smoking up to 15 cigarettes a day.

A lounge that pays its rent is a third place that stays open. The business model is not separate from the mission. It is what keeps the lights on for the Tuesday table.

## FAQ

### Is a board game lounge a profitable business?

It can be, if revenue comes from memberships, food, drink, and events rather than from selling games. Venues that rely on a cover charge alone are exposed to slow weeknights and seasonal dips in a way that membership venues are not.

### What is the difference between a board game cafe and a board game lounge?

A cafe usually charges per visit and sells coffee or beer alongside the games. A lounge usually runs on memberships and is designed for regulars rather than walk-ins.

### How much space does a board game lounge need?

Enough for a mix of table sizes, a quiet zone, and a small kitchen. Plenty of venues start small and grow into a second room once the regulars are in place.

### Why do game stores struggle financially?

Thin retail margins, online price competition, and the cost of providing free play space that generates community but not revenue.
