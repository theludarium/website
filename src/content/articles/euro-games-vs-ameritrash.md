The eurogame vs Ameritrash debate is the oldest argument in modern board gaming, and also the least useful. A eurogame is, roughly, a game built around efficient decisions, indirect competition, and low luck: think Catan, Agricola, or Puerto Rico. "Ameritrash" is the tongue-in-cheek label for games built around theme, direct conflict, dice, and drama: think Risk, Arkham Horror, or Twilight Imperium. Most games released today sit somewhere between the two.

This article explains what each label means, where the words came from, how to spot the traits at the table, and why the distinction matters less every year while still being worth knowing.

## What is a eurogame?

The style grew out of a [German design movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurogame) that took shape in the late 1970s and 1980s and boomed through the 1990s. Designers like Klaus Teuber, Reiner Knizia, Wolfgang Kramer, and Uwe Rosenberg built games for a family market where the Spiel des Jahres award could sell hundreds of thousands of copies. That market had preferences, and the preferences became a style.

The classic eurogame traits:

- **Low luck.** Dice are rare or tamed. What randomness exists usually comes before your decision, not after it, so you plan around it rather than pray.
- **Indirect conflict.** You compete for shared resources, spaces, or turn order. You rarely attack another player directly, and you almost never remove them from the game.
- **Everyone finishes.** Player elimination is close to forbidden. The game ends on a timer or a trigger, and everyone counts points.
- **Economy over story.** You are converting wood to houses to points. The theme is a coat of paint on a machine, and sometimes the paint is thin.
- **Shorter, tighter play.** Sixty to ninety minutes is typical, with a clear arc and little downtime.

Catan is the gateway example: trading and placement matter far more than the dice, nobody gets knocked out, and the game ends when someone reaches ten points. <!-- AFFILIATE --> Ticket to Ride, Carcassonne, Azul, and Wingspan all live comfortably in the euro camp.

The term "German-style game" was the original name. "Eurogame" took over as designers from France, Italy, and elsewhere joined in, and because it sounded less like a nationality and more like a genre.

## What does Ameritrash mean?

"Ameritrash" started as a jab and was adopted as a badge. The word [shows up in Usenet posts around 2000](https://www.wargamer.com/board-games/ameritrash) and took hold on BoardGameGeek in the mid-2000s, where it was used for the big, loud, luck-heavy American games that euro fans did not enjoy. Fans of those games liked the word more than the people hurling it, and it stuck.

The classic Ameritrash traits:

- **Theme first.** The game exists to make you feel like a space admiral, a dungeon crawler, or a cowboy. Mechanics serve the fiction, not the other way around.
- **Direct conflict.** You attack other players. You can take what they have. You can eliminate them.
- **Dice and drama.** Luck is a feature. A long shot that pays off is the story you tell afterward.
- **Plastic and scale.** Miniatures, large boards, long boxes, and long playtimes. Four hours is not unusual.
- **Asymmetry.** Players often have unique powers, factions, or characters.

Risk is the common ancestor. Axis & Allies, Talisman, Arkham Horror, Twilight Imperium, and Nemesis carry the line forward. <!-- AFFILIATE --> Many of these games have "attack" on the card and a small army on the table.

Some players prefer "thematic game" or "American-style game" as neutral alternatives. Those are fine, and more polite, but they lack the bite that made the original label memorable.

## How do the two styles compare at the table?

| Trait | Eurogame | Ameritrash |
|---|---|---|
| Where luck sits | Before your decision | After your decision |
| How you compete | Indirectly, for shared things | Directly, against people |
| Elimination | Almost never | Common |
| Typical length | 60–120 minutes | 2–4+ hours |
| What drives the design | The system | The story |
| Components | Wood, cardboard, cubes | Plastic, miniatures, lots of cards |
| Classic example | Agricola | Twilight Imperium |

Neither column is better. They produce different evenings. A euro night is quiet, focused, and ends with everyone comparing scores within a few points. An Ameritrash night is loud, swings wildly, and ends with one person triumphant and a story about the roll that did it. Both are good nights.

## Why do people argue about it?

Because it is really an argument about what games are for.

Euro fans tend to value fairness and control. If you lose, you want it to be because you played worse, not because a die hated you. The complaint about Ameritrash is that it lets a weak player win and makes a strong player sit out for three hours after an early elimination.

Ameritrash fans tend to value feeling and memory. If a game produces no stories, what was the point? The complaint about euros is that they are spreadsheets with pictures, that nobody remembers a game of Puerto Rico from 2015, and that "indirect conflict" is a polite phrase for multiplayer solitaire.

Both complaints are partly right, which is why the argument never ends. Our piece on [luck vs skill in board games](/articles/luck-vs-skill-board-games/) covers the underlying trade-off in more depth.

## Are most games now hybrids?

Yes, and this is the most important thing to understand about the labels in 2026.

Designers on both sides spent twenty years stealing each other's best ideas. Euro designers learned that theme sells and that a little drama keeps people awake. Thematic designers learned that player elimination empties the table and that a clean scoring system beats a vague one.

The results are everywhere:

- **Scythe** has miniatures, asymmetric factions, and a map full of mechs, and almost no fighting actually happens because the economics punish it. Euro bones in an Ameritrash body. <!-- AFFILIATE -->
- **Blood Rage** is a Viking brawl with drafting, area majority, and a tight scoring engine. It plays like a euro that lets you hit people.
- **Pandemic** is cooperative and card-driven, so the euro-vs-American axis barely applies. Yet it has a strong theme, dramatic losses, and a dice-free design.
- **Gloomhaven** is a dungeon crawl with no dice at all. Every attack is a card play with a modifier deck you can tune.
- **Root** gives each faction a completely different rulebook and puts them on a map to fight, but the fights are governed by a euro designer's sense of balance.

Hobby writers sometimes call this middle ground "Euro-trash" or "hybrid," and it describes the majority of well-reviewed games released in the last decade. The labels survive mainly as shorthand for which end of the spectrum a game leans toward, not as boxes a game fits inside.

## Which style should your group play?

Watch what people do after the game ends.

If they compare scores and talk about what they could have done differently, lean euro. Try Wingspan, Azul, or Ticket to Ride, and read our [guide to gateway games](/articles/gateway-board-games/) for more.

If they retell the moment someone's plan blew up, lean thematic. Try Cosmic Encounter, King of Tokyo, or a cooperative game like Pandemic that keeps the drama without the elimination.

If your group is mixed, which most are, hybrids are the answer. Scythe, Blood Rage, Root, and Dune: Imperium each give the strategists a system to optimize and the storytellers a reason to care. A group that stays mixed is a healthier group than one that purges its dice-rollers or its spreadsheet people.

## FAQ

### Is Catan a eurogame?

Yes. It is the game that brought the eurogame style to North America, with trading, low direct conflict, no elimination, and a points-based end.

### Is Ameritrash an insult?

It started as one and is now mostly affectionate. If you want a neutral term, say "thematic game" or "American-style game."

### What is the difference between a eurogame and a strategy game?

Most eurogames are strategy games, but not all strategy games are euros. Chess and Twilight Imperium are strategy games; neither is a euro.

### Are cooperative games euro or Ameritrash?

Neither, really. The axis is about how players compete, and co-ops remove that. They borrow from both traditions.

## The label is not the table

The eurogame vs Ameritrash argument is fun to have once and tiresome to have twice. What matters is whether the people at your table want control or drama, and whether you can find the game that gives them enough of each. Most games now do. The game is the reason. The people are the point.
