Most lists of activities for caregivers assume you have a free afternoon, a sitter, and the energy to use them. If you are caring for a parent, a spouse, or a child with serious needs, you probably have none of those. What you might have is an hour, now and then, and a kind of tiredness that sleep does not reach.

This article is about what to do with that hour, and why a game is often a better use of it than rest. It covers games you can play alone, games you can play with the person you care for, and the one game night a week that keeps caregivers from disappearing. It ends with where to find real support, because an hour off is not a substitute for help.

## Why is a game a better break than rest?

Because the tiredness of caregiving is not mainly physical. It is the weight of constant vigilance, of being the one who notices, decides, and remembers everything. Lying down does not turn that off. Many caregivers describe trying to nap and spending the hour listening for sounds from the next room.

A game turns it off for a while, for a specific reason: it takes your whole attention. You cannot plan tomorrow's medication schedule while deciding which Azul tiles to take. Psychologists call this absorption, and Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's work on [flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_(psychology)) described it as arising when the challenge of a task is balanced against your skill; a [2025 study of more than 500 board game players](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10902-024-00846-4) found flow ran highest when both challenge and skill were high. A good game hits that balance almost by design. For an hour, you are a person playing a game, not a caregiver.

A game with other people adds a second thing: being treated as yourself. Caregivers often say that every conversation they have is about the person they care for. At a game table, nobody asks. You are the one who just blocked the red route.

## What can you play alone in an hour?

Sometimes the hour arrives at 9 p.m. with nobody around. Solo games are good company for exactly that.

| Game | Time | Why it suits a tired caregiver |
|---|---|---|
| Patience or solitaire with real cards | 10 to 20 min | No setup, no rules to learn, hands busy |
| Jigsaw puzzle, left out on a table | Any | Can be picked up and put down between interruptions |
| Friday | 25 min | A solo card game that is absorbing without being heavy |
| Cascadia solo mode | 30 min | Calm, pretty, puzzle-like |
| Wingspan solo automa | 45 to 60 min | Deep enough to lose yourself in; only if the hour is real |
| Crossword or sudoku | 10 to 30 min | Portable; works in a waiting room |
| A phone app of a board game | 10 min | Fine, but a physical game is more of a break from screens |

The jigsaw deserves emphasis. A puzzle left out on a card table is the most interruptible game there is. Five pieces while the kettle boils counts.

If you want more options, [solo board games](/articles/best-solo-board-games/) goes deeper into playing alone without it feeling lonely.

## What can you play with the person you care for?

Sometimes the hour off is better spent changing the shape of the time together than escaping it. A game can turn an afternoon of managing into an afternoon of company.

**If they are cognitively well but physically limited.** Card games with large-print decks and card holders, Rummikub with the large-number edition, dominoes, Yahtzee with a dice cup, Qwirkle. Good light on the table matters more than the game.

**If they have memory loss.** Play for the moment, not the score. Dominoes, matching games, sorting a deck by suit, large picture cards, balloon volleyball. Familiar old games often survive long after new rules cannot be learned; a lifelong euchre player may still play a passable hand with a little prompting. Expect the rules to drift and let them. If the game produces frustration, stop and do something else. The goal is a pleasant half hour, and a pleasant half hour is a success.

**If they are a child with a serious illness.** Cooperative games remove the sting of losing on a bad day: Outfoxed!, Forbidden Island, The Crew for older kids. Short games are better than long ones, because energy can vanish mid-turn.

In every case, let the game be shorter than you planned and stop while it is still good.

## Why does a caregiver need one game night a week?

Because caregivers disappear. Not dramatically. Gradually, as the standing commitments drop away one at a time until the only people they see are the person they care for and the pharmacist.

The research on isolation is not gentle about what that costs. The [U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf) associated social disconnection with higher risk of heart disease, stroke, dementia, depression, and early death, and an [AARP poll](https://www.aarp.org/press/releases/2018-11-30-caregivers-people-with-dementia-greater-risk-social-isolation-loneliness.html) of dementia caregivers found that more than half said their social life had suffered and nearly 45 percent said they felt alone. Julianne Holt-Lunstad's [2010 meta-analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316) found that people with stronger social ties had about a 50 percent greater likelihood of survival over the study periods.

A single standing game night, weekly or even every other week, is the most defensible social commitment a caregiver can keep, for three reasons.

1. **It is a fixed time**, which makes arranging coverage possible. "Thursdays, seven to nine" can be handed to a sibling, a neighbor, or a respite service. "Sometime when I get a break" cannot.
2. **It requires nothing of you.** You do not host, cook, or plan. You show up and someone hands you a hand of cards.
3. **It is with people who know you as a person.** That is the part sleep cannot provide.

If you do not have a group, you do not need to start one. Find one. Libraries, community centers, game cafes, and places of worship run standing games, and a game lounge is built for exactly the person who needs to walk in alone and sit down. [Finding a table after losing a spouse](/articles/games-after-losing-a-spouse/) describes the same search from a different season of life, and most of it applies.

## How do you actually get the hour?

This is the honest obstacle, so here is the honest answer: you ask for it, specifically and in advance.

- Name the slot. "I need Thursday from 6:30 to 9:30, every week." Vague requests get vague help.
- Ask the people who have offered. "Let me know if you need anything" is an offer. Cash it in with a specific time.
- Look into respite care. Many Area Agencies on Aging, hospices, and disease-specific organizations fund or arrange a few hours of in-home respite. Adult day programs cover whole days.
- Trade with another caregiver. Two people in the same situation can cover each other's game night.
- Accept imperfect coverage. The person covering will not do it your way. That is fine for three hours.

## Where to find more than an hour

An hour off helps. It does not fix caregiver exhaustion, and if you are reading this at the end of your rope, please reach beyond a game night.

Your doctor is a reasonable first call; caregiver strain is something they see often and can help with directly or by referral. The Family Caregiver Alliance, the Alzheimer's Association helpline, and your local Area Agency on Aging can connect you to respite services, support groups, and counseling. Caregiver support groups, in person or online, are full of people who understand without explanation, and many of them meet around a table with coffee and, sometimes, a deck of cards. If you notice signs of depression in yourself, trouble sleeping, no interest in anything, or thoughts that everyone would be better off without you, please tell someone today.

The game is the reason. The people are the point. For a caregiver, the people include you.

## FAQ

### What are good activities for caregivers?

Short, absorbing activities that can be interrupted: a jigsaw left out, solitaire, a 20-minute solo card game, a walk, and one standing weekly social commitment such as a game night with people who know you outside your caregiving role.

### How can a caregiver take a break at home?

Pick something that takes your full attention for a defined time, such as a puzzle or a solo game, and treat it as a scheduled break rather than filler. Absorption rests the mind in a way lying down often does not.

### What games can you play with someone who has dementia?

Dominoes, matching and sorting games, large picture cards, and familiar old card games with gentle prompting. Play for enjoyment in the moment, let the rules drift, and stop if frustration appears.

### Where can caregivers get respite?

Area Agencies on Aging, hospices, the Alzheimer's Association, the Family Caregiver Alliance, and adult day programs arrange or fund respite care. Ask your doctor for local referrals, and ask friends for specific, scheduled hours.
