Cooperative games with hidden information are the cure for the quarterback problem. When nobody can see everything, nobody can run everything. Each player holds a piece the others cannot, and the team only wins if every piece gets played well.

This article is a list of twelve co-ops built that way, with a short explanation of the design tricks that make them work. If you have ever watched one person play Pandemic while three others moved pawns on command, this is the shelf you want.

## What is the quarterback problem, briefly?

In a co-op with fully open information, the most experienced or most confident player can calculate the best move for everyone. The others become hands. The game is still cooperative in name, but one person is playing it.

We wrote a full piece on the [quarterback problem](/articles/quarterback-problem-cooperative-games/) from a design angle. Here the question is practical: which games prevent it, and how?

## How do designers stop one player from taking over?

Four main tools show up again and again.

1. **Hidden hands.** You hold cards nobody else can see. Hanabi inverts this so you cannot see your own.
2. **Restricted talk.** The rules forbid discussing your cards, or allow only a scripted signal. The Crew and The Mind are the clearest examples.
3. **Time pressure.** A real-time clock means nobody has time to compute everyone's move. Space Alert and Kitchen Rush do this.
4. **Asymmetric roles with private goals.** Each player has a secret objective or a unique view of the board, so no single person has the full picture. Mysterium's ghost knows the answer but cannot speak.

Most of the games below use at least two of these at once.

## The 12 games

### 1. Hanabi

You hold your cards facing outward. You know everyone's hand except your own. Clues are limited and expensive. The entire game is figuring out what you hold from what people have chosen not to tell you. The founding text of the limited-communication co-op.

### 2. The Crew: Mission Deep Sea

Trick-taking with tasks and almost no talking. Each player's hand is private, and the only signal is one card shown once per mission. We have a [full article on The Crew](/articles/the-crew-card-game/), and it is our top recommendation for any group with a quarterback.

### 3. The Mind

Play numbered cards in ascending order, across every hand, without speaking or signaling. There is nothing to quarterback because there is nothing to say. Strangely moving when it works.

### 4. Mysterium

One player is a ghost who communicates only by handing out surreal picture cards. The psychics interpret. The ghost knows everything and can say nothing; the psychics can say anything and know nothing. The split is perfect.

### 5. Magic Maze

Real-time, no talking, and each player controls a direction of movement rather than a character. You can move any hero north; your neighbor can move any hero east. The pressure to communicate without words produces a specific kind of frantic staring that groups remember for years.

### 6. Space Alert

A ten-minute soundtrack calls out threats while the crew programs actions on a spaceship. There is no time to coordinate everyone. The best you can do is shout a plan and trust. Then the timer ends and you watch, in silence, whether it worked.

### 7. Codenames: Duet

Each side of the table has a key card showing which words are agents. You know your half; your partner knows theirs. Clues are one word and one number. Nobody can take over because nobody has the whole key.

### 8. Sky Team

Two players land a plane by placing dice. During the dice phase you may not talk. Your partner's dice are private until placed. Short, elegant, and tense enough that couples should decide beforehand who is pilot.

### 9. Letter Jam

Each player has a letter card facing away from them. Others give clues by spelling words with the visible letters. You deduce your own letter from clues built around it. A word-game cousin of Hanabi.

### 10. Bomb Busters

A newer entry where players hold hidden wire cards and must cut them in a safe order, using limited information about what others hold. Light, fast, and designed from the ground up around private hands.

### 11. The Game

Players try to empty a deck of numbered cards onto four shared piles, two counting up and two counting down. Hands are private and you may not say numbers. You can say "please don't play there" and little else. A quiet, portable puzzle that rewards reading your teammates' hesitation.

### 12. Shadows over Camelot

A co-op with a traitor. Everyone's loyalty card is hidden. A player who tries to run the table invites suspicion, which is its own corrective. We wrote more about this style in our article on [semi-cooperative games](/articles/semi-cooperative-board-games/).

## Which anti-quarterback mechanism fits your group?

| Mechanism | Example games | Feel | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hidden hands | Hanabi, The Crew, Letter Jam | Quiet, deductive | Frustration for talkers early on |
| Restricted talk | The Mind, Sky Team | Tense, focused | Rules lawyering about what counts as talking |
| Real time | Magic Maze, Space Alert | Loud, chaotic | Overwhelming for anxious players |
| Split knowledge | Mysterium, Codenames: Duet | Social, interpretive | The knowing player can get bored |
| Hidden traitor | Shadows over Camelot | Suspicious, dramatic | Hurt feelings if not framed as a game |

Pick by temperament. A thoughtful, quiet group will love Hanabi and hate Magic Maze. A loud, social group is the reverse.

## Does hidden information make co-ops less cooperative?

The opposite, in our experience. Open-information co-ops let a group feel cooperative while one person works. Hidden-information co-ops force actual cooperation, because the only way to win is to trust someone else's judgment about cards you cannot see.

That trust is the thing people remember. Winning a mission of The Crew because your teammate correctly read a low lead as a plea for help is a better story than winning Pandemic because Dave told everyone where to go.

## How do you handle a quarterback in an open-information game?

Sometimes you want to play Pandemic or Spirit Island anyway. A few habits help.

- Each player narrates their own turn before anyone else speaks.
- Questions are allowed; instructions are not. "What are you thinking?" is fine. "Go to Cairo" is not.
- Put a timer on group discussion. Thirty seconds, then the active player decides.
- Rotate who speaks first in planning. The quiet player goes first.

None of these work as well as a game that simply removes the option. But they turn a solo performance into something closer to a team.

## FAQ

### What is the quarterback problem in cooperative games?

It is when one experienced or assertive player directs everyone's moves in a co-op with open information, leaving the others with nothing to decide.

### What is the best co-op game with no quarterback problem?

The Crew: Mission Deep Sea for most groups. Hanabi if your group prefers something quieter and more deductive.

### Do hidden-information co-ops work with new players?

Yes, often better than open ones. New players cannot be overruled, so they learn by making their own decisions from the first game.

### Are there real-time cooperative games that avoid the quarterback problem?

Magic Maze and Space Alert both use a clock so nobody has time to plan for everyone. They are loud and fast and not for every table.
