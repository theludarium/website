The quarterback problem in cooperative games is simple to describe and hard to fix. One player, usually the most experienced or the most confident, starts telling everyone else what to do. The rest of the table nods, moves the pieces they are told to move, and spends the evening as spectators to someone else's game. The team might win. Nobody but the quarterback had much fun.

Designers have spent the last fifteen years attacking this problem from every angle, and the solutions tell you a lot about what makes a cooperative game actually cooperative. Here is why the problem exists, why it is not the loud player's fault, and the design patterns that prevent it.

## What is the quarterback problem?

The quarterback problem, also called the alpha player problem, occurs when a cooperative game gives every player full access to the same information. If everyone can see everything, then in principle one person can compute the best move for the whole team. The person most willing to do that computation out loud becomes the quarterback.

Pandemic is the classic example, and that is not a criticism of Pandemic. It is a well-made game. But every card, every cube, and every player's hand is public. Nothing stops an experienced player from saying "you fly to Cairo, treat twice, then pass me the yellow card." Nothing in the rules even discourages it. The game is a puzzle, and a puzzle with one solver is a solo game with an audience.

The important point is that the quarterback is usually not being rude. They are trying to help the team win, and the game has told them that winning is the goal. The design has created an incentive, and someone is responding to it. If you want different behavior, you have to change the incentive, not the person.

## Why does open information cause the problem?

Three conditions have to be present for a quarterback to emerge. Remove any one and the problem shrinks.

1. **Perfect information.** Everyone can see the whole state of the game, including other players' hands and options.
2. **Unlimited discussion.** Players can talk as much as they want about anything, including each other's turns.
3. **A single shared objective.** There is one win condition and no individual goals, so there is no reason for any player to hold anything back.

Pandemic has all three. So do Forbidden Island, Flash Point, and most of the first generation of modern cooperative games. They are good games, and with the right group they are wonderful. But put one strong personality and three agreeable people at the table, and the structure will do the rest.

## How do designers prevent the quarterback problem?

Each of the three conditions above is a lever. Modern co-op design pulls on them in different combinations.

### Hidden information

The most common fix. If I cannot see your hand, I cannot tell you what to play.

**Hanabi** is the purest example. You hold your cards facing away from yourself, so everyone sees your hand except you. Your teammates can give you limited, rule-bound clues about what you hold. Nobody can quarterback because nobody has the full picture, and the game is about building a shared language under constraint.

**The Crew** does the same with trick-taking. Each player's hand is private, communication is limited to a single token placed on one card per mission, and the team has to win specific tricks in a specific way. The silence is the game. The best moments in The Crew come from the look across the table when someone understands what you meant.

### Limited communication

Short of hiding information, designers can restrict how much players may say.

**Magic Maze** removes talking entirely for most of the game. Players move shared pawns through a mall in real time, each controlling a different direction, and the only way to signal a teammate is to slap a big red pawn in front of them. The quarterback cannot talk, so there is no quarterback. There is just a lot of pointing and laughter.

**Mysterium** restricts one player to communicating only through dream-like picture cards. The ghost knows the answers and cannot say them. Everyone else has to interpret.

### Real-time pressure

If there is no time to deliberate, there is no time to be told what to do.

**Space Alert** and **Escape: The Curse of the Temple** run on a ten-minute soundtrack or a sand timer. Decisions happen too fast for one player to manage everyone. People make their own calls, often badly, and the chaos is the point.

### Individual goals and traitors

Give players a reason not to follow orders.

**Dead of Winter** gives each player a secret personal objective they must complete alongside the group goal, and there may be a traitor at the table. Suddenly a player who says "give me all your medicine" is a player worth watching. The traitor mechanic borrows directly from social deduction, and the reasons it works are the same ones we cover in [Why Social Deduction Games Work](/articles/why-social-deduction-games-work/).

### Asymmetric roles with private decision space

Even with open information, a game can make each player's turn complex enough that nobody else wants to compute it.

**Spirit Island** gives each player a unique spirit with its own power cards, growth options, and board presence. The information is technically public, but the decision space per player is large enough that even a strong player cannot plan four turns at once. The quarterback runs out of attention before they run out of permission.

**Gloomhaven** does something similar with a twist: each player picks two cards face down, and you may describe your plan only in vague terms ("I'm going to hit the thing near the door"). Hidden card selection plus restricted communication, inside a game where every character's deck is different.

## Which approach works best?

It depends on the group and on what you want the evening to feel like.

| Technique | Example games | What it feels like | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hidden hands | Hanabi, The Crew | Quiet, tense, about reading each other | Can frustrate players who want to talk |
| Limited talk | Magic Maze, Mysterium | Funny, physical, frantic | Hard for some groups to not cheat |
| Real time | Space Alert, Escape | Loud, chaotic, memorable | Stressful for some players |
| Secret goals / traitor | Dead of Winter, Battlestar Galactica | Suspicious, dramatic | Long; can feel less cooperative |
| Deep asymmetric roles | Spirit Island, Gloomhaven | Heavy, satisfying, individual | Big teach, long sessions |

For most mixed groups, hidden hands and limited talk are the gentlest fixes. They work with new players, they run short, and they turn down the volume on the loudest voice without anyone feeling singled out.

## Can you fix the quarterback problem at the table without changing games?

Yes, partly. If your group loves Pandemic and one person keeps steering, a few house practices help.

- **The active player decides.** Others may answer questions but may not volunteer advice. This one rule changes Pandemic completely.
- **Rotate the first word.** Whoever goes last in turn order speaks first in group discussion.
- **Put the quarterback on the clock.** Give the experienced player the hardest role and tell them to focus on their own turn.
- **Call it out kindly.** "I want to figure this one out myself" is a complete sentence, and most quarterbacks are relieved to hear it.

These help, but they are patches. A game designed around hidden information does not need them, which is the strongest argument for the designs above.

## What does this mean for designers?

If you are building a cooperative game, decide early what you want the table to sound like. Then design the information structure to produce that sound.

- If you want quiet concentration, hide hands and restrict clues.
- If you want laughter, add real-time pressure or physical signaling.
- If you want suspicion, add a traitor.
- If you want a shared puzzle, go ahead and make information public, but give each player a decision space big enough that nobody can hold the whole thing in their head.

Then playtest with a known quarterback and watch what happens. If the other three players are reaching for the pieces before being told, you have solved it. If they are looking at the quarterback before they move, you have not. ## FAQ

### What is the quarterback problem in board games?

It is when one player in a cooperative game directs everyone else's moves because the game gives all players full information and unlimited discussion. The team may win, but only one person is really playing.

### Which cooperative games avoid the quarterback problem?

Hanabi, The Crew, Magic Maze, Mysterium, Spirit Island, and Gloomhaven all limit it through hidden information, restricted communication, or decision spaces too large for one player to manage.

### Is Pandemic a bad game because of the alpha player problem?

No. Pandemic is a well-designed puzzle that works beautifully with a group of equals. The problem only appears when one player is much more experienced or assertive than the rest, and a simple "active player decides" house rule mostly fixes it.

### How do you stop one player from taking over a co-op game?

Pick games with hidden hands or limited talk, let the active player make the final call, and tell the experienced player to focus on their own turn. Most quarterbacks are glad to step back once asked.
