Somewhere past $1,000, a collection stops being something you build and becomes something you manage. The shelf is full. Every new box displaces an old one, or it goes on the floor. Managing a board game collection at this stage is less about what to buy and more about what to keep, and the people who do it well think like curators rather than collectors.

This is the fifth article in Building Your Library on Purpose, our series from $0 to a lifetime library. If you arrived here from search with a shelf already overflowing, the system below works on any collection. If you are earlier in the journey, the [starter shelf](/articles/starter-shelf-40-to-300/) is where the series begins.

## Why managing a board game collection needs a cap

About 22 percent of players spend more than $1,000 a year on board games. Most of them do not play more than the people spending $300. They own more. Past a certain size, every additional game competes for the same number of game nights, and the plays per game fall. A collection of 60 games and a collection of 25 games get roughly the same number of plays a year; the difference is how many boxes those plays are spread across.

A shelf cap fixes the number of games you own and forces every purchase to answer a harder question than "do I want this?" The question becomes "what does this replace?" Common hobby advice says that with a collection cap, you buy what fits the collection rather than what you merely want. That is the entire shift from collector to curator in one sentence.

Pick a number that matches your shelving and your group. For many people it is 30 to 50 games. The number matters less than the fact that it exists. Our guide to [living with a shelf cap](/articles/one-in-one-out-shelf-cap/) covers how to choose it and what to do the first month it bites.

## One in, one out

The cap is enforced by one rule: for every game that comes in, one goes out. Not eventually. The same week.

This sounds harsh until you try it, and then it becomes the most clarifying habit in the hobby. Before you buy, you have to name the game that leaves. If you cannot name one, you do not want the new game enough. If you can, you have just identified a game that was already not earning its shelf space.

One in one out improves the shelf in two directions at once. The new game is better chosen because it had to beat something. The departing game goes to someone who will play it. The [cost per play](/articles/cost-per-play-explained/) of the whole collection improves, because the games with the worst numbers are the ones that leave.

Some people run a stricter version, one in, two out, for a season until the shelf is at the cap. If you are starting from an overflow, that is the fastest way back.

## Buying used at the curator stage

Once you have a cap and a log, buying used becomes the default rather than the exception. You know exactly which games get played, which means you know exactly which used copies are worth your time, and you are no longer paying full price for the chance that a game will land.

A game with 30 plays on it from a previous owner is a game that holds up. Wear on the box is evidence, not damage. Our full guide to [buying used board games without getting burned](/articles/how-to-buy-used-board-games/) covers component checks and fair pricing, but the curator's habit is simple: for any game over $40, look for a used copy first and buy new only when the used market is empty.

Prices have moved since 2025. US tariffs on goods from China, where most components and especially plastics are made, have raised the cost of new games, and publishers have said the effects would keep showing up through 2026. That means the gap between used and new has widened, and the curator notices.

## Selling and trading the unplayed

One in one out produces a steady trickle of departing games. What you do with them is the other half of curating.

**Sell what has value.** Hobby games in good condition hold a surprising share of their price. A game you paid $60 for and played twice may sell for $35, which funds most of its replacement. Our guide to [trading and selling board games you do not play](/articles/trading-and-selling-games/) covers where and how.

**Trade within your group.** This is the best option and the one most people skip. A game that is dead on your shelf may be alive on a friend's, especially if it is in their lane. A trade keeps the game on the group's [Shared Shelf](/articles/one-copy-rule/) where it still serves you, just from a different house. The [One Copy Rule](/articles/one-copy-rule/) cuts both ways: you do not need to own a game to keep playing it.

**Gift the rest.** Gateway games and fillers that have been replaced make wonderful gifts for someone starting their own shelf. Attach a note about who taught it to you. The game goes on to a new group and your shelf has room.

Whatever you do, do not let the departing games pile up in a closet. A closet is just a [Shelf of Shame](/articles/shelf-of-shame-infinite-cost/) with a door.

## Kickstarter and the curator

Crowdfunding is where curators get into trouble, because it is the one way to buy a game that does not have to displace anything today. The box arrives in a year, or two. One in one out does not fire until it does. By then you have backed three more.

Our full treatment is in [The Kickstarter Question](/articles/kickstarter-question/), but the curator's rule is short: back a game only if you can name, today, the game it will replace when it arrives. Write that name down. If, when the box lands, that game has somehow earned its place again, you have a decision to make. Most of the time it has not, and the swap is clean.

Also apply the role test from the [starter shelf](/articles/starter-shelf-40-to-300/). A crowdfunded game that fills a role your shelf already covers twice is a wish, not a purchase, no matter how good the miniatures are.

## Buy to complete the library, not to have

This is the mindset shift that defines the stage. A collector buys games to have them. A curator buys games to complete something.

Complete can mean several things. Completing a lane, if you have [claimed one](/articles/claim-a-lane/) in your group and there is a gap in it. Completing coverage for a player count your group has grown into, say six. Completing a family's needs as kids age into new weights. Completing a set of games you can teach in five minutes, if you have become the group's teacher.

What it never means is completing a publisher's line, a designer's catalog, or a ranked list. Those collections belong to someone else; your library belongs to your table.

A good test before any purchase past $1,000: can you say, in one sentence, what this game completes? "It is the co-op for six that we did not have." "It is the step between Ticket to Ride and Wingspan for the kids." If the sentence is "it looks great," wait a month.

## The annual review ritual

Once a year, go through the whole shelf with your play log open. Pick a time that is already quiet; many people do it the week after the holidays, when the new gifts have arrived and the shelf is at its most crowded.

For each game, answer three questions:

1. **How many plays this year?** Zero is a flag. Zero two years running is a verdict.
2. **Who asked for it?** A game that one person loves and requests is earning its place even at a low play count. A game nobody requested is not.
3. **What role does it fill, and is anything else filling it better?** Two games in a role, where one gets all the plays, means the other is a candidate to go.

Sort the shelf into keep, trade, sell, and gift. Then act on it within the month, before the resolve wears off. Most people find five to ten games leave in a review, and the collection feels larger afterward, because everything left is something they play.

The review also writes next year's buying plan. The roles that got the most plays are where the next purchases belong. The roles where games left are where you overbought. That is the core library logic from the [$300 to $1,000 stage](/articles/core-library-300-to-1000/), repeated every year for as long as you play.

## What curating is for

None of this is about owning less for its own sake. It is about keeping the shelf honest, so that every game on it is one you would pull down tonight. A curated collection is easier to choose from, easier to teach from, and easier to share, because everyone knows that whatever is on your shelf is good.

The curator stage is also where the long view starts. Some of the games that survive three or four annual reviews will survive twenty. The next article is about those games.

## FAQ

### How many board games is too many?

More than you can play. A useful cap for most households is 30 to 50 games, matched to your shelving and your group. Past that, new games split the same number of game nights across more boxes and the plays per game fall. The clearest sign of too many is a game with zero plays two years running. A shelf cap with a one in, one out rule keeps the number honest.

### What is the one in one out rule for board games?

For every game that enters the collection, one leaves the same week. Before buying, you name the game that goes. If you cannot name one, you do not want the new game enough. The rule keeps the shelf at its cap, makes each purchase compete against what you already own, and sends unplayed games to people who will play them, through sale, trade, or gift.

### Should I sell board games I do not play?

Yes, or trade them. Hobby games in good condition hold a meaningful share of their price, so selling a game with two plays often funds most of a replacement. Trading within your play group is even better: the game stays on the group's shared shelf at a friend's house, where you can still play it. Unplayed games in a closet have an infinite cost per hour.

### How often should you review your board game collection?

Once a year is enough, with the play log open. For each game, note plays this year, who asked for it, and whether another game fills the same role better. Sort into keep, trade, sell, and gift, and act within the month. Most people find five to ten games leave, and the collection feels bigger afterward because everything left gets played.
