Board game weight is a one-to-five rating of how complex a game is, as judged by the people who have played it. The scale comes from BoardGameGeek, where users rate each game's complexity and the site averages the votes. A 1.0 is Candy Land. A 5.0 is a multi-day wargame with a rulebook the size of a phone book. Most games people actually play fall between 1.5 and 3.5, and the difference between a 2.0 and a 3.0 is the difference between a good night and a frustrated one.

This article explains what the weight rating measures and what it does not, what each band feels like at the table, why the numbers drift, and how to use weight to pick a game for the people you are actually playing with.

## Where does the weight number come from?

BoardGameGeek asks users to rate each game's "weight" on a scale of one (light) to five (heavy), and the number you see on a game's page is a community rating built from those votes. The site's [own description of the scale](https://boardgamegeek.com/wiki/page/Weight) calls it a measure of complexity and admits the term is not clearly defined, so voters bring their own sense of what "heavy" means.

That matters. The weight rating is not a measurement. It is a poll of experienced hobbyists, and the poll has the usual quirks: small samples on obscure games, voters who played once, and a hobby-wide tendency to rate games against other hobby games rather than against Monopoly.

Still, it is remarkably useful. Thousands of votes on popular games smooth out the noise, and the numbers line up well with how games feel.

## What does each weight band feel like?

The numbers below are approximate bands, placed from BoardGameGeek community ratings as of October 2026, and specific games' ratings shift over time.

| Weight | Label people use | What it feels like | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0–1.5 | Light | Rules in two minutes; decisions are obvious or fun rather than hard | Codenames, Sushi Go!, Love Letter |
| 1.5–2.0 | Light-medium | Rules in five to ten minutes; a few real choices per turn | Ticket to Ride, Azul, Kingdomino, Carcassonne |
| 2.0–2.5 | Medium-light | Rules in ten to fifteen minutes; strategy matters; a new player can still enjoy the first game | Catan, Wingspan, Splendor, Pandemic |
| 2.5–3.0 | Medium | Rules in twenty minutes; planning two or three turns ahead; the first game is a learning game | 7 Wonders Duel, Everdell, Scythe, Dune: Imperium |
| 3.0–3.5 | Medium-heavy | Rules in thirty minutes or more; many systems interact; lots to track | Terraforming Mars, Agricola, Gloomhaven, Brass: Birmingham |
| 3.5–4.0 | Heavy | Dedicated teach; serious players; first play is an investment | Gaia Project, Food Chain Magnate |
| 4.0–5.0 | Very heavy | Long rulebooks, long sessions, often simulations or wargames | Twilight Imperium, Advanced Squad Leader, Campaign for North Africa |

A 2.5, the number in this article's title, is roughly the center of the hobby. It is a game you can teach to a willing adult in a quarter of an hour, where the first game will be a bit of a muddle and the second game will be good. Catan, Wingspan, and Pandemic all hover around this mark. <!-- AFFILIATE --> It is the weight most game nights for mixed groups should aim at.

## What does weight actually measure?

Mostly rules complexity: how much you need to hold in your head to play legally. But voters fold in several other things, and it helps to separate them.

**Rules load.** How many rules, exceptions, and phases there are. This is the biggest factor.

**Decision depth.** How hard it is to choose well. Chess has light rules and brutal decisions. Voters tend to rate it higher than its rulebook would suggest.

**Bookkeeping.** How much tracking, counting, and upkeep the game demands. A game with simple rules and forty tokens to adjust each round feels heavier than it is.

**Length.** Long games get rated heavier, partly because they usually are and partly because fatigue reads as complexity.

**Iconography.** Games with dense symbols instead of text feel heavier to new players, even if the rules are simple. Race for the Galaxy is the classic case.

What weight does not measure is quality, fun, or how much skill matters. A 1.5 can be a masterpiece. A 4.0 can be a mess. Our article on [luck vs skill](/articles/luck-vs-skill-board-games/) covers the separate question of how much a game rewards playing well.

## Why do the numbers drift?

Weight ratings move, and almost always downward over time for popular games. There are a few reasons.

**The hobby's baseline rises.** A game that felt heavy in 2010 feels medium in 2026 because the average hobbyist has played heavier things since. Catan's weight has drifted down as its players got more experienced.

**Early voters are enthusiasts.** The first people to rate a new game are often the ones who sought it out, and they compare it to the heavy games they already love. Later voters are a broader crowd.

**Familiarity shrinks weight.** Once you know a game, its rules feel simpler. People who have played Terraforming Mars forty times do not experience it as a 3.2.

The practical effect: treat a game's weight as a rough band, not a decimal. The difference between a 2.4 and a 2.6 is noise. The difference between a 2.0 and a 3.0 is real.

## How should you use weight to pick a game?

Weight is one of the most useful numbers on a game's page, but only if you apply it to your table rather than to yourself.

**Rate the group, not the game.** The right weight for a night is roughly the weight of the least experienced player's comfort zone, plus half a point if they are willing. A table of hobbyists can sit down to a 3.5. A table with two newcomers should stay near 2.0, even if the host loves heavy games.

**Account for the teach.** Weight correlates with teaching time. A 3.0 means someone will talk for twenty minutes before anyone takes a turn. If the group is restless or the night is short, that time comes out of play. Our guide to [teaching a game in five minutes](/articles/how-to-teach-a-board-game/) is built for this problem.

**Step up gradually.** Groups that climb the weight ladder half a point at a time keep more people. Jumping from Ticket to Ride to Terraforming Mars loses the person who was enjoying Ticket to Ride.

**Mix within the night.** A common pattern that works: open with a 1.5 while people arrive, play the main game at 2.5, close with another 1.5. Nobody has to think hard at eleven o'clock.

**Trust the number more than the box.** Publishers put ages and times on boxes; they do not put weight. Box times are optimistic and box ages are legal rather than practical. The BGG weight, averaged over hundreds of votes, is more honest than either.

## Is heavier better?

No, and the hobby has mostly gotten over the idea that it is.

There was a period when heavy games carried status and "light" was a mild insult. That has faded. Some of the best-regarded designs of the last decade are under 2.5: The Crew, Azul, Codenames, Cascadia. <!-- AFFILIATE --> What they have in common is a high ratio of decision to rule: little to learn, a lot to think about.

That ratio is a better measure of a good design than weight alone. A game that is heavy because it has many interlocking systems can be wonderful. A game that is heavy because it has many exceptions is just tiring. Weight tells you how much you have to learn. It does not tell you whether the learning pays off.

## FAQ

### What is a good board game weight for beginners?

Roughly 1.5 to 2.3. Ticket to Ride, Azul, Kingdomino, and Carcassonne sit in that band and teach quickly.

### Is weight the same as difficulty?

Not exactly. Weight is about how much there is to learn and track. Difficulty, in the sense of how hard it is to play well, is separate; chess is light and hard.

### What weight is Catan?

[Around 2.3](https://boardmatch.app/en/game/13) on BoardGameGeek at the time of writing, in the medium-light band.

### What is the heaviest popular board game?

Among games with a wide audience, Twilight Imperium Fourth Edition ([around 4.3](https://boardmatch.app/en/game/233078) at the time of writing) is the usual answer. True 5.0s are mostly wargames with small followings.

## The right weight is the one everyone can carry

The weight number exists to answer one question: can the people at this table enjoy this game tonight? Used that way, it is the most considerate statistic in the hobby. Used as a badge, it empties tables. The game is the reason. The people are the point.
