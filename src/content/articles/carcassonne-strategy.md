Carcassonne strategy divides into before you understand farmers and after. Before, the game is a pleasant tile-laying puzzle that the luckiest drawer wins. After, it is a game of territory, timing, and meeple economy, and the same player wins most of the time. This guide is about getting you to the second kind of game.

It covers the base game with the standard rules for farms (the scoring in the current edition, where each completed city touching a farm scores three points for the farm's majority holder). Older editions scored farms differently, and the River and Abbot additions in newer boxes do not change the strategy much. <!-- AFFILIATE -->

## How does Carcassonne scoring work?

A quick summary for shared understanding.

- **Roads:** one point per tile when completed; one per tile at game end if not.
- **Cities:** two points per tile and two per pennant when completed; one per tile and one per pennant at game end.
- **Monasteries:** nine points when surrounded by eight tiles; one per tile present at game end.
- **Farms:** three points per completed city touching the farm, scored only at game end, to the player with the most farmers on that farm.

You have seven meeples. A meeple placed on a road, city, or monastery comes back when that feature is completed. A farmer never comes back. That last rule is the heart of the game.

## When should you place a farmer in Carcassonne?

Later than you think, and more deliberately than most players do.

A farmer is a permanent commitment of one of your seven meeples. Place one on turn three and you play the rest of the game with six. Place two early and you are playing with five, which means you will often draw a tile that starts a good city and have nobody to put on it.

The case for an early farmer is that a farm adjoining the first few cities is likely to grow into the largest farm on the board, and getting there first means opponents must spend two farmers to beat your one. The case against is everything above.

Our rule for improving players:

1. **No farmers in the first quarter of the game.** Build cities and roads, cycle meeples, learn where the big farm is going to be.
2. **One farmer around the midpoint**, on the farm touching the most completed or nearly completed cities, placed on a tile that will be hard to connect to from outside.
3. **A second farmer only to defend or steal.** If an opponent places one farmer on your farm, matching them ties the farm and you both score. Adding a second takes it outright.
4. **In the last five or six tiles, any spare meeple is a farmer.** Meeples left in your supply at game end score nothing. A farmer on a small farm touching one city is three points you would not otherwise have.

The single most common way to lose at Carcassonne is to have three meeples in your hand when the last tile is placed.

## How do you share, steal, and defend cities?

You cannot place a meeple on a feature that already has one. But you can place a meeple on a separate feature and then connect the two, and when they merge, the player with the most meeples on the combined feature scores it. Ties score for everyone tied.

This is how cities are stolen. An opponent starts a large city. You start a small city nearby with one meeple, then draw and place the tile that joins them. Now you each have one meeple on the same city and you both score it in full. If you can add a second meeple before the merge, you take it alone.

Defense is the same thing in reverse. If your big city has an open edge that an opponent could connect to, close the edge, or add a second meeple of your own through a merge, or accept the share and make sure the city completes so you both get the points and your meeple comes back.

A few patterns that recur:

| Situation | Play |
|---|---|
| Opponent has a large open city | Start a one-tile city adjacent, aim to merge |
| Your city is near completion, opponent is trying to join | Complete it now, even for fewer points |
| A city is already tied | Complete it. Shared points are better than a dead meeple |
| A city is impossible to complete (odd edges) | Treat it as end-game points at one per tile, and do not add more meeples |

Shared features are not failures. A shared twelve-point city gives you twelve points and your meeple back. A sole ownership stand-off where neither player completes it gives you six points at game end and ties up a meeple all game.

## How should you use the tile distribution?

The base game has [seventy-two tiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcassonne_(board_game)), and the distribution is printed in most rulebooks and on many player aids. You do not need to memorize it. You need to know a few things.

- **The tile that completes your city may not exist.** A city with three open edges needing a specific three-sided city tile is waiting on one of very few tiles in the bag. A city with one open edge needing a simple city-cap has many tiles that fit.
- **Count the ones that matter.** Monasteries are a small fixed set. The four-way crossroads tiles are rare. If you are hoping for one of those, you can see how many are already out on the table.
- **Open edges are liabilities.** Every open edge of a feature is a shape that must be matched. Fewer open edges means more completing tiles.
- **Place the hard tile on the opponent.** When you draw a tile that fits nowhere useful for you, put it where it creates an impossible edge next to an opponent's feature. A city edge against their road's end, for example, blocks that road forever.

Players who count tiles do not win because they predict draws. They win because they stop waiting for tiles that are not coming and spend their meeples elsewhere.

## Is a monastery worth it in Carcassonne?

Usually, yes, if you place it early and in a spot that will fill in naturally.

A monastery scores nine points when surrounded, and it is often surrounded by other players' tiles, so it is the one feature in the game that other people help you complete. The best placement is in a gap between two developing areas where tiles will be placed anyway.

A monastery placed at the edge of the map late in the game will score three or four points and hold your meeple hostage. Place monasteries early, in the middle, and move on.

## What does a winning game of Carcassonne look like?

Here is the shape, round by round:

- **Early:** Build short roads and small cities. Complete them fast. Keep five or six meeples in circulation. Do not farm.
- **Middle:** Start one large city if you can defend it. Place your first farmer on the farm that touches the most finished cities. Begin stealing into any opponent's big city that has an open side.
- **Late:** Complete what you can. Add farmers to contest the biggest farm. Use awkward tiles to block opponents' open features.
- **Last few tiles:** Every meeple onto the board. Count the farms before placing. Deny the tile the opponent needs if yours has no better use.

This game is one of the best in the world for a mixed table, because a child can play it on the tile-fitting level while a grandparent plays it on the farm level and both have a good time. Carcassonne does not have a loud moment. It has a slow accumulation of small decisions, and a scoring round at the end where the farms turn the game around and someone says "wait, how did you get forty points?" That question is the game. The game is the reason. The people are the point.

## FAQ

### When should you place farmers in Carcassonne?

Not in the opening. Place your first farmer around the midpoint, on the farm touching the most completed cities, and add more only to contest or defend. In the final few tiles, place every spare meeple as a farmer.

### Can you take over a city in Carcassonne?

Yes, by placing a meeple on a separate adjacent city feature and then connecting it to the target city. When the features merge, the player with more meeples claims the whole city, and ties share it.

### How many points is a good Carcassonne score?

In a [2020 European online team championship](https://www.carcassonne.cat/2020/12/11/does-the-starting-player-have-an-advantage-in-carcassonne/) played with the base game, two-player finals averaged a little over ninety points per player, with farms making up a large share.

### Are monasteries good in Carcassonne?

Yes, when placed early in a gap where tiles will naturally be laid. Opponents help you complete them. A late monastery at the map's edge is a wasted meeple.
