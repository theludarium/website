Games of Ludus Latrunculorum have a shape. Right after placement, gaps everywhere turn into captures, and pieces come off fast. Then the board thins out, the survivors cluster into safe groups, and captures get rare. Crist and colleagues saw exactly this when they had computer players test reconstructed rules in their 2024 study: a fast capturing phase, then a long tail where finishing off scattered pieces is hard. Classroom players they cite reported the same thing.

That long tail is why the table rules end with a modern addition. This lesson covers how the game ends, and how to play the end well.

## The three endings

1. **One piece left.** When a player is down to one piece, the game ends.
2. **No legal move.** When a player cannot move on their turn, the game ends.
3. **The 30-turn rule.** When 30 turns in a row pass with no capture, stop. That is 15 moves each, counted from the first move after placement or from the last capture, whichever is later. Any capture starts the count again.

In the first two endings, the player who captured more wins. In the third, the player with more pieces on the board wins. These are the same test: both sides start with 16, so whoever captured more always has more pieces left. Equal is a draw either way. Like Julius Canus, you only ever need to know one thing: are you a piece ahead?

The 30-turn rule is modern. It is adapted from R. C. Bell's 1960 reconstruction, which ended a game after 30 moves without a capture. No Roman source tells us how a stalled game ended.

## Why games stall

Lesson 5 showed that some shapes can never be captured while they hold:

- a **2x2 block** anywhere on the board
- an **edge pair**: two pieces side by side along an edge
- a **corner pair**: a corner piece with a friend beside it

A player can tuck most of their army into these shapes and simply wait. The other side cannot break them. That is not a flaw to argue about at the table. It is the reason the 30-turn rule exists, and once you know it, it becomes part of the strategy.

## When you are ahead

Your goal is quiet. If no capture happens for 30 turns, you win.

- **Build fortresses.** Fold loose pieces into blocks and edge pairs, starting with the ones in danger.
- **Keep two free pieces for waiting moves.** You must move every turn, and moving a fortress piece breaks the fortress. So keep a couple of pieces in safe open squares that can step back and forth. Because of the no-shuffling rule, one piece cannot rock between two squares on consecutive turns, but two pieces taking turns can: move A, then B, then A back, then B back.
- **Avoid contact.** Every piece you push toward the enemy is a chance for them to reset the count with a capture.
- **Watch your own mobility.** If your waiting pieces get boxed in, you may run out of safe moves and have to step out of a fortress. Never let yourself be left with only bad moves.

## When you are behind

You need a capture before the count runs out, and probably more than one.

- **Ignore the fortresses.** You cannot break a 2x2 block or an edge pair. Aim at the pieces your opponent must move: their waiting pieces and anything loose.
- **Hunt in pairs.** A lone piece can never capture anything. Bring two pieces toward each target, one as the anvil and one as the hammer.
- **Use the edges and corners.** A target on an edge can be trapped only along the edge, so you need pieces on both sides of it there. A lone target in a corner falls to two pieces on its neighbouring squares. Cutting off a piece's escape squares is often worth more than chasing it.
- **Take away waiting moves.** Put your pieces next to their free squares. If their only legal moves break a fortress, your chance comes.
- **Watch the count.** With five turns left, a risky attack is better than a certain loss.

## Running out of moves

The no-legal-move ending is rare, but know how it works. Being blocked does not lose the game. It ends it, and then you count captures. So if you are ahead and your opponent is boxed in, the game ends with your win. If you are behind and you manage to trap all their moves, the game ends with your loss. Do not block a player who is behind you.

## Counting turns

A miscount can decide a stalled game. A few simple ways to keep it honest:

- Drop a coin or bead in a cup after every turn, and empty the cup after any capture.
- The player who is ahead counts aloud, and the other player checks.
- On paper, make a tally mark per turn and draw a line under it at each capture.
