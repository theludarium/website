Popular articles about Ludus Latrunculorum often state its rules with total confidence: a king piece, a fixed starting line, an official board. None of that survives the evidence. This lesson walks through what we actually have, what each source can and cannot tell us, and the myths worth retiring. It is the lesson to read before you teach the game to a history class.

## The texts

About a dozen Latin passages mention the game. The most useful are:

- **Varro**, *De Lingua Latina* 10.22: the earliest mention, comparing a table of word forms to the board *in qua latrunculis ludunt*, "on which they play latrunculi." It proves a grid. It does not prove a size.
- **Ovid**, *Ars Amatoria* 2.207 to 208 and 3.357 to 360, and *Tristia* 2.477 to 480: two colours, straight moves, glass pieces, and a piece that dies to a twin enemy.
- **The *Laus Pisonis***, lines 190 to 208: the longest description, with the open board, glass soldiers, binding, and a list of tactics.
- **Seneca**: Julius Canus counting his pieces (*De Tranquillitate Animi* 14.7), the game as a picture of wasted cleverness (*Epistulae* 106.11), and a "bound piece," *alligatus calculus*, on the *tabula latruncularia* (*Epistulae* 117.30).
- **Martial** 7.72 and 14.17 to 18: glass and gem pieces, the twin-enemy capture, and the mysterious *mandrae*.
- **Macrobius**, *Saturnalia* 1.5.11: still a common pastime in the early fifth century.

Notice what is missing: how pieces moved, how many there were, how a game started, and how it ended. That gap is why every full ruleset is a reconstruction.

## The boards

Boards are the hardest evidence. The 2024 survey by Crist and colleagues counts 25 intact quadrangular Roman boards, from 6x6 up to 11x16. Nine are 8x8, the most common size. Several are 6x7, 7x8, or 8x9, and 7x8 and 8x8 are the only intact sizes found in Italy. Larger boards cluster at frontier forts, and the authors suggest the biggest ones probably held other games.

In Britain alone, about 100 boards are catalogued, with 7x8, 8x8, and 9x10 the typical square grids (Penn and Courts 2021). Most boards everywhere are graffiti or secondary carvings. Two terracotta models, one from Athens and one from the Fayum in Egypt, show a 6x7 board.

So "8x8 is the most common surviving size" is true. "8x8 is the official size" is not.

## The counters

Typical Roman game counters are small glass discs, flat underneath and domed on top, often in very dark glass and white. A London burial held a set of 13 black and 10 white counters with four dice. Bone and pottery counters are common at forts.

One caution: many glass "counters" were probably decoration. A 2016 study of glass counters from Pompeii concluded most were unlikely to be game pieces. A counter proves a game only when it is found with a board or dice.

## Myth: the dux, the Roman king piece

No ancient source names a special piece. The *dux*, "leader," was introduced by R. C. Bell in his 1960 book *Board and Table Games from Many Civilizations*. The nearest Latin is *te duce* in the *Laus Pisonis* (195), which means "with you as leader," addressed to Piso himself, not a piece on the board. Bell's dux is a fine modern variant, and Level 3 teaches it. It is not Roman.

## Myth: the Stanway board was a latrunculi game

A grave at Stanway, near Colchester, from around the AD 40s or 50s, held a gaming board with 26 glass counters, 13 white and 13 blue, laid out as if mid-game. It is the only famous find with pieces apparently in place. But Ulrich Schädler's study argued that the counter spacing implies a 9x13 or 8x12 grid, matching no known Roman game, and argued it was a Celtic game instead. Crist and colleagues agree. It is a wonderful find, and probably not latrunculi.

## Myth: Isidore gives us the piece types

Isidore of Seville, in the seventh century, describes pieces that move in order (*ordinarii*), pieces that wander (*vagi*), and pieces that cannot move (*inciti*) (*Etymologiae* 18.67). Some reconstructions build on these terms. But the chapter sits in Isidore's section on dice board games, and the scholar R. G. Austin argued in 1934 that it describes a dice game, not latrunculi. Treat these terms as possible, not certain.

## Smaller myths

- **Quintilian and Petronius.** Both are sometimes cited. Quintilian's player replays a game of *duodecim scripta*, a different game. Petronius describes a board played with dice, which points to a race game.
- **"Ad incitas."** The Roman proverb for being brought to a standstill may come from a board game, but nobody has proven it comes from this one.
- **The chess connection.** Medieval Latin used *ludus latrunculorum* for chess, so older translations sometimes say "chess." Austin found no connection between the games.
- **What a *mandra* was.** Unknown. Pen, barrier, line of pieces, and camp wall have all been suggested.
