Welcome to Level 3, the top of the ladder. Here the table rules become a starting point. Each variant below swaps or adds one rule, and every one of them is modern. What differs is how much evidence stands behind each guess. Try them one at a time, so you can feel what each change does.

## Slide moves

**The rule:** a piece moves any number of empty squares in a straight line, forward, back, left, or right, like a rook in chess. It still cannot move diagonally or jump. Captures work as usual, closed by the piece that moved where it lands. The no-shuffling rule still applies: a piece cannot return to the square it just left on your next turn.

**The case for it:** Ovid's soldier moves *recto limite*, "on a straight path" (*Tristia* 2.477), which fits a long straight move at least as well as a single step. Crist and colleagues tested both movement styles on every known Roman board size with computer players in 2024. Slide games finished far more often, while step games tended to stall, especially on larger boards. They concluded a slide move fits the evidence better. Several other modern reconstructions, including Kowalski's and the Museum Quintana's, use rook moves too.

**What changes:** everything speeds up. A hammer can arrive from across the board, so a gap that looked safe in the step game can be closed in one move. Lines of sight matter as much as neighbouring squares. The poets' "reserve from far back" becomes literal: one open row or column can bring a piece from the far side of the board straight into the fight. Check every straight line that leads to a square beside your pieces.

## The bound capture

**The rule:** when you sandwich an enemy piece, it is not removed at once. It is **bound**. Turn the square token 45 degrees so it sits as a diamond (with round counters, flip it over). At the start of your next turn, remove it if both of the pieces that bound it are still free. If your opponent binds or captures one of those captors in between, the piece is freed and turned back.

In this course's version, a bound piece cannot move, but it still counts as one of its owner's pieces for trapping enemies. That last choice is ours, and it differs from the Locus Ludi rules, where a bound piece cannot help trap anything. Agree on it before you play.

**The case for it:** Seneca writes of a *calculus alligatus*, a "bound piece," and a player studying how it gets free (*Epistulae* 117.30). The *Laus Pisonis* has white and black binding each other, *alliget* (194), and a piece that, looking bound itself, binds two (201 to 202). Ulrich Schädler built his 1994 reconstruction around this two-step capture, and the Locus Ludi "Seneca variant" uses it.

**What changes:** every capture becomes a one-turn crisis. The bound player gets one move to rescue the piece, usually by trapping one of its captors. A bound piece that still counts for trapping can be part of the rescue, which may be what the poem's "bound, it binds two" describes. Reset the 30-turn count whenever a bound piece is removed.

## The 7x8 board

**The rule:** the same rules, on a board of 7 by 8 squares. The free print-and-play kit includes one as a bonus board.

**The case for it:** 7x8 and 8x8 are the only intact board sizes found in Italy, and 7x8 is one of the typical sizes in Roman Britain (Crist et al. 2024; Penn and Courts 2021).

**What changes:** 32 pieces now fill 32 of 56 squares, leaving 24 empty instead of 32. Placement gets tense, because most pieces end up next to an enemy, and the first moves after placement are a scramble.

## More pieces

The Locus Ludi rules use 20 pieces a side, and Schädler allows anywhere from 16 to 24. With 20 each on an 8x8 board, only 24 squares stay empty. Try it once you are comfortable with 16.

## Bell's dux

**The rule:** each side adds one special piece, the *dux* ("leader"), marked so both players can tell it apart. In summaries of R. C. Bell's 1960 rules, each player adds the dux after all the other pieces are down. The dux moves like the others and may also jump over an adjacent enemy piece to the empty square beyond, without capturing it. The dux can be captured like any other piece.

**The case for it:** none from Rome. No ancient source names a special piece. The nearest phrase, *te duce* in the *Laus Pisonis* (195), means "with you as leader" and is addressed to Piso. Bell invented the dux in 1960, and later rulesets, including Kowalski's and a museum handout built on it, kept a dux of their own.

**What changes:** you get a piece worth protecting and a target worth chasing. It is a fine modern game. Teach it as Bell's invention, never as the Roman rule.
