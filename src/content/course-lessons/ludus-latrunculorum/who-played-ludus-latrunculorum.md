A game lives through the people who play it. For latrunculi, those people left two kinds of traces: boards cut into stone, and lines in Latin books. Put together, they show a game that crossed the whole Roman world and every rank in it. This lesson meets the players. Every story here comes from an ancient text or a dig, with the source at the end.

## The soldiers

The clearest pattern in the archaeology is military. A 2024 survey of the known Roman boards by Crist and colleagues found them at forts in Britain, Spain, and Egypt, including Chesters in Britain, Monte de Santa Tegra in Spain, and Abu Sha'ar on the Red Sea coast, where a late Roman fort held about twenty boards, some in what looks like a gaming room.

The fort at Vindolanda has 16 boards, the largest collection in Roman Britain. They turned up inside the fort, near the commanding officer's house and in the barracks, and outside near the baths. The Vindolanda Trust calls latrunculi the most popular game in Roman Britain. English Heritage holds 11 fragmentary boards and 689 counters from Hadrian's Wall sites.

It fits the game's own words. The pieces are *miles*, "soldier," and *latro*, "robber" or "mercenary," and the military language in every text matches what the forts show.

## The city

Soldiers were not the only players. Boards are scratched into the Basilica Iulia in the Roman Forum and into the Parthenon in Athens. Others turn up in leisure spaces, such as the Serapeum at Hadrian's Villa and the Antonine Baths at Carthage. Most surviving boards are graffiti cut into stone that was built for something else. Wooden boards have not survived.

## Ovid: a game for lovers

The poet Ovid treats the game as part of social life for men and women alike. In the *Ars Amatoria*, he tells a young man that if his beloved plays, he should let his soldier die to her glass enemy, *vitreo ... ab hoste* (2.207 to 208). In the next book, he tells women to learn "the battles of robbers," *latronum proelia*, and describes a piece dying to a twin enemy (3.357 to 360). In the *Tristia*, he even mentions books written to teach games like this one (2.471 to 482). Game manuals existed. None of them survive.

## Piso: a gentleman's exercise

The anonymous poem *Laus Pisonis*, "In Praise of Piso," probably written under Claudius or Nero, flatters a nobleman usually identified as the Piso of the conspiracy of AD 65. Among his talents, right after weapons drill and ball games, is skill at the board. The poem's passage on the game (190 to 208) is the longest description we have: glass soldiers, pieces that bind each other, retreats and ambushes, and a winner whose hands rattle with captured pieces.

## Martial: the gift board

The poet Martial wrote short verses to go with presents. One is for a two-sided board, with a dice game on one face and on the other a piece of a different colour dying to a twin enemy (14.17). Another goes with a set of counters: "this gem piece," *gemmeus*, "will be your soldier and your enemy" (14.18). A game board was a good gift.

## Julius Canus: one piece ahead

The best story comes from Seneca. Julius Canus had been condemned to death by the emperor Caligula. When the centurion arrived to lead him away, Canus was playing latrunculi, *ludebat latrunculis*. He counted his pieces, *numeravit calculos*, told his partner not to claim a win after his death, and said to the centurion that he would be witness that Canus was one piece ahead (Seneca, *De Tranquillitate Animi* 14.7). Seneca tells it as a picture of calm in the face of death. It also hints that being ahead in pieces was how a player kept score.

## The banquet emperor

The *Historia Augusta*, a collection of imperial lives from the late fourth century, tells of a man named Proculus who came out *imperator*, "commander," ten times in a banquet game of latrunculi, and was jokingly hailed "Augustus" (*Quadrigae Tyrannorum* 13.2). The *Historia Augusta* is famously unreliable, so treat this as an anecdote, perhaps invented. It is where this course's top level name, Imperator, comes from, and it is the only source for the modern claim that winners were called imperator.

## Still playing at the end

The last Roman mention comes from Macrobius in the early fifth century. A speaker in his dialogue grumbles that most people waste the holiday on *abaco et latrunculis*, "the abacus and latrunculi" (*Saturnalia* 1.5.11). It was still a common pastime.
