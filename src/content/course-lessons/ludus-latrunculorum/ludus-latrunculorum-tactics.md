Welcome to Level 2. Two Roman poems describe the game in action. The *Laus Pisonis* (lines 197 to 208) praises Piso's play move by move, and Ovid's *Tristia* (2.477 to 480) lists what a game manual would teach. Neither gives rules, and both are poetry, so we cannot know how literally to read them. What we can do is take each tactic they name and ask what it looks like under our table rules. The poems supply the ideas. The board analysis is modern, and you can test every line of it.

Diagrams use L for light, D for dark, and a dot for an empty square. Light is the side we are playing.

## The feigned retreat

The *Laus Pisonis* describes a piece that flees and then seizes its pursuer (197 to 198).

Here is how that works under our rules. Your piece is threatened, so you step it back, toward a friend. If the enemy follows, it has to step onto a square next to your pieces, and a square next to two of your pieces in a line is where a trap waits.

```
. L . L .
. . . . .
```

Suppose a dark piece chases into the gap between these two light pieces. It is safe on arrival (safe entry). But now it has light pieces on both sides, and only the squares above and below to escape through. Put a light piece on one of those squares, and a second light piece arriving on the other captures it. Or step one flanking piece aside and later bring a light piece back onto its square. The retreat drew the chaser into your territory, where you have more anvils than it has friends.

## The reserve from far back

The poem's next piece comes up from far behind, where it stood on watch (198 to 199).

Every capture needs two pieces. So the side that can bring a second piece to a fight first usually wins it. Keep a few pieces back from the front line with open squares in front of them. When a fight starts, count how many moves each side needs to bring a hammer to the key square. The reserve that arrives one move sooner decides it.

## The bait

Another piece dares to join the fight and tricks an enemy coming for prey (199 to 200).

Under our rules, bait works like this: you leave a piece where the enemy can capture it, but the hammer they must move lands on a square where you can trap it next turn. You lose one piece and take one back. The score is unchanged, so bait only pays when it gains something more: two pieces back for one, or an enemy piece dragged out of a safe block. Count before you offer.

## Looking bound, it binds two

The most striking line describes a piece that accepts a risky delay and, while it looks bound itself, binds two (201 to 202).

Under the Ludarium table rules, the clearest version is safe entry that captures on both sides:

```
L D . D L
```

Step a light piece into the gap. It sits between two dark pieces and looks trapped, but Light is moving, so it is safe. And each dark piece is now between two light pieces. Both are captured. Whenever you see two enemy pieces with one gap between them, check the squares beyond them. If you hold both, the gap is a double capture.

The poem may have meant something else, since the Seneca-style bound capture in Level 3 offers another reading. On our board, this is the move.

## Through the broken line

The poem also has a piece burst through a broken *mandra* into the enemy columns (202 to 204). Nobody knows exactly what a *mandra* was. Suggestions include a pen, a barrier, or a line of pieces, so do not treat any one meaning as fact.

The board lesson still holds. A row of enemy pieces protects whatever stands behind it. Once a gap opens in that row, pieces behind it are exposed from a new side, and one of your pieces slipping through can find anvils waiting.

## Keep the phalanx

The poem's Piso wins whether his phalanx is full or only lightly stripped (205 to 207). Under our rules, winning is counting, so every piece you keep matters as much as every piece you take. Trade only when the trade gains you something.

## Never go alone

Ovid adds the defensive half. The manual teaches how to fight while following and how to call back the piece in front, and warns that a piece fleeing alone is not safe: *nec tuto fugiens incomitatus eat*, "nor may the fleeing piece go safely unescorted" (*Tristia* 2.479 to 480). In the *Ars Amatoria*, a warrior caught *sine compare*, "without its partner," has to fight on alone (3.359).

On our board, a pair cannot be sandwiched along its shared line, and a lone piece in the open can be trapped from two directions. So advance in pairs, retreat toward friends, and when you pull a piece back, make sure it lands beside another.
