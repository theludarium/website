You have reached the last lesson in the course. Ludus Latrunculorum belongs to a family. Games where you capture by trapping a piece between two of yours turn up across the world and across centuries. Some are ancient, and some were written down only in the last few centuries. Each relative is worth knowing for itself, and each one helps explain why scholars reconstruct latrunculi the way they do.

## The Greek ancestor? Polis and petteia

The Greeks played board games with counters they called *pessoi*, and the playing was *petteia*. Plato has a character compare clumsy debaters to weak players who get shut in by experts until they have no move left (*Republic* 487b to c), and he puns on a game called *polis*, "city" (422e to 423a).

The later writer Pollux (*Onomasticon* 9.98) says *polis* used two sets of counters on a board of spaces formed by lines. The board was the "city," each piece a "dog," and the aim was to surround and take enemy pieces. The number of spaces and pieces is unresolved.

That sounds a lot like latrunculi. The Locus Ludi team calls the two games "similar, if not identical." But a direct line from *polis* to the Roman game is plausible, not proven.

## Seega: placement and steps

Seega was recorded in Egypt by E. W. Lane in 1836, and it is the closest documented match to the table rules. It is played on a 5x5, 7x7, or 9x9 board that starts empty. Players place two pieces at a time, leaving the centre square empty. Then pieces step one square forward, back, or sideways, and a piece trapped between two enemies is captured, by the moving player only, so stepping between two enemies is safe. Unlike our rules, a capturing piece may keep moving while it keeps capturing.

If the table rules feel natural to you, that is partly because Seega shows a placement-and-sandwich game working in real life.

## Kharebga: Seega with slides

Kharebga, recorded at El Oued in Algeria by Bellin in 1964, plays like Seega but pieces slide any distance in a straight line. The goal is to capture everything. It is the model behind the slide-move variant in [lesson 10](/academy/ludus-latrunculorum/ludus-latrunculorum-variants/), which Crist and colleagues found fits the Roman evidence well.

## Hasami shogi: Japan's sandwich game

Hasami shogi, as usually described, is played on a 9x9 shogi board with nine pieces each, starting on each player's back row. Pieces move like rooks. A piece is captured by sandwiching, a corner piece falls to enemies on both neighbouring squares, and stepping between two enemies is safe, all much like our rules. One big difference: a whole line of enemy pieces trapped between two of yours is captured at once. That means the pairs and 2x2 blocks that protect you in our game are not safe in hasami shogi.

## Tafl: the king's game

The tafl games of northern Europe, such as Tablut, recorded by Linnaeus in 1732, also use rook moves and sandwich captures. But the sides are unequal: a king and his defenders in the middle of the board against a larger force of attackers. A link from latrunculi to tafl is often claimed. Crist and colleagues point out that no medieval source confirms tafl even used the sandwich capture, so treat the link as a question, not a fact.

## A useful contrast: alquerque

Alquerque captures by jumping over a piece, as checkers does. Put it beside latrunculi and you see how different "capture" can be. If you know checkers, our guide on [how to play checkers](/articles/how-to-play-checkers/) shows that family in full.

## Where to play online

- **Ludii.** The Digital Ludeme Project's game system hosts many latrunculi reconstructions side by side: Schädler's 1994 and 2001 rules, both Locus Ludi variants, Bell's, Kowalski's, Falkener's, and the Museum Quintana's. Find them on the [Ludii Ludus Latrunculorum page](https://ludii.games/details.php?keyword=Ludus+Latrunculorum). The Locus Ludi "Piso variant" is the closest to this course's rules, with 20 pieces a side instead of 16.
- **Locus Ludi.** The project has its own [online versions of ancient games](https://locusludi.ch/play-ancient-online-games/).

Playing a few different reconstructions is the best way to feel how much each guess changes the game.

## Where to play in person

The easiest way is to teach it: the free kit on [the course page](/academy/ludus-latrunculorum/#course-offer) prints a full set in minutes. It is also worth asking a local museum, history club, or board game cafe whether they ever run an ancient-games day. For Indianapolis, join the list at [/lounges/indianapolis/](/lounges/indianapolis/) for first word on local learn-to-play nights as The Ludarium grows.
