Ludus Latrunculorum has very few rules, which makes each one matter. Below are the mistakes that turn up most often in first games, each with the rule as this course plays it. After that comes the part every curious player eventually asks about: which of these rules come from Rome, and which are modern choices.

## Seven common mistakes

**1. Capturing during placement.** Nothing is captured while pieces are being placed, even if a piece lands between two enemies. Captures begin with the first move.

**2. Counting an old sandwich.** A capture happens only when the piece you just moved closes the trap. If placement left one of your pieces sitting between two enemies, it is safe until an enemy move closes a new sandwich around it. The same goes for a piece that stepped safely into a gap. Watch out, though: if one of those enemy pieces steps away and an enemy piece later steps back into that square, the trap closes and the piece is captured.

**3. Thinking safe entry is suicide.** You may move into the gap between two enemy pieces. Only the player who moves captures, so your piece is safe there. Players who forget this skip some of the best moves in the game, including ones that capture on both sides at once (Level 2 covers that).

**4. Capturing two in a row.** The trap holds one piece. If two enemy pieces stand side by side between two of yours, neither is captured. This is why pairs are strong (lesson 5).

**5. Using the board edge as a partner.** The edge of the board is not one of your pieces. A piece on an edge can only be trapped along that edge, by enemies on both sides of it. The single exception is the corner: a piece in a corner is captured by enemies on its two neighbouring squares.

**6. Rocking back and forth.** You may not move a piece straight back to the square it just left on your very next turn. You can move it back one turn later, or move it somewhere else.

**7. Getting the ending wrong.** Blocking every enemy move does not win by itself. When a player cannot move, the game ends and you compare captures, so the blocked player can still win if they captured more. And remember the 30-turn rule: 30 turns in a row with no capture ends the game, with more pieces on the board winning.

## What is ancient, what is a reading, what is modern

Every complete ruleset for this game is a reconstruction, this one included. Here is how its parts sort out, from most certain to least.

### Ancient: the sources say so

- **Two players, two colours.** Ovid calls the soldier *discolor*, "of a different colour" (*Tristia* 2.477), and so does Martial (14.17). The *Laus Pisonis* has white and black binding each other (line 194).
- **Glass soldiers.** Ovid, Martial, and the *Laus Pisonis* all mention glass pieces; the poem's phrase *vitreo milite*, "with glass soldiers" (193), gives this course its title.
- **Straight moves.** Ovid's soldier advances *recto limite*, "on a straight path" (*Tristia* 2.477). No diagonals is the standard reading of that line.
- **A grid board.** Varro compares a table of word forms to "the board on which they play latrunculi" (*De Lingua Latina* 10.22).
- **The sandwich capture.** A piece "dies to a twin enemy," *gemino ... hoste perit*, in Ovid (*Ars Amatoria* 3.358; *Tristia* 2.477 to 480) and Martial (14.17). This is the one rule every scholar accepts.
- **Skill, not luck.** No source mentions dice in this game.
- **Winning by taking pieces.** The *Laus Pisonis* winner ends with both hands full of captured pieces (208), and the story of Julius Canus turns on counting them (Seneca, *De Tranquillitate Animi* 14.7).

### A strong scholarly reading

- **The empty board and placement.** The *Laus Pisonis* speaks of pieces set out "on the open board" (192 to 193). Scholars from R. G. Austin in the 1930s to Schädler and the Locus Ludi team read that as a board that starts empty and fills by placement.
- **All pieces the same.** No text names a unique piece, such as a king or leader.

### Modern choices

- **8x8 and 16 a side.** Roman boards came in many sizes. Eight by eight is the most common surviving size, not an official one. Reconstructions differ on the count: Schädler allows 16 to 24 a side and Locus Ludi uses 20. This course picks 16 because it plays fast and fits a chessboard.
- **One square per move.** No source says how far a piece moved. Level 3 teaches the slide move as an option.
- **The corner capture, multiple captures, and safe entry.** Sensible rules, but not attested.
- **No shuffling.** Taken from the Locus Ludi rules.
- **The ending.** No source says exactly how a game ended. "Most captures wins" follows the poems' emphasis on taking pieces. The 30-turn rule is adapted from R. C. Bell's 1960 reconstruction.

The Locus Ludi rules say it plainly: there is no information about how the pieces moved, so the team chose rules for fluent play. That is the honest position, and the one this course takes.
