Roman players did not buy their boards. They scratched them into stone at forts, into public buildings in Rome and Athens, and onto stone first meant for something else. That makes Ludus Latrunculorum one of the easiest ancient games to recreate. This lesson covers three ways to make a set, then how to run the game for a group.

## The quickest set: print it

The free print-and-play kit from [the course page](/academy/ludus-latrunculorum/#course-offer) has an 8x8 board, a bonus 7x8 board, 16 light and 16 dark pieces with two spares each, and the rules on one page.

- Print every page at 100% or "actual size."
- Print the pieces on card stock, or glue the page to thin card first.
- Cut the pieces into strips, then into squares.
- Glue the board to card, such as the side of a cereal box, so it lies flat.

The pieces are square on purpose. If you play the bound capture from [lesson 10](/academy/ludus-latrunculorum/ludus-latrunculorum-variants/), turn a bound piece 45 degrees so it sits as a diamond. Anyone at the table can see it at a glance.

For more on the format, see our article on [print and play board games](/articles/print-and-play-board-games/).

## A Roman-style board

Surviving Roman boards are grids cut into stone or tile, most of them graffiti or later carvings. No wooden latrunculi boards survive. To make your own:

- **Card or wood.** Draw an 8 by 8 grid with a ruler. Make each square a little wider than your counters, so pieces never touch across a line. Go over the lines with a marker, or score them into soft wood with a nail.
- **Stone or tile.** A flat paving slab or a large tile with a grid in chalk or paint feels closest to the real thing. Most surviving boards are graffiti, so a hand-drawn grid fits the spirit.
- **A second size.** Draw a 7x8 grid on the back for the variant. Both sizes are well attested.

## Roman-style counters

Typical Roman counters were small glass discs, flat underneath and domed on top, often very dark glass and white. Bone and pottery counters were common at forts. Good modern stand-ins:

- **Flat-backed glass gems** from a craft shop, in two clearly different colours. These are the closest match to Roman glass.
- **Air-dry clay discs**, pressed flat on one side and rounded on top, painted in two colours.
- **Coins or buttons**, two kinds, for a set you can make in two minutes.

Make 16 of each colour, plus a couple of spares. The Locus Ludi rules assume domed counters with a flat side, so a trapped piece can be flipped over. If you plan to play the bound capture with round counters, flipping is your marker.

## Running it for a class or group

One board per pair is all you need. A 50-minute class period works like this:

1. **Hook (5 minutes).** Show a photo of a Roman board from a fort, or tell the Julius Canus story from [lesson 6](/academy/ludus-latrunculorum/who-played-ludus-latrunculorum/).
2. **Teach (10 minutes).** Use the script from [lesson 2](/academy/ludus-latrunculorum/how-to-teach-ludus-latrunculorum/) to the whole room at once, with the sandwich shown on one board everyone can see.
3. **Play (25 minutes).** Pairs play one game. Extra students become witnesses who count turns for the 30-turn rule, as Canus asked the centurion to witness his count.
4. **Debrief (10 minutes).** Ask which rules came from Rome and which were modern choices. Lesson 3 has the full list.

A few things help a group session go well:

- **Keep the 30-turn rule.** Classroom players cited by Crist and colleagues found that games slow down once the board thins out. The count keeps every game inside the period.
- **Use the 7x8 board for short sessions.** With fewer empty squares, contact comes sooner.
- **Try a source activity.** Give students one short Latin line with its translation, such as Ovid's piece that "dies to a twin enemy" (*Ars Amatoria* 3.358), and ask them to write the rule they think it means. Then compare their rules with the table rules. It teaches how historians work from thin evidence.
- **Run a small tournament.** For a club or a second session, a round robin or Swiss pairing works well. Our guide to [running a board game tournament](/articles/how-to-run-a-board-game-tournament/) covers formats and tiebreaks.

For a family afternoon, start younger players on the three-a-side warm-up from lesson 2 before the full board.
