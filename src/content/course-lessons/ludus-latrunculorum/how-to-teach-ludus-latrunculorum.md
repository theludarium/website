Ludus Latrunculorum is one of the easiest games in the Academy to teach. Every piece is the same, there are no dice or cards, and the whole capture rule fits in one sentence. The trick is the order. Start with the capture, because it is the reason every other rule exists. This lesson is a host script in the order you would say it. The printable [Ludus Latrunculorum teach sheet](/academy/ludus-latrunculorum/teach-sheet/) covers the same rules on one page.

For general teaching habits that work with any game, see [how to teach a board game](/articles/how-to-teach-a-board-game/).

## Before you start

- **Clear the board.** The game starts empty, so you have nothing to set up but two piles of 16.
- **Give your learner Light.** Light places first and moves first, which is one less thing to think about.
- **Have the rules handy.** The teach sheet, or the free printed rules page from [the course page](/academy/ludus-latrunculorum/#course-offer).

## The script, in order

### 1. The hook (30 seconds)

"This is a game Roman soldiers played. They scratched the boards into stone at their forts, and some of those boards are still there. Nobody wrote down the full rules, so what we play is a careful reconstruction. The one rule everyone agrees on is the capture. Let me show you."

### 2. The sandwich (two minutes)

Put one of your pieces on an empty square. Put one of theirs next to it. Hand them a second piece and ask them to trap yours. Let them find it: their piece on the far side, in a straight line.

Then say the four details while the pieces are still there:

- "It works along a row or a column. Never on a diagonal."
- "It only captures when you move a piece in to close it."
- "Only one piece at a time. Two of mine in a row between yours are safe."
- "If you step into the gap between two of mine, you are safe. Only the player who moves captures."

Show the corner last: a piece in a corner falls to two enemies on its two neighbouring squares.

### 3. Moving (one minute)

"On your turn, move one piece one square: forward, back, left, or right. No diagonals, no jumping. One limit: you cannot move a piece straight back where it came from on your next turn."

Demonstrate with one piece. Move it out and try to move it straight back, and show that it has to wait.

### 4. Placement (one minute)

"The board starts empty. We take turns placing one piece anywhere until all 32 are down. Nobody captures during placing, so you can put a piece anywhere. But the moment placing ends, I can move, so think about what I could trap on my first move."

### 5. The ending (one minute)

"The game ends when someone is down to one piece or cannot move. Whoever captured more wins. If 30 turns go by with no capture, we stop, and whoever has more pieces left wins."

Then start placing. Five or six minutes have passed.

## While you play

- **Narrate your own threats.** "If I move here next turn, your piece is trapped." It teaches them to look one move ahead, which is the whole game.
- **Point out their danger in the first game.** Before they finish a move, say "check the squares next to that piece." Stop doing it in the second game.
- **Count turns out loud near the end.** The 30-turn count is easy to forget. A pile of coins, one per turn, works well.
- **Keep captures in two piles.** Each player keeps the pieces they captured beside them, and you count them together at the end.

## A warm-up for kids: three-a-side

Younger players can start with a mini game. Use a 4x4 area of the board (mark it with string or sticky notes) and three pieces each. Place them in turns, then play by the full capture rules until one side has one piece left. It takes two or three minutes and teaches the sandwich, the corner, and safe entry without the long placement phase. Two warm-ups, then the full board.

## Common teaching mistakes

- **Explaining placement first.** Without the capture, placement is just putting pieces down. With it, every placement is a choice.
- **Skipping safe entry.** New players often think stepping between two enemies is suicide, and they avoid good moves because of it.
- **Forgetting the one-piece limit.** If you do not say it, someone will try to capture two in a row and the argument costs you five minutes.
- **Treating the history as a lecture.** One sentence about the soldiers is enough at the start. Save the poets and the evidence for after the game, when the learner asks. They usually do.
