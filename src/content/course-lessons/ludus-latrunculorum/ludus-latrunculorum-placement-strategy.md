Welcome to Level 1. In most board games someone else decides the starting position. In Ludus Latrunculorum you build it yourself, one piece at a time, while your opponent builds theirs around it. By the end, 32 pieces fill half the board. That makes placement the opening of the game, and the place where many games are won or lost before anyone moves.

Placing on an empty board is the strong scholarly reading of the *Laus Pisonis*, whose pieces are set out *tabula ... aperta*, "on the open board" (192 to 193). The strategy below is not Roman advice. It is modern reasoning from the table rules, and you can check every line of it on a board.

## The one fact that shapes placement

Nothing is captured while placing. Then Light makes the first move.

So placement is a quiet build-up to a sudden start. Every gap you leave next to your pieces is harmless until the last piece goes down, and then it is a target. Dark places the 32nd piece and gets the last word on the position. Light moves first and gets the first chance to capture. Both sides have something to watch for at the end.

## Read a one-move threat

Learn to spot this pattern. In the small diagrams below, L is a light piece, D is dark, and a dot is an empty square.

```
. D L .
. . . D
```

The light piece has a dark piece on its left. The square on its right is empty, and a dark piece sits just below that square. If Dark steps up, the light piece is caught between two dark pieces and captured. That is a **one-move threat**: an enemy beside your piece, an empty square on the other side, and an enemy piece one step from that square.

The simplest fix is to fill the gap yourself:

```
. D L L
. . . D
```

Now the two light pieces form a pair along that row, and neither can be trapped along it. Check the new piece's column too: it has a dark piece below it, so make sure no dark piece can step onto the square above it.

## Build safe shapes early

Because a capture needs one enemy piece on each side of yours, some shapes are hard or impossible to attack:

- **A pair.** Two of your pieces side by side cannot be sandwiched along their shared line. Each can still be trapped across it.
- **A 2x2 block.** Every piece in the block has a friend in its row and a friend in its column, so no sandwich can form. While the block holds, it cannot be captured at all.
- **The edge.** A piece on the edge can be trapped only along the edge, never across it, because there is no square beyond. That halves the ways to attack it.
- **The corner, with care.** A lone corner piece has only two neighbouring squares, and enemy pieces on both capture it. But put one of your pieces beside it on the edge, and the pair can no longer be captured at all: the corner piece needs both neighbours to be enemies, and its partner can only be trapped along the edge, where the corner piece already blocks.

## Do not overbuild

Safe shapes have a cost. A 2x2 block cannot move without breaking itself open, and a piece buried in your own group may have no square to go to. Remember that a player who cannot move ends the game. More often, an overbuilt position simply has nothing to attack with, and you need two pieces working together for every capture.

A good balance in early placements: build one or two solid groups, and keep several pieces loose enough to move toward the action once play begins.

## Placements that threaten

Placement is not only defence. Look for enemy pieces that sit next to one of yours with an empty square beyond them. A piece you place one step from that empty square is a threat for the first moves of play.

The strongest late placement creates **two threats at once**. Your opponent can usually answer only one with their next placement or move, so the other may fall. Light can aim for this with the 31st placement. Dark, placing last, can do the same with the 32nd, knowing Light gets one move to respond before Dark can strike.

## A placement routine

1. **First four or five pieces:** start a solid group, often near an edge or around a pair.
2. **Middle placements:** answer your opponent's shapes. Put pieces next to their loose pieces, where a second piece of yours could later close a trap.
3. **Last four placements:** stop building. Scan every one of your pieces for one-move threats and close the gaps, most dangerous first.
4. **Final placement:** if you have no gaps to close, set up a threat of your own.
