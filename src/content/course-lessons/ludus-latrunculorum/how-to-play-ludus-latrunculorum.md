Roman soldiers scratched game boards into the stone of their forts. Others cut them into public buildings, from the Basilica Iulia in the Roman Forum to the Parthenon in Athens. Poets wrote about the game, and one condemned man was calmly playing it when the centurion came to lead him away. The Romans called it *latrunculi*, "little robbers" or "mercenaries," and today it usually goes by the longer name Ludus Latrunculorum, "the game of the little robbers."

This lesson teaches the game the way this course plays it, the Ludarium table rules. You can learn it in ten minutes and play a game in about thirty. All you need is a board of 8 by 8 squares and 16 pieces for each player in two colours. Print the free board and pieces from [the course page](/academy/ludus-latrunculorum/#course-offer), or use any chessboard with checkers, coins, or buttons.

## First, a word about the rules

No Roman rulebook survives. What we have are a handful of lines in Roman poems and letters, plus boards found across the empire. They tell us the core of the game for certain. The rest has been rebuilt by scholars. This course plays a reconstruction from the Locus Ludi project, led by Ulrich Schädler at the University of Fribourg, with one modern rule added for the endgame.

Here is what the ancient sources support:

- **Ancient:** a grid board, two colours of pieces (often glass), pieces that move in straight lines, no dice, the capture of a piece caught between two enemies, and winning by taking pieces.
- **A strong scholarly reading:** the board starts empty and the players place their pieces, based on a line in the poem *Laus Pisonis* (192 to 193).
- **Modern choices:** the board size for "the" game, 16 pieces a side, one square per move, the corner capture, safe entry, the no-shuffling rule, and how the game ends.

Lesson 3 sorts all of this in detail. For now, the game.

## The goal

Capture more of your opponent's pieces than they capture of yours. Because both sides start with 16, that is the same as ending with more pieces on the board.

## Setup: the placement phase

![An empty 8x8 board, with a side panel showing the placement order and three capture diagrams](/academy/ludus-latrunculorum/setup.svg)

1. **The board starts empty.** Each player takes 16 pieces of one colour.
2. **Choose who is Light** by any fair method: a coin toss, or a hidden piece in one hand.
3. **Light places first.** Put one piece on any empty square.
4. **Take turns** placing one piece at a time, anywhere you like, until all 32 pieces are down.
5. **Nothing is captured while placing,** even if a piece ends up between two enemies.

Half the board is now full and half is empty. Turns keep alternating, so Light makes the first move.

## On your turn: move one piece

Move one of your pieces **one square** forward, back, left, or right onto an empty square. Never diagonally, and never jump over anything.

One limit: you may not move a piece straight back to the square it just left on your very next turn. This stops two players from rocking the same piece back and forth forever.

## Capturing: the sandwich

If the piece you just moved traps an enemy piece between itself and another of your pieces, in a straight line along a row or column, the enemy piece is captured. Take it off the board and keep it beside you.

- **More than one at once.** If your move closes a sandwich on two sides, or even three, all of those pieces are captured.
- **Corners.** A piece in a corner is captured when enemy pieces stand on both of its neighbouring squares.
- **One piece only.** Two enemy pieces side by side between yours are not captured. The trap holds one piece at a time.
- **Only the mover captures.** You may step into the gap between two enemy pieces. That is safe entry, and your piece is not captured.

Diagonals never count, for moving or for capturing.

## How the game ends

The game ends when a player is down to **one piece**, or when a player **cannot make a legal move** on their turn. Count captures. The player who captured more wins. Equal captures is a draw.

One modern rule keeps games from dragging. If **30 turns in a row** pass with no capture (15 moves each, counted from the end of placement or from the last capture), stop the game. The player with more pieces on the board wins, and equal is a draw. The idea is adapted from the 30-move rule in R. C. Bell's 1960 reconstruction.

## Your first game

Play once with every rule above, and say "sandwich" each time a capture happens. A single double capture can swing a game, so look at every square around the piece you are about to move. Then swap colours and play again. The second game is when the board starts to make sense.
