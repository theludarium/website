Wingspan has a gentle reputation, and most turns are about your own mat. But Stonemaier's own FAQ lists the ways players touch each other: the dice you take and when you reroll, the birds you take from the tray, the ranked goals, and powers that give other players things. At Level 3, those touches start deciding close games.

## What you can see

Nearly everything is public.

- **Every rival's mat:** their birds, rows, eggs, cached food, and tucked cards.
- **Their food:** the rulebook calls food tokens public information.
- **Their hand size:** you cannot see the cards, but you can count them.
- **The goals and their standings,** for every round.

What you cannot see is their hand and their bonus card. Even those leak. A player who keeps playing birds with platform nests, or who passes on a cheap bird to take a predator, is telling you something about their bonus card.

## What each rival needs next

Before your turn, glance at the player who goes after you and ask three questions:

1. **What food are they short of?** Look at their food and guess at their hand from what they just drew.
2. **Which goal are they racing for?** Count their items on the current goal and the next.
3. **Which tray bird fits them?** Their bonus card, their engine row, the goal.

You will not get all three right. But even one right answer lets you spot moves that cost you nothing and cost them something.

## The tray

Taking a face-up bird means the next player cannot. The question is when that is worth doing.

- **Free denial.** If a tray bird is good for you and also clearly good for them, take it over a blind draw. That is the most common and fairest kind.
- **Pure denial.** Taking a bird only to keep it from a rival usually costs you more than it costs them. It is worth it when the swing is large: the bird would win them first place on a goal or the top step of their bonus card.

Remember the tray refills only at the end of each turn, and the whole tray is replaced at the end of each round. A bird you leave there in a player's last turn of a round is gone for everyone.

## The birdfeeder

Dice come out one at a time, and you choose which ones. The [odds lesson](/academy/wingspan/wingspan-odds-and-probability/) showed how rare fish, fruit, and rodents are on a fresh roll. If a rival needs the one fish die and you can use it, take it. If you are about to leave a single die and reset the feeder, notice that a full fresh feeder is a gift to the next player.

## Pink powers: what your actions trigger

In the base game, 12 birds have pink powers that fire on other players' turns. Most trigger on one of these:

- **Another player takes the lay eggs action.** Five base birds lay an egg for their owner when this happens.
- **Another player plays a bird in a certain habitat.** These gain food or tuck a card.
- **Another player's predator succeeds.** Three base birds gain a die from the feeder.

Read each rival's pink powers once, early. When two actions are close in value for you, pick the one that does not feed a rival's power. Do not twist your whole game around it; a pink power fires at most once between its owner's turns.

## Goal races

Count every rival on the current goal before your last two turns of a round. If someone has a lead you cannot catch, second place costs less and pays only 3 fewer points than first. If two rivals are tied for first and you can join the tie or pass them with one move, that is often the best move on the board, because a three-way tie pays very little.

## The two-player duel

At two players, everything above gets simpler and sharper.

- **Goals are head to head.** On the green side, 1st and 2nd are 3 points apart every round, and there is no third player to share the pain. Every goal is a straight swing.
- **The tray and feeder move less.** Only one other player touches them between your turns, so a plan to take a bird or a die next turn often holds.
- **Denial is worth more.** Whatever your one rival loses is your margin. A tray bird that gives them a goal is worth taking more often than at four.
- **Pink powers fire more reliably.** Your rival takes every other turn, so a pink power that triggers on a common action fires almost every round.

## Keep it kind

Reading the table is not about being cutthroat. It is about noticing. Most of the time the best move for you is simply the best move. When two moves are close, the tiebreaker is what they leave for everyone else. Our piece on [kingmaking](/articles/kingmaking-board-games/) has a few thoughts on keeping that friendly at the end of a close game.
