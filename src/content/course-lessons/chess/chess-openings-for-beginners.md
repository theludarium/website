Opening books can run hundreds of pages, and none of that matters yet. At this level, an opening is just the first ten or so moves, and its job is simple: get your pieces into the game and your king to safety before the fighting starts. This lesson gives you three principles, a quick guide to chess notation, and two named openings that put the principles into practice.

## Three principles

**1. Take the center.** The four middle squares, d4, e4, d5, and e5, matter most. A knight in the center reaches up to eight squares. A knight in a corner reaches two. Start with a center pawn, usually e4 or d4 as White.

**2. Develop your pieces.** Bring out knights and bishops early, ideally each one move to a useful square. Knights usually come out before bishops, because their best squares are clear first. A piece still on its starting square is not playing.

**3. Castle early.** The king is safest tucked behind its pawns in a corner. Castling, usually by move ten, also brings a rook toward the center files.

Three habits follow from these:

- **Do not move the same piece twice** in the opening without a good reason, such as a capture or a threat.
- **Do not bring the queen out early.** Smaller pieces can chase her, and each retreat costs you a move.
- **Do not grab pawns** on the edge while your pieces sit at home.

## Reading chess notation

Chess games are written in algebraic notation, the system the FIDE Laws require on a scoresheet. Each move names the piece and the square it moves to.

- **Pieces:** K king, Q queen, R rook, B bishop, N knight. Pawns have no letter.
- **A move:** Nf3 means a knight moves to f3. e4 means a pawn moves to e4.
- **A capture:** add an x. Bxf7 means a bishop captures on f7. A pawn capture names the file it came from: exd5.
- **Castling:** O-O for kingside, O-O-O for queenside.
- **Other marks:** + for check, # for checkmate, and =Q for a pawn promoting to a queen.

Moves are numbered in pairs: 1.e4 e5 means White played e4 and Black answered e5.

## A trap to know first

Two short games every beginner should see once.

- **Fool's mate:** 1.f3 e5 2.g4 Qh4#. White weakened the diagonal to the king, and Black mated in two moves. Lesson: be careful moving the f and g pawns early.
- **Scholar's mate:** 1.e4 e5 2.Bc4 Nc6 3.Qh5 Nf6?? 4.Qxf7#. The queen and bishop both hit f7, a square only the king defends. The fix for Black is easy: 3...g6, and if 4.Qf3, then 4...Nf6 blocks the queen and develops a piece.

The early queen attack wins against beginners who do not know it, and loses time against everyone else. Learn the defense. Do not build your games around the attack.

## First opening for White: the Italian Game

1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4

White takes the center, develops a knight with a threat on e5, and aims the bishop at f7. A calm main line:

3...Bc5 4.c3 Nf6 5.d3 d6 6.O-O O-O

Every principle is there: center pawns, knights and bishops out, both kings castled by move six. From here, White often plays Re1, Nbd2, and later a pawn to d4 to claim more of the center.

## Second opening for White: the London System

1.d4 d5 2.Bf4 Nf6 3.e3 e6 4.Nf3 c5 5.c3

The London is a setup more than a line. White puts pawns on d4, e3, and c3, the bishop on f4, knights on f3 and d2, the other bishop on d3, and castles kingside. It works against most Black replies, so it is a good choice if you want fewer surprises.

## As Black

Mirror the same ideas. Against 1.e4, answer 1...e5 and develop: Nc6, Nf6, a bishop, then castle. Against 1.d4, answer 1...d5 and do the same. You will meet plenty of other first moves. When you do, return to the three principles.

## When to learn more opening theory

Learn more theory when you keep losing in the same opening, not before. Look up that one line, see where you went wrong, and add a move or two to what you know. Most of your games at this stage will be decided by tactics, which is exactly where Level 2 begins.

For the bigger picture of when to think hard and when to move, our article on [analysis paralysis](/articles/analysis-paralysis-board-games/) applies to openings too: in a known position, play the principle and save your thinking time for later.
