Welcome to Level 3. After the opening, many developing players stop knowing what to do. They make moves that look fine but lead nowhere. This lesson is about finding a plan when no tactic is on the board, and the place to start is the pawns.

Pieces come and go in a move. Pawns cannot move backward, so every pawn move leaves a lasting change. That makes the pawn structure the most stable thing on the board and the best guide to a plan.

## Weak squares and outposts

A square is **weak** when no enemy pawn can ever attack it again. If Black's c and e pawns have both advanced to c5 and e5, for example, neither of those pawns can ever attack d5 again, so a White piece placed there is hard to chase away with a pawn.

A weak square that you can occupy and support with your own pawn is called an **outpost**. Knights love outposts: a knight on a protected square in the center or deep in enemy territory, out of reach of pawns, can be stronger than a rook.

**Plan:** find a weak square in the enemy camp, then route a knight to it. **Defense:** think twice before pushing a pawn, and ask which squares it will never guard again.

## Open files and rooks

A **file** with no pawns on it is **open**. A file with only enemy pawns on it is **half-open** for you. Rooks belong on open and half-open files, where they reach deep into the enemy position.

- Put a rook on the open file first, before your opponent does.
- Double your rooks on the file, one behind the other, to control it.
- Aim for the seventh rank (the second rank for Black). A rook there attacks pawns from the side and traps the king on the back rank.

**Plan:** if you can open a file with a pawn trade, decide first whose rooks will get there faster.

## The isolated pawn

A pawn with no friendly pawns on the files next to it is **isolated**. No pawn can defend it, so pieces must, and the square in front of it is a natural outpost for the enemy.

The best-known version is the **isolated queen's pawn**, a lone pawn on the d file. It cuts both ways:

- **With the isolated pawn**, you usually get more space, open files for your rooks, and active pieces. Your plan is to attack, often on the kingside, while pieces are still on the board.
- **Against it**, you want to trade pieces, block the pawn with a piece on the square in front of it, and win it in the endgame, where its weakness matters most.

The same idea holds for other weaknesses. **Doubled pawns**, two of the same color on one file, and **backward pawns**, ones left behind that cannot safely advance, are targets for the opponent and reasons for the owner to stay active.

## Passed pawns

A **passed pawn** has no enemy pawns in front of it or on the files next to it. Nothing but pieces can stop it from promoting. Passed pawns get stronger as pieces come off the board, which is why players say they "must be pushed." When you have one, trade pieces. When your opponent has one, blockade it with a piece, ideally a knight, on the square directly in front of it.

## Piece activity

When you are not sure what to do, improve your worst piece. Ask which of your pieces does the least, then find it a better square.

- **Bishops** want open diagonals. A bishop blocked by its own pawns on the same color is called a **bad bishop**. Put your pawns on the opposite color from your remaining bishop.
- **Knights** want outposts and central squares.
- **Rooks** want open files and the seventh rank.
- **The queen** wants to support an attack, not lead it alone.

## Choosing a plan, step by step

When the opening is over and nothing is hanging:

1. **Check tactics first.** Checks, captures, threats, for both sides.
2. **Read the pawns.** Where are the weak squares, open files, and weak pawns?
3. **Find the side to play on.** Usually, attack where your pawns point and where you have more space.
4. **Improve your worst piece** toward that side.

A plan does not need to be brilliant. A simple plan you carry out move by move beats a string of good-looking moves with no aim.
