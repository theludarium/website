Chess is a game for two players on a board of 64 squares, eight by eight, alternating light and dark. Each player starts with 16 pieces: one king, one queen, two rooks, two bishops, two knights, and eight pawns. The sides are called White and Black, whatever color the pieces really are. This lesson covers the setup and how each piece moves, checked against the FIDE Laws of Chess in force since January 1, 2023.

If you want the whole beginner picture in one long read, including the special moves and draws, our article on [how to play chess](/articles/how-to-play-chess/) puts it all on one page. This course goes one step at a time and then keeps going.

## The goal

You win by **checkmate**: attacking the enemy king so that it has no legal way to escape. The king is never captured. The game simply ends when it is trapped. A player can also resign, and in a timed game a player can lose by running out of time.

## Setting up the board

![The chess starting position with White at the bottom, the light square h1 in White's right corner, and each queen on her own color](/academy/chess/setup.svg)

1. **Light on the right.** Turn the board so each player has a light square in the near right corner. The FIDE Laws state this directly, and it is the step most often missed.
2. **Pawns on the second row.** Each player lines up eight pawns on the row in front of them.
3. **Rooks, knights, bishops.** Fill the back row from the corners inward: rooks in the corners, knights beside them, then bishops.
4. **Queen on her color.** The two middle squares are left. The white queen goes on the light one and the black queen on the dark one. The kings take the last squares, so the queens face each other and the kings face each other.

White always moves first. After that, players alternate, one move each.

## Names for the squares

Each square has a name. The columns, called **files**, are lettered a to h from White's left. The rows, called **ranks**, are numbered 1 to 8 from White's side. So White's queen starts on d1 and Black's king on e8. You will see these names all through this course, and in Level 1 you will learn to write a whole game down with them.

## How the pieces move

Every piece except the knight moves in a straight line and stops when something is in the way. A piece **captures** by moving onto a square held by an enemy piece, which is taken off the board. You can never move onto your own piece.

- **King:** one square in any direction. It can never move to a square where it would be attacked.
- **Queen:** any distance along a rank, file, or diagonal. The most powerful piece.
- **Rook:** any distance along a rank or file.
- **Bishop:** any distance along a diagonal. Each bishop stays on its starting color all game.
- **Knight:** an L shape, two squares one way and one square to the side. It is the only piece that jumps over others, and it always lands on the opposite color.
- **Pawn:** straight forward one square, or two squares on its first move if both squares are empty. It captures one square diagonally forward. It never moves backward, and a piece directly in front of it blocks it.

## Check and checkmate

When your king is attacked, it is **in check**, and your next move must end the attack. There are three ways:

1. **Move** the king to a square that is not attacked.
2. **Block** the line of attack with one of your pieces. This does not work against a knight, which jumps.
3. **Capture** the attacking piece.

You may never make a move that leaves your own king attacked, even when you are not in check. That is why pinned pieces sometimes cannot move, an idea you will use a lot in Level 2. If the king is in check and none of the three escapes works, that is **checkmate**, and the game is over.

Saying "check" out loud is a friendly habit at home. It is not a rule, and in tournaments you do not need to say it.

## The three special moves

Three rules feel strange at first: **castling**, a king and rook move made together; **en passant**, a special pawn capture; and **promotion**, when a pawn reaching the far side becomes a queen, rook, bishop, or knight. They each get careful treatment in lesson 3, [chess rules beginners get wrong](/academy/chess/chess-rules-beginners-get-wrong/), because they are where most home games quietly go off the rules.

## How a game can end

- **Checkmate:** the side that delivers it wins.
- **Resignation:** a player may give up at any time.
- **Time:** in a timed game, running out of time loses, unless the opponent has no way to ever checkmate.
- **A draw:** by agreement, by **stalemate** (the player to move has no legal move and is not in check), or by one of the draw rules in lesson 3.

## Your first game

Play one game with only the six piece moves and check. Add castling, en passant, and promotion once the moves feel natural. Swap colors each game, so both players get the first move. That is enough to start, and it is the same game played in every club and tournament in the world.
