Welcome to Level 2. At this point you know the rules and how to open a game. Now comes the skill that decides most games between developing players: tactics. A tactic is a short, forcing sequence that wins material or delivers mate. Nearly all of them work for the same reason. One move creates two threats, and the opponent can only answer one.

## First, the piece values

To know whether a trade is good, you need a common scale. The traditional values, which you will find in almost every beginner book, are:

| Piece | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Pawn | 1 |
| Knight | 3 |
| Bishop | 3 |
| Rook | 5 |
| Queen | 9 |

The king has no value on this scale, because losing it loses the game. These numbers are a guide, not a law. A well-placed knight can be worth more than a passive rook. But they tell you that winning a rook for a knight, called winning the exchange, is usually good, and that trading your queen for a rook is not.

## The fork

A **fork** is one piece attacking two or more enemy pieces at once. The opponent saves one, and you take the other.

Knights are the classic forking piece, because their L-shaped attack is hard to see coming. A knight that lands on a square checking the king and attacking the queen at the same time is called a royal fork, and it wins the queen for a knight. Pawns fork too: a pawn that steps forward between two enemy pieces attacks both. So do queens, which can hit pieces in eight directions.

**How to find forks:** look for two enemy pieces that are a knight's move apart from the same empty square, or two undefended pieces on lines your queen can reach.

## The pin

A **pin** is an attack on a piece that cannot move without exposing something more valuable behind it. Only bishops, rooks, and queens pin, because pins need a straight line.

- An **absolute pin** is against the king. The pinned piece cannot legally move at all, because that would expose the king to check.
- A **relative pin** is against the queen or another big piece. The pinned piece may move, but it loses material.

A common example: Black's knight on f6 with the queen on d8 behind it. A White bishop on g5 pins the knight to the queen. The knight can move, but then the bishop takes the queen.

**How to use a pin:** once a piece is pinned, attack it again, often with a pawn. It cannot run.

## The skewer

A **skewer** is a pin turned around. The more valuable piece is in front. When it moves out of the way, you capture the piece behind it.

The most common skewer is a check. A rook checks the king along a rank, the king steps aside, and the rook takes the queen or rook that stood behind it. Skewers often appear in endgames, when kings and rooks share open lines.

## The discovered attack

A **discovered attack** happens when one piece moves out of the way and uncovers an attack by a piece behind it. The moving piece makes its own threat at the same time, so the opponent faces two attacks from one move.

If the uncovered attack is a check, it is a **discovered check**, and it is especially strong: the opponent must deal with the check, so the piece that moved can capture almost anything. If both the moving piece and the uncovered piece give check, it is a **double check**, and the only answer is a king move.

**How to find them:** look for your own bishops, rooks, and queen that are blocked by one of your pieces, with an enemy king or queen at the end of the line.

## Defending against tactics

The same patterns warn you of danger. Before every move, check three things:

1. **Checks, captures, and threats** your opponent could play next.
2. **Undefended pieces** of yours. Loose pieces are the first targets of forks.
3. **Pieces lined up** with your king or queen on a rank, file, or diagonal. That is where pins and skewers live.

## A 15-minute daily routine

Tactics improve with frequent short practice more than with rare long sessions.

1. **Ten minutes of puzzles.** Use a free puzzle trainer, such as the ones on [Lichess](https://lichess.org/training) or Chess.com. Solve each one fully in your head before you move a piece.
2. **Name the pattern.** After each puzzle, say which tactic it was: fork, pin, skewer, discovery, or a mix.
3. **Five minutes on misses.** Go back over the puzzles you got wrong, and find the move you did not consider. Usually it was a check or a capture.

Do this most days for a month and you will start to see forks before they happen in your own games.
