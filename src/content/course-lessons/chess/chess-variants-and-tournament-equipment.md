Welcome to Level 4. By now you know the rules, the patterns, and the basic endgames. This lesson steps sideways into two variants that sharpen those skills, then covers the equipment that clubs and tournaments use, with product picks for your own table.

## Chess960

Chess960, also called Fischer Random, uses the normal rules with one change: the back-rank pieces start in a shuffled order. The name counts the possible starting positions. FIDE publishes rules for it as Guidelines II of the Laws of Chess.

**Setup, per those guidelines:**

- Pawns go on the second rank as usual.
- White's other pieces are placed on the first rank at random, with two limits: the king stands somewhere between the two rooks, and the two bishops stand on squares of opposite colors.
- Black mirrors White exactly, piece for piece on the same files.
- The position can be chosen by a computer program or with dice, coins, or cards.

**Castling** is the rule that trips people up. Each player may castle once per game, on either side, under the same conditions as normal chess: king and rook unmoved, no check, and no attacked square on the king's path. After castling, the king and rook land on the same squares as in normal chess. Castling toward the h file puts the king on g1 and the rook on f1. Castling toward the a file puts the king on c1 and the rook on d1. All squares between the king's start and finish, and the rook's start and finish, must be empty except for the king and that rook. Sometimes this means only the rook moves, or only the king.

To castle on a real board without confusion, the FIDE guidelines suggest saying that you are about to castle, then placing the pieces on their final squares.

**Why play it:** from move one, nobody is following memorized lines. You play on the principles from [lesson 4](/academy/chess/chess-openings-for-beginners/): center, development, king safety. It is a sharp test of whether you understand them.

## Bughouse

Bughouse is a team game for four players on two boards. Partners sit side by side and play opposite colors. When you capture a piece, you hand it to your partner, who can, on their turn, drop it onto an empty square of their own board instead of making a normal move. A team wins when either of its players wins.

Bughouse is not part of the FIDE Laws, and house rules vary. Common choices to agree on before you start:

- Whether a pawn can be dropped on the first or last rank (usually not).
- Whether a dropped piece can give check or checkmate at once (usually yes).
- What a promoted pawn becomes when captured (usually a pawn again).
- Whether partners can talk, and how loudly.

It is usually played fast, with a clock on each board. It is loud, social, and a fine way to teach new players to look at every square, because pieces can appear anywhere.

## A tournament-standard set and board

Clubs and tournaments use a **Staunton** style set, the familiar shapes with a crown-topped king and a horse-head knight. FIDE's equipment standards, in force from March 1, 2026, call for a king about 95 millimeters tall, close to 3 3/4 inches, with a base 40 to 50 percent of its height, and board squares 5 to 6 centimeters on a side, about 2 to 2 3/8 inches. Four pawns should fit comfortably on one square.

What to look for:

- **Weighted plastic pieces** with felt bottoms. They do not tip over and they survive years of play.
- **A roll-up vinyl board** with a 20 inch playing area, which gives squares in the standard range.
- **Two extra queens**, for promotions.

A good starting pick is a [WE Games travel tournament chess set](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B003DMIBGM?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) with plastic Staunton pieces, a 3 3/4 inch king, and a 20 inch roll-up board. Wooden sets look beautiful at home, but weighted plastic is the practical choice for a set you carry to a club: sturdy, light, and easy to replace.

## A digital clock

A digital clock lets you play with an increment or a delay, which most tournaments use and lesson 10 explains. Look for one that offers both, with presets for common time controls. The [DGT 3000 digital chess clock](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00X65TVX8?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) is a widely used choice with increment, delay, and many presets. In US events, check the tournament flyer before you go: many ask players to bring their own set, board, and clock.

## A scorebook

Under both the FIDE and US Chess rules, you write down your moves in most slower games. A scorebook keeps all your games in one place for review later, which matters a lot in Level 5. A hardcover [Chess Scorebook 100 Games](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BJN7H81S?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) holds a hundred games with room for long ones, though any plain notation scoresheet pad will do.
