A chess clock has two timers, one for each player. Only one runs at a time: when you finish your move, you press your side, which stops your time and starts your opponent's. In most competitive chess, running out of time loses the game. This lesson explains the time controls you will see, how increment and delay work, and the clock rules from the FIDE Laws of Chess and the US Chess rulebook.

## Classical, rapid, and blitz

The FIDE Laws define the speeds by each player's time for the game, counting 60 moves of any increment:

- **Blitz:** 10 minutes or less per player. Appendix B of the Laws covers it.
- **Rapid:** more than 10 minutes and less than 60 minutes per player. Appendix A covers it.
- **Standard, often called classical:** 60 minutes or more per player.

So a 15 minute game with a 10 second increment counts as 15 plus 10 times 60 seconds, or 25 minutes: rapid.

US Chess sorts games by a similar total, though its lines are drawn a little differently, with its own Regular, Quick, and Blitz ratings and some games rated in two at once. The time control on a tournament flyer tells you which rating it counts toward.

## Increment and delay

Most modern time controls add a few seconds per move, so a player is never forced to lose a won position just because the clock hit zero in a scramble.

- **Increment** (also called Fischer mode): a fixed amount of time is added to your clock on every move. Spend less than the increment and your time grows.
- **Delay:** with a simple delay, common on US clocks, your clock waits a few seconds before it starts counting down each move. A close cousin, the Bronstein delay, instead gives back the time you used, up to those same few seconds. Either way, if you move within the delay you lose no time, but you never gain any either.

US Chess lists a 5 second delay as its standard for most slower games, a 3 second delay for its Quick events, and no delay for standard blitz. Tournaments may use either delay or increment, so read the flyer.

## Reading a time control

Time controls are written in a shorthand that varies a little by country.

- **15+10:** 15 minutes each, plus a 10 second increment per move. The usual online style.
- **90+30:** 90 minutes each, plus 30 seconds per move from move one.
- **G/60 d5:** US style. Game in 60 minutes, sudden death, with a 5 second delay.
- **40/90, SD/30 inc30:** 90 minutes for the first 40 moves, then 30 more minutes for the rest of the game, with a 30 second increment throughout.

**Sudden death** means the last period of the game. When your time runs out in it, you lose, unless the opponent cannot checkmate.

## Clock rules to know

These are from the FIDE Laws, with US Chess differences where they matter.

- **Same hand.** Press the clock with the same hand you moved with. Both rulebooks require it.
- **Do not hover.** FIDE forbids keeping your hand on or over the clock.
- **Complete the move first.** Your move is not finished until you press the clock. Under FIDE rules, pressing the clock after an illegal move gives your opponent two extra minutes, and a second one loses the game. In rapid and blitz the extra time is one minute. US Chess also uses a two minute time bonus as its standard penalty.
- **Who calls the flag.** In US Chess events, only the players may point out a fallen flag, and the director must not. Under FIDE rules, the arbiter can call it.
- **Flag with no mating chances.** Under FIDE rules, if your time runs out but your opponent cannot checkmate you by any series of legal moves, the game is drawn. US Chess lists its own cases, such as an opponent with only a lone king, or only a king and a bishop or knight with no forced win.
- **Draw offers.** Make your move, offer the draw, then press the clock.
- **Keeping score in time trouble.** Under FIDE rules, with less than five minutes left and no increment of 30 seconds or more, you may stop writing moves until the period ends. US Chess has a similar rule.

## Clock etiquette

- **Press, do not slam.** A firm press is enough.
- **Place the clock** where both players can see it. If the director has not set which way clocks face, agree before you start. In US Chess events, unless the director rules otherwise, the player with Black chooses which side of the board the clock goes on.
- **Do not touch your opponent's side** or reach across the board to adjust anything during play.
- **Stop the clock** to call the director, not to think.

## Managing your time

Losing on time with a better position is common and painful. A few habits help:

- **Know your budget.** In a 90+30 game, divide your time roughly by the number of moves you expect, and check the clock every ten moves.
- **Play known positions fast.** Save time in the opening for the moments that matter.
- **Spend time at turning points.** When pawns are about to open the position, or a trade changes the endgame, think hard there.
- **In a scramble, keep it simple.** With seconds left, a safe move beats a brilliant one you cannot calculate.
