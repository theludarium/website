Welcome to Level 5. Plenty of people play chess for life without a rating, and that is a fine way to enjoy it. But a rated tournament is the clearest test of your progress, and a good place to meet other players who love the game. This lesson covers how ratings work and walks you through a first event in the United States, with the rules from the US Chess Official Rules of Chess, 7th edition, and the FIDE Laws of Chess.

## How a rating works

Chess ratings are often called Elo ratings. Every rated player has a number. The difference between two players' numbers predicts how they should score against each other. The FIDE rating regulations publish the table: a player rated 200 points higher is expected to score about 0.76 points per game, roughly three wins out of four.

After each event, your rating moves by how much you beat or missed that expectation. In the FIDE system, the change is a factor called K times the difference between your actual and expected score. New players use a K of 40, so their ratings move quickly. It drops to 20 and later 10 as players settle and get stronger.

Three points follow from this:

- **Beating a higher-rated player** gains more than beating a lower-rated one.
- **Losing to a lower-rated player** costs more.
- **One event is noise.** Judge your progress over months.

## US Chess, FIDE, and online ratings

These are three separate scales. Do not compare numbers across them.

- **US Chess** rates most over-the-board events in the United States. You must be a current US Chess member to play in a rated event. You get a published rating after at least four rated games, and your first 25 games are provisional, so the number moves a lot. US Chess keeps separate Regular, Quick, Blitz, and online ratings.
- **FIDE** is the world chess federation. Its ratings come from FIDE-rated events, which in the US are often the larger tournaments. A first FIDE rating needs at least five games against FIDE-rated opponents, and the lowest published rating is 1400.
- **Online sites** such as Lichess and Chess.com have their own rating pools. They are useful for tracking your own progress on that site, and that is all.

## Finding a club or tournament

- **Clubs:** the [US Chess club search and affiliate directory](https://new.uschess.org/club-search-and-affiliate-directory) searches by distance from your ZIP code, and you can filter for clubs that welcome beginners or run US Chess rated play.
- **Tournaments:** the [US Chess upcoming tournaments list](https://new.uschess.org/upcoming-tournaments) filters by state and by distance from your ZIP code.
- **For a first event,** look for a one-day event with a slow time control, or an "under" section with a rating cap, such as Under 1200. Many club tournaments run one round a week, which is a gentle start.

If you live near Indianapolis, join the [Indianapolis list](/lounges/indianapolis/) for first word on local game nights as The Ludarium grows.

## Before the first round

- **Read the flyer.** It lists the time control, sections, rounds, entry rules, and whether to bring a set, board, and clock. Lesson 9 covers the gear.
- **Arrive early** and check in with the tournament director, the TD. FIDE events call this official the arbiter.
- **Find the pairings.** Most open tournaments use the Swiss system. Nobody is knocked out. Each round you play someone with a similar score, and you play every round.
- **Silence your phone** and put it away as the event allows. Under the FIDE Laws, a phone on your person during play can lose the game unless the event rules say otherwise, and many events follow similar rules.

## At the board

- **Shake hands,** check the setup and the clock settings, and start the clock as the TD directs.
- **Keep score.** Write both players' moves in algebraic notation, as lesson 4 explained. Both rulebooks say to make the move first and then write it, though US Chess lets TDs allow players with paper scoresheets to write first. Keep your scoresheet where your opponent and the TD can see it.
- **Touch-move is real.** Touch a piece deliberately and you must move it if you can. Say "I adjust" before straightening a piece.
- **Offer a draw** only after making your move and before pressing your clock. Your opponent accepts or declines, out loud or by making a move.
- **Claiming a draw by repetition or the 50-move rule:** it must be your move. If the claim needs one more move, write that move on your scoresheet without playing it. Then stop the clock and make the claim to the TD. If the claim is wrong, you must play the move you wrote, and your opponent gets two extra minutes.
- **Anything odd:** an illegal move, a clock problem, a noisy neighbor. Stop the clock and call the TD. Do not argue with your opponent.
- **No help.** Players may not get advice during a game, and spectators may not give it.

## After the game

Both players report the result as the TD directs, often by marking a pairing sheet. Many players also review the game with their opponent afterward, quietly and away from games still in progress. Keep your scoresheet: it is the most useful training material you will ever own, as the last lesson explains.
