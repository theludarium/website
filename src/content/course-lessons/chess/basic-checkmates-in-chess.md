Many developing players win a queen, then spend thirty moves chasing the enemy king around the board, and sometimes stalemate it. The cure is to learn a few mates until they are automatic. This lesson covers four: the back rank mate, which wins games out of nowhere, and three methods for finishing with a big material edge.

## The back rank mate

A castled king often sits behind three unmoved pawns: for Black, a king on g8 with pawns on f7, g7, and h7. Those pawns protect the king, but they also wall it in. If a White rook or queen lands on the eighth rank and nothing can capture it or block, it is mate. For example, with no Black piece covering e8, Re8 is checkmate.

**To use it:** look for an enemy king with no escape square and a back rank with no defender.

**To avoid it:** give your king an escape square, often by moving the h-pawn one square (h3 for White, h6 for Black) when you have a quiet moment. Players call that escape square "luft," from the German word for air. Or keep a rook on your back rank until the danger passes.

## Mating ideas: use the edge

Every basic mate with a big material edge works the same way. You cannot mate a king in the middle of the board with one heavy piece. You push it to the edge, then bring your own king close to take away its last squares. Your king is not just a target here. It is a fighting piece.

## King and queen against king

**The method: the shrinking box.**

1. Place your queen a knight's move away from the enemy king. This boxes it into a rectangle without giving check.
2. Each time the king moves, move the queen to keep that knight's distance, making the box smaller.
3. When the king is trapped on the edge, stop shrinking. Bring your own king toward it.
4. Mate when your king guards the squares in front of the enemy king. For example, with Black's king on h8 and White's king on g6, Qa8 is mate: the queen checks along the rank, and the White king covers g7 and h7.

**The stalemate trap:** with Black's king on a8 and nothing else, a White queen on c7 leaves Black to move with no legal move. That is stalemate, a draw. When the king has only two or three squares left, stop moving the queen and bring up your king. Before every queen move, ask: does the lone king still have a move?

## King and rook against king

The rook cannot cover diagonals, so your king does more of the work.

**The method: the box, then the opposition.**

1. Use the rook to cut the enemy king off along a rank or file, the same box idea as with the queen.
2. Walk your king toward the enemy king. When the rook is attacked, move it to the far side of its line, keeping the box.
3. When the kings stand face to face with one square between them, called the opposition, check with the rook. The enemy king must step back toward the edge.
4. Repeat until it reaches the edge, then mate with the kings facing each other. For example, with Black's king on e8 and White's king on e6, Ra8 is mate.

If the kings are not facing each other when you want to check, make a waiting move with the rook along its line, so the enemy king has to step into the opposition.

This mate takes more moves than the queen mate, and practice pays off. Under the 50-move rule from lesson 3, you have plenty of moves, but only if you know the method.

## Two rooks: the ladder

Two rooks mate without any help from your king.

1. Put one rook on a rank to cut the king off.
2. Check with the other rook on the next rank toward the edge.
3. Leapfrog: the first rook moves to the rank beyond, giving check, and so on, until the king reaches the edge and the last check is mate.

If the enemy king walks toward one of your rooks, move that rook to the far end of its rank, out of reach, and continue. A queen and a rook can do the same ladder.

## Why practice these

These mates come up again and again, and the time to learn them is not during a real game. Each method is a skill your hands should know without thinking. Once they do, every winning position becomes a win, and your thinking time goes into finding the next tactic instead.
