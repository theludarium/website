Welcome to Level 1. Every rule here comes from the FIDE Laws of Chess in force since January 1, 2023, the rules used by clubs and tournaments worldwide. Where US tournament practice differs, the US Chess Official Rules of Chess, 7th edition, is noted. Each mistake below is common in home games, and each one can change a result.

## Castling

Castling is one move by the king and a rook together. The king moves two squares toward the rook, and the rook jumps over to the square the king just crossed. Kingside, White's king goes e1 to g1 and the rook h1 to f1. Queenside, the king goes e1 to c1 and the rook a1 to d1.

**Mistake: castling after the king has moved and come back.** Castling is gone for good once the king has moved, even if it returns to its square. It is gone on one side if that rook has moved.

**Mistake: castling out of, through, or into check.** You cannot castle while in check, the king cannot cross a square an enemy piece attacks, and it cannot land in check. Note that only the king's path matters. On the queenside, the rook may cross an attacked square (b1 for White).

**Mistake: thinking a check takes castling away forever.** Being checked does not cost you the right. Once the check is gone, and if the other conditions hold, you can castle later.

**Mistake: touching the rook first.** Under the FIDE Laws, castling is a king move. Touch the king first, then the rook. If you deliberately touch the rook first, you cannot castle on that side on that move.

## En passant

**Mistake: thinking it is optional forever, or not a rule at all.** When an enemy pawn moves two squares from its starting square and lands right beside your pawn, your pawn may capture it as if it had moved only one square. Your pawn moves diagonally to the square it skipped, and the enemy pawn is removed. You can only do this on the very next move. Wait one move and the chance is gone.

In practice, only a pawn on its fifth rank can do it: White pawns on rank 5, Black pawns on rank 4.

## Promotion

**Mistake: limiting the choice to captured pieces.** A pawn that reaches the last rank must be replaced by a queen, rook, bishop, or knight of the same color. The choice does not depend on what has been captured, so a second queen is fine. It cannot stay a pawn and cannot become a king.

**Mistake: always picking a queen.** A queen is almost always right. Now and then a knight gives a check the queen cannot, or a rook avoids stalemate. Choosing anything but a queen is called underpromotion.

## Stalemate

**Mistake: thinking stalemate is a win.** If the player to move has no legal move and is not in check, the game is drawn on the spot. This catches winning players who box in a lone king without checking it. When you are far ahead, always ask: after my move, does the other side have a legal move?

## Touch-move

**Mistake: picking up a piece, then choosing a different one.** In a game under the FIDE Laws, if you deliberately touch one of your pieces, you must move it if it has a legal move. If you deliberately touch an enemy piece, you must capture it if you can. Once you let go of a piece on a legal square, the move stands.

To straighten a piece, say "I adjust" (or the French "j'adoube") before you touch it. Many families skip touch-move at home. That is fine, but play it whenever you want to practice for a club or tournament.

## Check and the king

**Mistake: capturing the king.** The king is never captured. The game ends at checkmate.

**Mistake: moving a pinned piece.** You may never make a move that exposes your own king to attack, even if it would capture something big.

**Mistake: thinking you must say "check."** The rules do not require it.

## Draws beyond stalemate

**Mistake: thinking repetition means three moves in a row.** The rule is about positions, not moves. If the same position appears for the third time, with the same player to move and the same castling and en passant options, the player to move can claim a draw. The three times need not come in a row.

**Mistake: forgetting the 50-move rule.** If each side has made 50 moves with no pawn move and no capture, the player to move can claim a draw.

**Mistake: playing on when no one can win.** If neither side can checkmate by any series of legal moves, such as king against king, or king and bishop against king, the game is drawn at once. The Laws call this a dead position.

FIDE also ends a game automatically as a draw if the same position appears five times, or after 75 moves each with no pawn move or capture. In US Chess events, a tournament director may declare a draw if the same position repeats five times in a row, or after 75 moves each with no pawn move or capture.

## Running out of time

**Mistake: thinking a flag fall always loses.** Under FIDE rules, you lose on time unless your opponent could not checkmate you by any series of legal moves. Then it is a draw. The US Chess rules handle this with their own list of insufficient material, and lesson 10 covers the clock in full.
