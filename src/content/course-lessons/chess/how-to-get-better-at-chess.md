You have come a long way in this course: the rules, openings, tactics, mates, plans, endgames, the clock, and your first tournament. This last lesson ties it into a practice plan you can keep for months. Chess rewards steady practice more than bursts of study, and the plan below is built to fit a busy week.

## The loop

Every part of the plan feeds one loop:

1. **Play** games slow enough to think.
2. **Review** them, yourself first.
3. **Find** the pattern behind your mistakes.
4. **Practice** that pattern with puzzles or study.

Then play again. If you only play, you repeat your mistakes. If you only study, you never test what you learned.

## Puzzles: a little, most days

Tactics decide most games between developing players, which is why lesson 5 built a daily puzzle habit. Keep it.

- **Ten to fifteen minutes** most days beats an hour once a week.
- **Solve fully** before you move. Name the forcing line to the end, then check.
- **Mix themes.** Puzzle sets sorted by theme, such as forks or back rank mates, are good for a weakness. Mixed sets train you to spot the pattern without a hint, as in a real game.

Both [Lichess](https://lichess.org/training) and Chess.com offer daily puzzles, with Lichess free in full.

## Play slower games

Fast games are fun, and a few blitz games are fine. But a blitz game gives you no time to use what you study. To improve, most of your games should give you time to think.

- **Online:** play rapid, such as 15+10 or longer, rather than three minute blitz.
- **Correspondence:** daily games, with a day or more per move, let you think as long as you like.
- **Over the board:** a weekly club night or a monthly tournament gives you the real thing, with a clock and a scoresheet. See [lesson 11](/academy/chess/chess-ratings-and-first-tournament/) for how to find one.

Free online play is an option on both [Lichess](https://lichess.org/) and [Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/). Neither is required. A friend and a set work too.

## Review your games, engine last

The review is where most improvement happens, and the order matters.

1. **Right after the game**, write down what you were thinking at the key moments: what you feared, what you planned, and when you felt things go wrong.
2. **Go through it yourself**, at the board or in an app with the engine off. Find the move where the game turned.
3. **Talk it over** with your opponent if they are willing. They saw things you did not.
4. **Then check with an engine.** Look for the one or two biggest swings in evaluation, not every small inaccuracy. Ask why the engine's move is better, in words you could explain to a friend. If you cannot, the lesson is not learned yet.

An engine is a powerful tool and a poor teacher. It shows what is best, never why. Your own review supplies the why.

**Keep a mistakes log.** One line per game: the moment it turned and the cause, such as "missed a knight fork," "lost on time," or "traded into a lost pawn ending." After ten games, the log tells you what to study.

## Study endgames each week

Endgames are the best value study at almost every level. They are less tied to memory than openings, and they teach how every piece really works.

- **Review the basics from lessons 6 and 8** until you can play them without thinking: the basic mates, opposition, the rule of the square, Lucena, and Philidor.
- **Add one new position a week.** Play it out against an engine or a friend from both sides.
- **Use a book or course** for structure, so you build on what you know rather than jumping around.

## Openings: only what your games ask for

Learn opening lines only when your games show you need them. If you keep reaching a bad position in one line, look that line up and add a few moves. Otherwise, stick with the principles from lesson 4 and your two first openings.

## A sample week

| Day | Practice |
| --- | --- |
| Most days | 10 to 15 minutes of puzzles |
| Twice a week | One slow game (rapid, club, or daily) |
| After each game | Self-review, then engine, then one line in the log |
| Once a week | 20 to 30 minutes of endgame study |
| Once a month | A club night or tournament, if you can |

Adjust it to your life. The habit matters more than the hours.

## Keep it social

Chess is a two-player game, and it is better with people around it. Find a club, a study partner, or a friend at about your level and review games together.
