This is the last lesson in the course. You know the rules, the card play, the ambitions, the dice, the draft, and the events. What is left is turning knowledge into habits that hold up at a crowded table with a clock running. This plan assumes about two games a week. Adjust the pace to your group.

## How to review a game

Every game in this plan ends with the same five-minute review. Write down:

1. **The turning round.** The round where the Power race was decided, and why.
2. **One card play to change.** A lead, Surpass, Copy, Pivot, or seize you would play differently.
3. **One declaration to change.** An ambition you declared, or should have, and its timing.
4. **One fight to change.** A battle you took or skipped, and the dice you chose.

Patterns in these notes tell you what to drill next. A player who writes "should have seized" three times has found their week.

## Week 1: the card play

Play base games with no leaders or lore, so nothing distracts from the cards. Before every card, say your choice and its cost aloud: "Pivot, one action, saving my 6." Track how often you hold the initiative across the game. If you rarely lead, look at whether you are spending your high cards early.

## Week 2: counting cards

Use the rulebook's split discard piles, which keep cards played face up apart from cards played face down. In each round, note which high cards of each suit have been played. By the last two rounds of a chapter, try to name which suits still hold a 6 or a 7. The point is not perfect memory. It is knowing when your 5 is the highest card left in its suit, which makes it a safe lead.

## Week 3: ambitions and timing

Keep a small table of the five ambitions and who leads each, updated every round, as the [ambitions lesson](/academy/arcs/arcs-ambitions-scoring/) suggests. Then hold yourself to one rule: declare only an ambition where you are first, or a clear second. At each chapter's end, compare the Power you expected with what you scored.

Add a second habit this week: before every resource you spend in your Prelude, check whether it is holding up a declared ambition.

## Week 4: battle choices

Before each battle, name the job and the pool, using the tables in the [battle dice lesson](/academy/arcs/arcs-battle-dice-odds/). Count fresh defending ships for the intercept, and check whether Warlord is declared, since your losses become their Trophies. After the game, list every battle and whether it moved an ambition. Fights that moved nothing are the ones to cut.

## Week 5: leaders and the draft

Switch to Leaders and Lore, as tournaments use. Draft quickly, then after each game write one line on each leader at the table: which ambitions it wanted and how it scored. Rotate so you play a different leader every game. A tournament player should be able to read any leader card and name its plan within a minute. The [leaders and lore lesson](/academy/arcs/arcs-leaders-and-lore/) has the draft habits to practice.

## Week 6: event conditions

Play four-player Leaders and Lore games under the current WBC rules, with a clock. The 2026 event gave each round four hours. Aim to finish in about two hours, the pace of 30 minutes per player that the rulebook gives for experienced tables. If a game runs long, find where the time went in your review. It is usually long Preludes and debates over rules that the rulebook would settle in seconds.

Also practice finishing well when you cannot win. In the heat tiebreak points, second place is worth ten times third, so play for the best finish available right to the last scoring.

## Reading your Rivals

Across all six weeks, keep one eye on the other players. Arcs hides hands, but play reveals them:

- **Pivots show suits.** A player who Pivots twice in Aggression probably holds no Construction.
- **Copies hide weak cards.** A player Copying often is likely saving a strong hand for later.
- **Seizes cost cards.** A player who seized has one fewer turn left this chapter.
- **Resources tell plans.** Relics held rather than spent mean Keeper. Ships massing near your city mean Warlord.

Say your read aloud to yourself before you act on it, and check it against what they play next.

## After the six weeks

Find an event. The [competitive Arcs lesson](/academy/arcs/arcs-tournaments/) covers the WBC event and how to find others. Read the current preview, play two games under its exact rules, and go. The first tournament is mostly about learning how events feel, so set a modest goal, like finishing every heat with a clear plan.

For broader help with long thinking turns at the table, our article on [analysis paralysis](/articles/analysis-paralysis-board-games/) has practical habits that fit Arcs well. And when you want a different challenge from the same designer, the [Root course](/academy/root/) is waiting.
