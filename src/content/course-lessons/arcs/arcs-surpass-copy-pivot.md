Welcome to Level 2. At Level 1 you learned a plan. Now you learn the engine under the plan: the action cards. Arcs borrows from trick-taking games like Hearts or Spades. A suit is led, others follow or break suit, and the highest card in the led suit takes control. But here, every card you play is also your turn's actions.

## What is the initiative worth?

The player with initiative leads, and the leader gets three things nobody else gets:

- **Full actions from any card.** The leader gets every pip on whatever suit they choose.
- **The suit choice.** Followers must match it to Surpass. Anyone without that suit can only Copy or Pivot for one action.
- **The right to declare an ambition.** Only the leader can declare.

So the initiative is not a small edge. It decides who sets the agenda for the whole round.

## Lead

Lead your best card for the job you need done. A card with many pips in the suit you need is the classic lead, because followers will mostly get one action while you get three or four.

Lead a suit your Rivals are short in when you can. If they cannot Surpass, they cannot take the initiative from you without seizing, and the round's actions tilt your way. You will not see their hands, but you can watch what they Pivot with and draw conclusions.

## Surpass

Surpass when your card in the lead suit is higher. You get all your card's pips, and the highest Surpass takes the initiative at round end. Two notes:

- You only need to beat the **lead card**, not other followers. If the lead was a declared 0, any card in that suit Surpasses it.
- Surpassing with a mid card can still lose the initiative to a later player with a higher card. Players later in turn order see more before they choose.

## Copy

Copy plays any card face down for one action from the lead card. Use it when the lead card's actions are what you need, and you want to throw away your weakest card. Copy hides what you played, so it also keeps information from your Rivals. Remember that all played cards go into a face-down discard, so in a normal game nobody can check what was copied later.

## Pivot

Pivot plays a card of another suit for one action on your own card. It is the flexible choice: if the leader played Construction but you need to battle, Pivot an Aggression card. Pivoting shows your card face up, so it tells everyone a little about your hand.

A Pivot or Copy gives one action. With a resource in your Prelude, though, you can stretch a weak turn. A Fuel gives a Move, a Material a Build or Repair, a Relic a Secure. A Psionic takes an action listed on the lead card, which makes it a good partner for Copy and Pivot turns.

## Seize the initiative

Seizing costs you a whole card, which is a whole turn later in the chapter. It is worth it when the next lead is worth more than a turn, for example:

- You need to declare an ambition you are winning before the chapter ends, and you do not otherwise lead.
- A Rival is about to lead and declare an ambition they would win.
- It is late in the chapter and leading next lets you act twice in a row: last this round, first next round.

In four-player games, a 7 that Surpasses seizes for free. That makes 7s strong, and it also means you should expect them when the lead suit has not shown its 7 yet.

## Planning six cards

You get six cards a chapter, so plan the whole hand. A useful frame:

| Role | Good cards |
| --- | --- |
| Leads | Many pips, a suit you hold more of |
| Surpass threats | High numbers, especially 6s and 7s |
| Declare cards | Low numbers whose ambition you can win |
| Throwaways | Your weakest card, saved for a Copy or a seize |

Hands differ, so the frame bends. But a player who knows which card does which job will beat a player who plays the best-looking card each turn.

## Count the deck

The deck holds four suits with one card of each number per suit: 20 cards from 2 to 6, plus eight 1s and 7s with four players. Once a suit's high cards have been seen, a modest card in that suit becomes a safe lead. The rulebook notes that all cards are discarded face down, which rewards memory. Tournament groups can use its split discard option instead, with separate face-up and face-down piles. The [practice plan lesson](/academy/arcs/arcs-practice-plan/) turns this into a counting drill.
