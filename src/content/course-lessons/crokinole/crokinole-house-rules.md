Crokinole was a family game for more than a century before it had a rulebook anyone agreed on. Every family has its own version, and many of those versions are good. The trouble starts when two families meet at one board. This lesson lists the common house rules by name, sets each against the [NCA Official Rules of Crokinole](https://nationalcrokinoleassociation.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/NCA-Official-Rules-of-Crokinole.docx.pdf), and suggests versions for young kids.

Even the board makers agree: [Tracey Boards' FAQ](https://traceyboards.com/faq/) says it is a big believer in house rules, and that competitive play follows the NCA.

## Scoring house rules

**The race to 100.** Only the higher side scores each round, and it scores the difference. First to 100 wins. This is the most common home method. The NCA instead gives 2 points for a round win and 1 for a tie, over four rounds. Lesson 5 shows how the choice changes strategy.

**Play to a different target.** Some groups play to 50 for a short game or 200 for a long night. Fine, as long as you say so first.

**Count 20s at the end.** Some families leave sunk 20s in the hole until the round ends. The NCA takes them out after each turn and keeps them in view (rule 2a ii). Leaving them in changes the board, since a disc in the hole can be bumped or block the next 20.

## Shot house rules

**Fully inside the 15.** On a board with no opposing discs, some groups require a disc to end fully inside the 15 line. Under the NCA rule, touching the line is enough (rule 3a ii). Fully inside is a harder game. Touching is the official one.

**No must-hit.** Some homes drop the requirement to hit an opposing disc. This changes crokinole a great deal: players can simply hide in the 5 ring or shoot for 20 every turn.

**Muskoka rules.** A known variant, usually described as dropping the No Hiding Rule, making contact with opposing discs optional, and giving the opponent one chance to knock a disc out of the 20 hole. <!-- VERIFY: find a primary source for Muskoka rules beyond the Wikipedia summary, and whether shots may start anywhere on the board --> If your group grew up on these, play them, and call them by name.

**Loose posture.** Many homes skip the one cheek rule and let players lean or stand. The NCA requires part of your seat on the chair, all four chair legs on the floor, and nothing but your feet touching the floor (rules 7k to 7m). If anyone at your table might play at a club someday, practice seated.

## Player-count house rules

**Disc counts.** The NCA sets 8 discs each in singles and 6 each in doubles (rules 4a and 5a). Many home sets hold about 12 per color, and plenty of families shoot all 12 in singles. Any count works if both sides shoot the same number.

**Four-player cutthroat.** Each of four players takes their own color and plays alone, rather than in teams. Tracey Boards' FAQ calls this 4-Player Singles and notes that it needs four colors of discs. The NCA has no rules for it, so agree in advance how the must-hit rule works: most groups treat every other color as an opponent.

**Three players.** There is no official three-player crokinole. Groups that try it usually play cutthroat with three colors, or rotate: two play singles and the winner stays on.

## Kids' variants

For young players, the goal is a hit or a 20 on most turns. Each of these is a house rule, so say so out loud. That way the child learns the real rule later without feeling tricked.

- **Shoot from closer.** Let small children start their disc inside the 5 ring, or from the 10 line.
- **Use a cue.** Crokinole cues are small sticks for pushing the disc. The World Crokinole Championship has cue categories, and its FAQ invites young players who want to use a cue into the youth doubles and the junior and intermediate singles.
- **Hits only.** For the youngest, drop the No Hiding Rule but keep the must-hit rule, so there is always something to aim at.
- **Score 20s only.** A simple game of who sinks the most. Good for a first lesson in aim.
- **Fewer discs.** Four each keeps a round short enough for a young attention span.
- **Partner up.** In doubles, partners may talk. Pair each child with an adult who coaches every shot.

## How to choose

A simple test for any house rule: does it make the game more fun for this table, today? If yes, keep it. Then write it down. A house rule only works if everyone plays the same one.
