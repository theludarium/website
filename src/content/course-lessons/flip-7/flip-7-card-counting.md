Most card games reshuffle every hand. Flip 7 does not. The rulebook says to set every card from a round to the side and pass the rest of the deck to the next dealer. Only when the deck runs out do the set-aside cards get shuffled into a new one. It even asks players to "keep the card count in mind."

That rule gives Flip 7 a memory. Lesson 4 used the cards on the table for a single moment; this lesson stretches the idea across rounds.

## How long does a deck last?

It depends on how many people are flipping. In our simulation of tables where players stay around 25 points, each player uses a little under 4 cards a round, action cards included. That gives a rough guide:

| Players | Cards used per round | First reshuffle comes around |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | about 11 | round 9 |
| 4 | about 15 | round 7 |
| 6 | about 22 | round 5 |
| 8 | about 29 | round 4 |
| 10 | about 37 | round 3 |

Bolder tables burn cards faster, careful ones slower. But the pattern holds. At three or four players, one deck covers most of a game, and the set-aside pile grows large enough to matter. At a big party table the deck turns over every few rounds, and counting buys you little. Lesson 10 has more on how player count changes the game.

## Why it matters late in the deck

Say you hold a 12 and an 11, worth 23, and the deck is down to its last 30 cards. In a fresh deck your bust count would be 11 + 10 = 21. Now some of those 21 cards are gone, sitting in the set-aside pile or in other rows.

If you have not counted, your best guess is that the missing cards came out evenly, which leaves about 7 bust cards in those 30, or about 23 percent. But the real number swings. Working out the odds for a random 30-card deck, the bust cards left will be somewhere from 4 to 9 nearly 90 percent of the time. That puts your true bust chance anywhere from about 13 to 30 percent on the same row.

That spread is the whole case for counting. At 13 percent, a 23-point row is a clear hit. At 30 percent, it is a clear stay. Only the count tells you which one you are in.

## What to track

Nobody remembers 94 cards. You do not need to. Track the few cards that move your decisions most.

**The 12s and 11s.** Together they are 23 of the 79 number cards, and they are the ones that bust big rows. If you hold one, knowing how many of its copies are gone changes your bust count more than anything else.

**The three Flip Threes.** Late in a round, a Flip Three is the main danger for anyone still flipping with a long row. If all three have gone by, the threat is off for the rest of the deck.

**The three Second Chances.** If they are all gone, nobody will get rescued, which makes a Flip Three you aim much more dangerous.

**The x2.** Once it has gone by, the biggest catch-up card in the game is out of reach until the reshuffle. That matters most in the endgame from lesson 9.

A simple method: a small tally in the margin of the scoresheet, two rows of marks for 12s and 11s, and three boxes each for Flip Three and Second Chance.

## The reshuffle resets everything

When the deck runs out, the rulebook says to shuffle all the set-aside cards into a new deck. If that happens mid-round, every card in front of players stays where it is, even the cards of busted players. So right after a mid-round reshuffle, the new deck is missing exactly the cards on the table.

Two habits follow:

- **Clear your tally at the reshuffle,** then mark the cards still sitting in front of players as gone.
- **Watch the last few cards of a deck.** When only a handful remain, the pile and the table tell you almost exactly what is left. A player who counts can make near-certain calls in those moments.

## Does counting really pay?

Yes, but modestly, and mostly late in a deck. In our solo simulation, a player using the exact cards left in the deck to make each call averaged about half a point more per round than a player using only their own row. Over a ten-round game that is about 5 points. Small, but Flip 7 races are often decided by less.

Counting also trains your attention: who holds a Second Chance, which rows are long, how close the leader is. Those reads power lessons 6 and 9.

## Keep it light

Counting should never slow the table. If your group plays fast and loud, skip the tally and just glance at the set-aside pile before a big decision. If you play at three or four and want an edge, the tally takes a few seconds per round.
