Flip 7 is a game for three or more. But the rulebook has a line for smaller parties: with two or fewer, how many rounds does it take you to reach 200? It looks like a filler mode, but it is the cleanest way to practice everything in Levels 1 and 2, and with a few benchmarks it becomes a real test.

## How the solo challenge plays

Use the normal rules with one player:

- Deal yourself a card, then hit or stay as usual.
- Every action card goes to you, because you are the only active player. The rulebook says the last active player must use an action card on themself.
- A **Freeze** banks your row and ends the round.
- A **Flip Three** forces three cards on your own row.
- A **Second Chance** is yours to keep. A second one, with no one to give it to, is discarded.
- Set the cards aside after each round, and reshuffle only when the deck runs out, just like a full game.
- Count the rounds until your total reaches 200.

Two players can each run the challenge with their own deck and compare round counts.

## Benchmarks to beat

We simulated thousands of solo runs on the real 94-card deck, with every action card resolved on the solo player. Here is how many rounds it took to reach 200 with different stopping habits:

| Stopping habit | Average rounds | Typical range (middle 80%) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stay at 15 points | about 12.8 | 11 to 15 |
| Stay at 20 points | about 11.6 | 9 to 14 |
| Stay at 25 points | about 11.2 | 8 to 15 |
| Stay at 30 points | about 11.5 | 8 to 16 |
| Stay at 40 points | about 13.6 | 8 to 20 |
| Best one-card calls | about 10.6 | 8 to 14 |

"Best one-card calls" means a player who, before every hit, works out whether one more card adds points on average, using the bust count and the cards in their own row. That is the math from lessons 4, 5, and 8, done perfectly.

So:

- **Par is 11.** A steady player who stays near 25 finishes in 11 rounds or fewer about 60 percent of the time.
- **9 or fewer is a good run.** Even perfect calls get there only about a third of the time.
- **8 or fewer is a great run.** About 1 run in 5 with perfect calls, fewer with a fixed stop.
- **16 or more means something went wrong,** or the deck was cruel. Check your bust log.

Staying anywhere from 20 to 30 lands within about half a round on average. What beats a good stop line is reading each row instead of using any fixed line.

## Why one run tells you little

Look at the ranges. Even with perfect calls, the middle 80 percent of runs spread from 8 rounds to 14. A single run of 13 does not mean you played badly, and a run of 8 does not mean you played well. Luck is loud in Flip 7. Our piece on [luck and skill in board games](/articles/luck-vs-skill-board-games/) explains why that mix keeps a game fresh.

That is why the practice plan below asks for ten runs. Over ten, the average settles down enough to show real progress.

## A ten-run practice plan

**Runs 1 to 3: baseline.** Play the way you normally would. Do not try to improve. Log the rounds per run and every bust: the row, the points lost, and whether you held a modifier.

**Runs 4 to 6: the bust count out loud.** Before every hit, say your bust count and the cards left in the deck. Use lesson 4's rule: if your points times your bust chance is clearly above the 4 to 6 points the next card usually adds, stay.

**Runs 7 and 8: the modifiers and the bonus.** Apply lesson 5: judge on number cards, ignore the x2, let plus cards pull you earlier. Apply lesson 8 at six numbers: count finishers against busters.

**Runs 9 and 10: count the deck.** Keep the tally from lesson 7 and use the real cards left, not the fresh-deck count, especially in the last third of each deck.

Then compare your average for runs 1 to 3 with your average for runs 8 to 10. A drop of a round or more is a real gain.

## Reviewing your busts

The bust log is the most useful part of the practice. For each bust, ask one question: **did the math say stay?**

- **Yes:** that is a habit to fix. Note the row, because most players bust on the same kind of row again and again, usually two or three big numbers.
- **No:** that was the price of a good call. Let it go. A 20 percent risk still happens one time in five.

Over ten runs, most players find that their busts sort into one or two patterns. Fix those and the round count drops.

## Take it to the table

The solo challenge trains your own calls, not the table reads from lesson 6 or the race from lesson 9. Back at a full table, keep your solo habits for your own row and spend your attention on everyone else's.
