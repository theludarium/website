Every experienced Twilight Imperium player has seen it: a home system with two poor planets and an anomaly in front, next to a neighbor with a rich wormhole system. The official setup can produce that. This lesson covers the official rules for building the galaxy, then the community's favorite fix, the slice draft.

## The official way

The Living Rules Reference builds the galaxy in steps (Complete Setup, step 6):

1. **Separate the tiles.** Blue-backed systems have planets. Red-backed systems are anomalies or have no planets. Green-backed tiles are home systems.
2. **Deal tiles to each player** by player count. With six players, each gets three blue and two red.
3. **Place them in turns.** Starting with the speaker and going clockwise, each player places one tile in the first ring around Mecatol Rex. The order then snakes back and forth until every tile is placed.
4. **Fill rings in order.** Each ring must be complete before the next begins.
5. **Keep anomalies apart.** Anomalies cannot be placed next to each other, and neither can two systems with the same wormhole type, unless there is no other choice.

This gives every player some say, but it rewards whoever knows the tiles best, and the dealt tiles are random. New groups often end up with very uneven positions without noticing until round three.

## What is a slice?

With six players, the standard galaxy has 30 systems around Mecatol Rex outside the home systems: 6 in the first ring, 12 in the second, and 12 in the third once the homes take their 6 spaces. That is five systems per player. A slice is one player's five: the systems nearest their home, plus one shared system between them and the next seat.

Drafting slices instead of dealing tiles means each player knows exactly what is in front of them before they choose.

## How a Milty draft works

The Milty draft is a community format, run by free online tools such as the [Milty Draft Generator](https://milty.shenanigans.be), a fan-made site based on the original miltydraft.com. It is not part of the official rules, so agree to use it before game day. Here is how the generator runs it:

- **The pool.** The tool builds a set of slices, each with five systems: three blue-backed and two red-backed. It also deals a pool of factions. Its guide suggests one more slice than players, and three more factions than players.
- **The picks.** Each pick is one of three things: a faction, a slice, or a seat, meaning your position relative to the speaker.
- **The order.** The draft runs three rounds in a snake: random order, then reversed, then the first order again. Everyone ends with one faction, one slice, and one seat.
- **The map.** Each slice goes in front of its owner's seat, and the game begins.

The three-way choice is the clever part. Taking the best slice first means someone else takes the best faction. Taking the speaker seat means a later pick on factions. Every first pick costs you something.

## Reading a slice

Many tools score slices by **optimal value**: each planet counts only its better number, resources or influence, since a planet is usually spent for one or the other. A planet worth 3 resources and 1 influence counts as 3 resources. Compare slices on three things:

- **Total optimal resources and influence.** Resources build fleets and buy technology. Influence buys command tokens and votes.
- **Technology specialties.** You can exhaust a planet with a specialty to ignore one matching prerequisite when you research (90.14).
- **Wormholes and anomalies.** A wormhole gives mobility and exposure at once. An anomaly can be a wall that protects you or a wall that boxes you in.

Then read the slice next to its likely faction. A combat faction may want a slice that reaches Mecatol Rex quickly. A trade faction may want neighbors. A technology faction wants specialties.

## Picking order

There is no single right first pick, and that is the point. Some patterns hold up:

- **If one faction in the pool is much stronger, or fits the group's style,** taking it first is common.
- **If the slices are very uneven,** the best slice can matter more than any faction.
- **Seat order matters for strategy card picks.** The speaker picks first in round one, and players near the end of the order pick from what is left.

## Balance settings

The generator lets you set minimum resources and influence per slice, require a spread of wormholes, and require legendary planets when you play with Prophecy of Kings. For a home group, the default settings are a fine start. Raise the minimums if players complain about weak slices.

## Without a computer

You can run a simple slice draft by hand. Before game day, the host builds slices of three blue and two red tiles, trying to keep their total values close, and lays them out with numbered cards. Factions can be dealt at random, two or three per player. Then draft in a snake as above.

The galaxy you build becomes part of the story of the game. A fair one makes the story about the players.
