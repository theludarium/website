Roxley lists Brass: Birmingham for 2 to 4 players, and it plays well at every count. But it is not the same game at each. Four players fight over a crowded map. Two players share a quiet one. Knowing what changes helps you bring the right plan.

## What setup removes

With fewer than 4 players, every card and merchant tile marked for a higher player count goes back in the box.

- **Location cards.** The banner color of each town shows which cards are in the deck. With 3 players, the teal-banner towns' cards are out. With 2 players, the blue and teal towns' cards are out. You can still build in those towns: you just need a Wild Location, or an industry card and a town already in your network.
- **Merchants.** With 3 players, Nottingham gets no merchant tiles. With 2, neither Nottingham nor Warrington does. Fewer merchants means fewer buyers, fewer barrels of merchant beer, and fewer bonuses.

## Longer eras, more actions

| Players | Rounds per era | Your actions, Canal | Your actions, Rail |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4 | 8 | 15 | 16 |
| 3 | 9 | 17 | 18 |
| 2 | 10 | 19 | 20 |

With 2 players you take 39 actions in a game, against 31 with 4. Those extra actions make longer plans possible. A pottery line that needs two expensive builds before the 20 VP level 5 tile is a stretch in a four-player game. With 20 Rail Era actions, it is a real option.

Income paydays grow too: 17 with 3 players and 19 with 2, against 15 with 4. A loan taken early costs more income at lower player counts, because there are more paydays left to lose. By the same logic, early income tiles earn more.

## The shared economy shrinks

In a four-player game, three rivals use your coal, drain your iron, and drink your beer. With one rival, your resource tiles flip more slowly, because fewer players need them. A coal mine you count on a rival to empty may sit full for a long time.

So at lower counts, plan to flip more of your own tiles yourself. Use your own beer for your own sales. Build iron works when you will Develop soon, so you drain your own iron. And treat a rival's mine as a cost to them as much as a gift to you: if they need its coal and you are the only one taking it, the flip is on your schedule.

## The map is roomier

With the same board and fewer players, more spaces stay open. Blocking a single rival is harder, because they usually have another town to go to. Links can still score well, but towns fill more slowly, so count on fewer flipped tiles at the ends of your links than in a four-player game.

## Three players: the middle game

Three players keeps most of the four-player game's tension with an extra round per era. Two rivals means the shared economy still works for you, and the table is quieter than at four. Turn order still swings: with three character tiles, moving from last to first is a jump of two places.

## Two players: a duel of plans

With two players, read your rival's mat every turn. You know both hands will run out in the same round, and with one opponent you can usually see exactly which merchant barrel, coal mine, or town space they need next. Take it first when it matters to you, and leave it when it does not.

For the wider design question of why games feel so different at each count, see our article on [player count design](/articles/player-count-design/).
