Welcome to Level 2. At Level 1 you learned to play clean Duels and to buy cards on purpose. Now you build a deck that does the same thing most games. Yu-Gi-Oh! decks are drawn from a known set of cards, which means "how often will I open this?" has a real answer.

## How many cards do you see?

Both players draw 5 cards to start. The player going first skips their first draw, so they plan their first turn with 5 cards. The player going second draws on their first turn, so they see 6.

That one card matters. Every number below comes in two columns for that reason.

## The odds table

This table gives the chance of opening **at least one** card from a group in a 40-card deck. It uses the standard formula for drawing without replacement, worked out from the real counts.

| Copies in a 40-card deck | Going first (5 cards) | Going second (6 cards) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 12.5% | 15.0% |
| 3 | 33.8% | 39.4% |
| 6 | 57.7% | 65.0% |
| 9 | 74.2% | 80.8% |
| 12 | 85.1% | 90.2% |
| 15 | 91.9% | 95.4% |

Read it like this: one copy of a card shows up in your opening five only about one game in eight. Three copies, about one game in three. Twelve cards that all start your plan, about six games in seven going first.

## Why 40 cards, not 45

The rulebook allows a Main Deck of 40 to 60 cards. Its own advice, and most competitive practice, is to stay close to 40. Here is why, for 12 starters:

| Deck size | Going first (5 cards) | Going second (6 cards) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 40 | 85.1% | 90.2% |
| 45 | 80.6% | 86.4% |
| 60 | 68.6% | 75.5% |

Five extra cards cost you almost five points of consistency on the cards you care most about. Add a card only when it earns that cost.

## Count your starters with care

Lesson 8 sorted cards into three jobs. Now count them strictly.

- **Starters** begin your main plan from a normal hand, alone. A card that needs a second specific card is not a starter. It is half of one.
- **Extenders** keep the plan going after your opponent stops the first piece. They rarely start anything alone.
- **Interaction** stops your opponent. Hand traps, which lesson 13 covers, are the main example.

Many decks also run cards that search for a starter. Those count as starters too, since they do the same job in your opening hand. That is how modern decks reach 12 or more starters without running 12 different cards.

## A worked example

Say your plan begins with any of three different monsters, you run 3 copies of each, and you add 3 copies of a Spell that searches one of them. That is 12 starters in 40 cards.

- Going first, you open at least one about 85% of the time.
- Going second, about 90%.
- So roughly one game in seven going first, you open no starter at all. Plan for that hand too: what does your deck do with only extenders and interaction?

Now say you cut the Spell to make room for three favorite cards. Nine starters drop you to about 74% going first. That is one game in four with no plan. Seeing the number makes the trade clear.

## Two in hand, not just one

Some plans want a starter plus an extender. The odds of two specific roles together fall fast, so build the deck so that most single starters can make a decent play alone. For a feel of the numbers: with 12 interaction cards in 40, the chance of opening two or more is about 48% going first and about 60% going second.

## Test hands

Numbers set the shape. Hands tell you the truth. Shuffle, deal five, and ask: can this hand make my play? Write a tick or a cross. After 20 hands, compare your ticks to the table. If they are far below it, something you counted as a starter is not really one.

## Ratios to start from, not rules

Every deck is different, but many competitive builds land in a shape like this: enough starters to open one around 85% of the time, a block of hand traps, a few extenders, and very few cards that do nothing alone. Treat that as a first draft. Your test hands and your losses will tell you what to change.
