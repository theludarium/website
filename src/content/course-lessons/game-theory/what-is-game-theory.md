You hold a card back because your neighbor wants it. You offer a trade, then wonder why it was accepted so fast. You stop helping the leader two turns before the end. None of that is luck or math homework. It is game theory, and you have been doing it for years.

## The short answer

Game theory is the study of choices made by people whose results depend on each other. A puzzle does not care what you do. A deck of solitaire cards does not plot against you. A table of friends does. The moment another person's choice changes your result, and yours changes theirs, you are in a game in the game theory sense.

That covers far more than board games. Economists use it for prices and auctions, biologists for animal behavior, diplomats for treaties. But board games are the cleanest place to learn it, because the rules are written down, the payoffs are on the score pad, and you get to practice every week.

## Where it came from

The field grew out of games from the start.

- **1913.** The mathematician Ernst Zermelo asked whether chess, played perfectly, has a fixed result. It was one of the first formal results about a game.
- **1928.** John von Neumann published a paper on the theory of parlor games. It proved the minimax theorem, which says that in a two-player game where one player's gain is the other's loss, each side has a way to play, sometimes by mixing its moves at random, that guarantees it a certain result on average. Lesson 8 uses it to explain bluffing.
- **1944.** Von Neumann and the economist Oskar Morgenstern published *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior*, the book that founded the modern field. It includes a long analysis of poker.
- **1950.** John Nash, then a graduate student at Princeton, published a short paper that extended the idea of a stable outcome to games with any number of players. That idea, the Nash equilibrium, is lesson 5.

The Nobel prize in economics went to game theorists in 1994 (John Harsanyi, John Nash, and Reinhard Selten) and in 2005 (Robert Aumann and Thomas Schelling), the second time for work on conflict and cooperation.

## You already play it

Here is a quick tour of ideas this course will name, in games you may own.

- **A Catan trade.** Before you accept, you ask what the other player gets out of it. That is reading payoffs.
- **A Codenames clue.** The spymaster hunts for a word the team will read the same way. That is a coordination game, and it has its own tools.
- **A skull in Skull.** If you never play your skull, people learn it. Being unpredictable on purpose is a mixed strategy.
- **Military in 7 Wonders.** You and a neighbor both spend picks on shields, tie, and neither of you scores, when skipping military would have tied too. That shape is the famous prisoner's dilemma.
- **The last round of almost anything.** Deals dry up, because there is no next turn to reward a favor. Thinking back from the end is called backward induction.

## The four questions

Every lesson in this course leans on four questions. They are on the [teach sheet](/academy/game-theory/teach-sheet/) too.

1. **Players.** Who decides? Count anyone who can change the result.
2. **Choices.** What can each player do, and when?
3. **Payoffs.** What does each player really want? Points, yes, but also a win, a laugh, or revenge for last week.
4. **Information.** Who knows what, and when do they learn it?

Answer those and you have a map of the game. Lesson 3 shows how to draw it.

## What game theory is not

**It is not a formula that wins every game.** Most real games are far too big to solve. Chess has never been solved, more than a century after Zermelo.

**It is not cold.** Payoffs are whatever people care about, kindness included. A friend who would rather see you win than the leader is part of the map.

**It is not about tricking people.** Many of its best ideas explain why people cooperate, why trust breaks, and how better rules bring it back. Those are the ideas this course cares about most.

## At the table

Pick one turn per game to slow down. Ask the four questions, then ask one more: if everyone sees my move coming, what do they do? That single habit is most of what strong players do on instinct. Luck still plays its part, and our article on [luck versus skill](/articles/luck-vs-skill-board-games/) shows why the best games keep both.

## At work

The same four questions work in a meeting. Before a project kickoff, list the players, including the ones not in the room. List what each can do and what each is rewarded for. Note who knows what. "Why is finance blocking this?" becomes "What is finance measured on, and how could this plan help them too?" That is the spirit of the At work sections in this course: clearer decisions and better incentives, never tricks.

## How the course climbs

Read the Table has five levels. **Player** maps games. **Tactician** finds the best move with dominance, equilibrium, and backward induction. **Strategist** mixes, cooperates, signals, and bids. **Negotiator** bargains, commits, and handles coalitions. **Architect** designs rules and incentives, at the table and at work.
