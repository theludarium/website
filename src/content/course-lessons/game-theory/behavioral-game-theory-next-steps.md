This course has mostly asked what a perfectly logical player would do. That is the right place to start, because it shows you the shape of every game. But you never play against perfectly logical players. You play against people. **Behavioral game theory** studies how people really choose, using experiments. Colin Camerer's book *Behavioral Game Theory* (2003) gathered the field's results. Here are three of the most useful, and what they mean for your next game.

## People punish unfair offers

Lesson 12 introduced the ultimatum game. One player proposes how to split a pot, and the other accepts or rejects, and a rejection leaves both with nothing. Pure logic says accept any offer above zero, so the proposer should offer almost nothing.

Since Güth, Schmittberger, and Schwarze first ran it in 1982, the game has been repeated many times, and the result keeps coming back. Camerer and Thaler's 1995 review reported that offers usually average 30 to 40 percent, an even split is often the most common offer, and offers under 20 percent are often rejected. Responders give up real rewards to punish what they see as unfair. Offers so far above the minimum suggest many proposers expect this.

At the table, this is the trade offer that gets refused even though it helps the other player. Fairness is part of the payoff. Leave it out of your map, and your map is wrong.

## How many steps ahead?

Try this game. Everyone writes a whole number from 0 to 100. The winner is closest to two-thirds of the average.

Work through the reasoning in steps:

| Step | Thinking | Guess |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | "I will pick anything." Guesses spread out, averaging about 50. | Any |
| 1 | "Others average about 50, so I want two-thirds of 50." | About 33 |
| 2 | "Others think like step 1 and pick 33, so two-thirds of that." | About 22 |
| 3 | "Others are at step 2." | About 15 |
| All the way | Every step lowers the target, until only 0 is left. | 0 |

Follow the logic all the way down and you reach 0, the game's equilibrium in theory. But people do not pick it. Rosemarie Nagel's 1995 experiments found that many first-round choices matched one or two steps of this reasoning, and that guesses fell toward 0 as the same group played again. When Richard Thaler ran the game as a contest in the Financial Times in 1997, 1,382 readers entered, the average was 18.9, and the winning guess was 13. He ran it again in 2015: 583 entries, an average of 17.3, and a winner of 12. That year, Thaler noted, many entrants chose 0 or 1. They had done the logic perfectly, and they lost.

Researchers call this **level-k thinking**. Dale Stahl and Paul Wilson modeled players as types who reason to different depths, and Camerer, Ho, and Chong's cognitive hierarchy model estimated that people take about 1.5 steps on average across many games. The lesson for the table: **you win by being one step deeper than the other players, not by being infinitely deep.** Against new players, the "obvious" move may be right. Against a table of experts, think one level further.

## What game theory cannot tell you

- **It needs the payoffs.** Every table in this course started with numbers. Real payoffs include fun, friendship, pride, and fairness, and they are hard to measure.
- **It often gives more than one answer.** Lesson 5 showed games with several equilibria. Theory says which outcomes are stable, not always which one you will land on.
- **It assumes others reason well.** As this lesson shows, they reason a step or two, and that changes your best move.
- **It describes. It does not decide your values.** It can tell you a threat would work. It cannot tell you whether making it is right.

## The ethics

Game theory is a way to see what everyone wants and what each will do. Used well, it makes you a better partner as well as a stronger opponent. You make fairer offers, keep promises that matter, design rules that bring out good play, and build workplaces where the right move is also the rewarded one. Used badly, the same tools can manipulate. The line is simple: use what you learn about people's payoffs to find moves that work for them too, and never to deceive them. At the table, the other players are thinking as well. Respect for that is where good strategy starts.

## Your practice plan

Spend a month on it, one game night a week. Before each game, map it in three lines: players, payoffs, information. After each game, find one moment where a different payoff would have changed your choice.

1. **Week 1: mixing and signals.** Skull, or poker with chips. Practice lessons 8 and 10: vary your bluffs and ask what each bet cost.
2. **Week 2: auctions.** [Power Grid](/academy/power-grid/) or Modern Art. Practice lesson 11: write your top price before each auction.
3. **Week 3: deals.** [Catan](/academy/catan/) or Cosmic Encounter. Practice lessons 12 and 13: name your walk-away, and keep every promise you make.
4. **Week 4: the multiplayer table.** [Root](/academy/root/), Diplomacy, or [Twilight Imperium](/academy/twilight-imperium/), or The Resistance for a shorter night. Practice lesson 14: who is the leader, and who will stop them?

Keep one cooperative game in rotation too, such as [The Crew](/academy/the-crew/) or [Codenames](/academy/codenames/), to practice signaling when everyone shares the goal. And try one design experiment from lesson 15: change a house rule and watch how the best replies change. For a wider view of where skill shows up, our article on [luck versus skill in board games](/articles/luck-vs-skill-board-games/) is a good next read.
