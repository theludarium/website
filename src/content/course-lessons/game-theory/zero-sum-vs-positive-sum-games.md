Some games have one winner and one loser, and nothing can change that. Others let everyone come out ahead. Most of the games on your shelf are a blend, and the best move depends on which part of the blend you are in. This lesson gives you the vocabulary and a quick test.

## Zero-sum: my gain is your loss

A game is **zero-sum** when the players' payoffs always add up to the same total, so anything one player gains, another loses. Two-player chess is the classic case. A win for you is a loss for your opponent. A draw splits it. No move helps you both.

You saw this in the rock, paper, scissors table in [lesson 3](/academy/game-theory/game-theory-basics-payoff-matrix/): every box added to zero. John von Neumann's first big result in game theory, in 1928, was about exactly these games, and von Neumann and Morgenstern made them the core of their 1944 book. Lesson 8 shows what he proved.

In a two-player zero-sum game, there is no deal worth making, because any deal your opponent likes is worse for you. Be friendly, but do not look for a win for both. There is none.

## Positive-sum: the pie can grow

A game is **positive-sum** when some choices leave everyone better off. Trade is the everyday example. In Catan, if you are drowning in wool and short on ore, and your neighbor is the opposite, swapping helps you both build.

| | They trade | They refuse |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **You trade** | 2, 2 | 0, 0 |
| **You refuse** | 0, 0 | 0, 0 |

These boxes do not add up to the same total. "Trade, trade" makes 4 points of progress out of nothing but a swap. That is what positive-sum means.

## The twist: one winner, many trades

Here is where multiplayer games get interesting. Catan has one winner. Taken as a whole, it is a contest for a single prize. But a trade between two players can help both of them, at the expense of the players left out.

So the right question about a Catan trade is not only "does this help me?" It is "does this help me more than it helps my partner, and does it help us both against the leader?" A trade that gives you one step and the leader two steps is a loss for you, even if it felt friendly. A trade that gives two trailing players a step each might be the best move on the board. The Catan course covers this in [the art of the trade](/academy/catan/art-of-the-trade/), and our article on [how to win at Catan](/articles/how-to-win-at-catan/) shows where trades fit in a full plan.

## Co-ops: everyone wins or nobody does

In a cooperative game such as The Crew, Sky Team, or Pandemic, all the players share one payoff. The team wins or loses together. There is no conflict of interest at all, so the game theory is about coordination: making sure everyone picks the same plan. The focal points and shared habits from [lesson 5](/academy/game-theory/nash-equilibrium-explained/) are the tools that matter here.

The one danger in a co-op is that one player plans for everyone. That is the [quarterback problem](/articles/quarterback-problem-cooperative-games/), and it is a people problem, not a math one.

## Semi-co-ops: the mixed-motive game

Some games let the whole group lose together, but only one player can win. Thomas Schelling called situations like this **mixed-motive games**: partly shared interest, partly conflict. Most of real life looks like this, and so do semi-cooperative and hidden-traitor games. You must help the group enough to avoid losing together, while making sure the group's success is your success. Our article on [semi-cooperative board games](/articles/semi-cooperative-board-games/) covers how these games work and why they can strain a table.

## A quick test at the table

Ask three questions about any moment in a game:

1. **Can we both gain from this?** If not, it is a zero-sum moment. Compete.
2. **Who gains more?** If you both gain, check the split. In a race, a lopsided win for your partner is a loss for you.
3. **Who is left out?** In a multiplayer game, every deal also affects the players not in it. Sometimes that is the point.

## At work

**Most work is positive-sum, even when it feels zero-sum.** A single promotion or a fixed budget line can be zero-sum. Most of what a team does is not: customers, products, and reputations can all grow. Max Bazerman and Margaret Neale named the "mythical fixed pie," the habit of assuming the other side's gain must be your loss, as one of the most common mistakes in negotiation. Before you defend your slice, ask whether there is a bigger pie to bake together.

**Shared credit grows.** When a project goes well and the credit is shared fairly, everyone's standing rises, and the next project gets easier to staff. Treating credit as zero-sum is a choice, and usually a costly one.

**Name the zero-sum parts plainly.** When something really is fixed, such as one open role, say so. People handle a clear contest better than a contest dressed up as teamwork.
