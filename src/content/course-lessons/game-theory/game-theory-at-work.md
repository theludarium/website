A board game hands you the rules, the players, and the scoring before you start. Work hands you none of them. The players change, the payoffs are hidden, and the game never ends. That makes the habits from this course more useful at work, not less. This lesson gives you one repeatable method, then applies it to four common situations.

## The map the game worksheet

Before a decision that involves other people, take one page and answer seven lines. Lessons 3 to 15 feed these lines.

1. **Players.** Who decides, and who can change the result? Include people who are not in the room.
2. **Choices.** What can each player do, and when? Who moves first?
3. **Payoffs.** What does each player want? Use their goals, not yours: their targets, their workload, their reputation.
4. **Information.** Who knows what? What does each player not know, and what are they signaling (lesson 10)?
5. **Equilibrium.** If everyone does what is best for them, where does this end up (lesson 5)? Is it a prisoner's dilemma (lesson 9)?
6. **The move you want.** What is your best reply, given all of the above? Work back from the end (lesson 6).
7. **The incentive you could change.** If the equilibrium is bad for everyone, which rule, reward, or piece of information could you change so the good outcome becomes everyone's best move (lesson 15)?

Line 7 is where most of the value is. It turns "why are they being difficult?" into "what makes their choice sensible, and can I change that?"

## Credit and blame

Shared work has a built-in problem. Bengt Holmström's 1982 paper "Moral Hazard in Teams" showed that when a group shares one result, each person's effort is partly hidden in it, and that weakens everyone's incentive to put it in. Blame does not fix this. Visibility does: clear owners for clear pieces, and credit that names who did what.

Credit is also a repeated game. As lesson 9 showed, the future keeps people honest. Colleagues who share credit generously get help the next time, and people who grab it find that help dries up.

## A tiny table: helping a teammate

Rewards shape the equilibrium. Say helping a teammate costs you a little of your own output but adds a lot to theirs. Here are your results under two reward schemes.

| Reward scheme | You help | You do not help | Your best move |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Only your own output counts | You 4, teammate 7 | You 5, teammate 4 | Do not help |
| Help is recognized in reviews (+2) | You 6, teammate 7 | You 5, teammate 4 | Help |

Under the first scheme, the team total is better with help (11 against 9), but your best move is not to help. Nobody is being selfish. The scheme is. Steven Kerr's classic 1975 article "On the Folly of Rewarding A, While Hoping for B" is full of cases like this: organizations reward one behavior and then wonder why they do not get another.

## Meetings

Meetings are full of cheap talk, in the sense of lesson 10. If disagreeing with the most senior person is risky, then agreeing is the safe move, and the meeting learns nothing. To get real information, change the payoffs: collect views in writing before the discussion, ask the most junior people first, and thank whoever brings bad news. A clear draft or a default also helps. As lesson 5 showed, a group coordinates faster when there is an obvious focal point to gather around.

## Raises and promotions

- **Know your walk-away.** Lesson 12's BATNA applies directly. Your alternative is what you would do if the answer is no. Improve it with real options, and never claim an offer you do not have.
- **Send costly signals.** Documented results, shipped work, and people you have trained are harder to fake than a confident pitch, so they are believed.
- **Watch the tournament.** Edward Lazear and Sherwin Rosen's 1981 paper described promotions as tournaments: pay by rank can drive effort. The same setup can make helping a rival peer look like a bad move, as the table above showed. If you manage people, reward the help you want to see.

## At the table

Game nights are good practice for all of this. Cooperative games like The Crew show how a team shares limited information, and negotiation games show how walk-aways shape deals. Our list of [team building games for work](/articles/team-building-board-games/) suggests games that fit a team.

## At work, fairly

Game theory at work is for seeing clearly and designing better incentives, never for tricking people. Map their payoffs so you can offer deals that are good for them too. Share information that helps the group reach a better equilibrium. When a system rewards the wrong thing, say so and propose a fix, in the open. The goal is a workplace where the best move for each person is also the best move for the team.
