"If you attack me, I will spend the rest of the game making sure you lose." Everyone has heard a line like that. Sometimes it works. Often the table ignores it. Game theory explains the difference, and it starts with one question: when the moment comes, would you really do it?

## Threats and promises

Thomas Schelling's *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960) studied threats and promises as strategic moves. A **threat** says: if you do this, I will do something that hurts you, even though it costs me too. A **promise** says: if you do this, I will reward you, even though rewarding you costs me. Both have the same weakness. Once the moment arrives, carrying them out is no longer in your interest.

Lesson 6 showed the problem with game trees: work back from the end, and an empty threat falls away, because a player will not choose a branch that hurts them. Schelling's big insight was what to do about it. Sometimes the strongest move is to **take away your own options**, so that the move you threatened becomes the only one left. He shared the 2005 Nobel Prize with Robert Aumann for work on conflict and cooperation through game theory.

## A standoff on a score pad

Two players both want the same contested space. If both commit to it, they wear each other down and a third player profits. If one backs off, the other takes it. This is the game of chicken.

| | They back off | They push in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **You back off** | 3, 3 | 1, 5 |
| **You push in** | 5, 1 | 0, 0 |

There are two pure equilibria, in the sense of lesson 5: one player pushes in and the other backs off. (There is also a mixed one, in the sense of lesson 8, where each pushes in two times out of three and the crash happens often.) Each player would like to be the one who pushes. Now suppose you commit first, in a way the other player can see and cannot doubt: your units are already there and cannot leave. Their choice is now 1 for backing off or 0 for pushing in. They back off. By giving up your own option to retreat, you won the standoff.

The catch: a commitment only helps if it is visible and believed. If they doubt it and push in anyway, you both end up at 0, 0.

## Games where your word is all you have

- **Diplomacy.** Nothing anyone says during negotiation binds them when orders are written. Every promise is only as good as the player making it.
- **Root.** The Law of Root lets players make agreements, but they are not binding, and actions never need consent. The [Root table talk lesson](/academy/root/root-table-talk-kingmaking/) covers how to deal well anyway.
- **Twilight Imperium.** The rules split deals in two. A deal resolved right away, such as trade goods for votes now, is binding. A promise about the future is not. The [agenda phase lesson](/academy/twilight-imperium/twilight-imperium-agenda-phase/) has the details.

Twilight Imperium's line is the whole lesson in a rule. When you can, turn a promise into an immediate swap, because the game enforces it.

## Ways to make your word count

Avinash Dixit and Barry Nalebuff list a set of ways to make commitments believable in *Thinking Strategically*. Several work at the table:

- **Reputation.** In a repeated game, as lesson 9 showed, the future keeps people honest. A player who keeps promises across many games gets better deals in every one.
- **Contracts.** Use whatever the rules enforce, like Twilight Imperium's immediate deals.
- **Small steps.** Split a big deal into pieces, so neither side risks much at any one time.
- **Burning bridges.** Commit publicly or move pieces where they cannot easily come back, so retreat stops being an option.

## At the table

- **Only threaten what you would do.** If carrying it out would hurt you more than them, assume they know.
- **Keep threats proportionate.** "I will block your next road" is believable. "I will ruin your whole game" usually is not.
- **Build a record with small promises.** Keep them in plain view early, when they are cheap to keep.
- **Read others' threats with lesson 6.** Ask what they would actually choose when the moment comes, not what they say now.

## At work

A deadline that always slips, or a policy with endless exceptions, is an empty threat, and teams learn that fast. A manager who says "no exceptions" and then makes them has taught everyone that the rule is open to negotiation. The fix is to promise less and deliver every time. Make commitments you are able and willing to keep, put important ones in writing, and keep small ones visibly. Credibility built that way is the honest kind of power: people trust you because you have been worth trusting, not because you scared them.
