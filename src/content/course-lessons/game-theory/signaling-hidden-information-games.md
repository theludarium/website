Some games put everything on the table. Chess hides nothing. But poker hides your cards, The Resistance hides who the spies are, and Skull hides what is under every disc. In those games, each move does two jobs. It changes the game, and it tells the table something. This lesson is about which messages you should believe.

## Two kinds of message

Game theory splits messages into two families.

- **Costly signals.** The move itself costs something. Michael Spence's 1973 paper on job market signaling showed why these work: a signal is believable when it is cheap enough for the type of player it points to, and too costly for anyone pretending.
- **Cheap talk.** Words that cost nothing and change no payoffs. Vincent Crawford and Joel Sobel showed in 1982 that cheap talk can still carry real information, but only as much as the speaker's and listener's interests line up. When they want opposite things, words tell you very little.

Spence shared the 2001 Nobel Prize with George Akerlof and Joseph Stiglitz for their work on markets where one side knows more than the other.

## Costly signals at the table

A poker bet costs chips. That is what makes it a signal. A big bet with a strong hand is cheap, because you expect the chips back. The same bet with a weak hand is expensive, because you lose them whenever you are called. Lesson 8 showed why you still bluff sometimes: if you never did, your bets would give your hand away.

Skull builds the cost right into the rules. If you win the bidding, you must flip all of your own discs before anyone else's. A player who put down their own skull is taking a real risk by bidding high. So a confident bid tells you something, though never everything.

## A signal on a score pad

Strip it down to two kinds of player, strong and weak, and one costly action. Anyone the table reads as strong gets 10. Anyone read as weak gets 5. The action costs a strong player 2 and a weak player 6.

| Player | Cost of the signal | Result with the signal | Result without it | Best move |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strong | 2 | 10 minus 2 = 8 | 5 | Send it |
| Weak | 6 | 10 minus 6 = 4 | 5 | Skip it |

Only strong players send it, so the signal sorts them, and the table can trust it. Now change the weak player's cost to 4. Sending it earns them 6, which beats 5, so they send it too. Everyone signals, and the signal stops meaning anything, even though it still costs the weak player more. **A signal works only when it costs a bluffer more than it would win them**, while the real type can still afford it. A gap in cost is not enough on its own. It has to be wide enough.

## Cheap talk and hidden roles

In The Resistance, everyone says "I am loyal." The spies know each other, and they want missions to fail while the Resistance wants them to pass. With interests that opposite, the claim is close to worthless. What carries information is what players do: how they vote on teams, who they pick, and which missions fail with them on board. Resistance players must play success cards, so a failed mission is hard evidence that a spy was on that team. Our article on [why social deduction games work](/articles/why-social-deduction-games-work/) goes further into the art of lying well.

Now flip it. In Codenames, the spymaster and the team want the same thing, so a clue is cheap talk you can trust. The design problem is the opposite: if talk were free and unlimited, the game would be over in a minute. So the rules ration it to one word and one number, with no faces or hints. [The Crew](/academy/the-crew/the-crew-communication-strategy/) goes further: each player may signal with one card, once per mission, and Sky Team asks for silence while the dice go down. When players share interests, designers limit the channel. When players oppose each other, the channel is wide open, and mostly noise.

## The lemon problem

George Akerlof's 1970 paper "The Market for Lemons" made a related point about used cars. A seller knows more than the buyer, so the buyer worries that any car for sale is a bad one, and good cars get harder to sell. At the table, the same logic says: when someone is very eager to make a trade, ask what they know. A player happy to hand over a resource may be about to lose it anyway, or may know they cannot use it.

## At the table

- **Ask what it cost.** A move that would hurt a bluffer is worth more than a speech.
- **Watch actions over claims.** Votes, bids, and plays are harder to fake than words.
- **In team games, agree on conventions.** Shared interests make cheap talk work, so make your signals mean the same thing to everyone.
- **Spend your own signals on purpose.** If your bets always match your hand, you are easy to read. Mix them, as lesson 8 showed.

## At work

Work is full of signals. A working sample, a reference, or a finished project costs effort to produce, so it is believed more than a line on a résumé. Status updates are cheap talk. They are believable when people share the goal, and they turn into noise when bad news gets punished. If you lead a team, the fix is not to demand more honesty. It is to make bad news safe to report, so that telling the truth is the best move. And never fake a signal: once people learn your signals are cheap, they stop listening.
