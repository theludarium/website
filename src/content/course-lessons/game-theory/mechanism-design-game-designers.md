Welcome to Level 4. Up to now, the rules were fixed and you looked for the best move inside them. Designers work the other way. They decide what they want to happen at the table, and then they write rules that make players want to do it. Economists call this **mechanism design**. Leonid Hurwicz, Eric Maskin, and Roger Myerson shared the 2007 Nobel Prize for laying its foundations.

## The key test: is good play the best reply?

A mechanism is any set of rules that turns players' choices into results: an auction, a vote, a scoring system, a board game. Mechanism design asks one question of every rule. **If every player does what is best for them, do we get the result we wanted?** If the answer is no, players are not the problem. The rule is.

You have already seen one answer in lesson 11. In a second-price auction, bidding your true value is a weakly dominant strategy: never worse, and sometimes better. A seller who wants honest bids does not need to ask for honesty. The rule makes it the best move.

## I cut, you choose

The oldest mechanism in the book is for sharing a cake between two people: one cuts, the other chooses. Hugo Steinhaus and his students later worked out fair methods for more than two people, and Steven Brams and Alan Taylor's book on fair division surveys these methods. Run it through lesson 6. The chooser will take the piece they like better. So the cutter's best move is to cut two pieces they would be equally happy with. Nobody needs to trust anybody. The rule produces fairness from pure self-interest.

Board games use this all the time. In Hanamikoji, two of your four actions are divide and choose. With the Gift action, you offer three cards and your opponent takes one, and you keep the other two. With Competition, you offer two pairs and your opponent picks a pair. Your best offer is one you are happy with whichever way they choose, which is exactly the cutter's logic. Draft games such as Sushi Go! spread the same idea around the table: every hand you pass is a choice you leave to someone else.

## Catch-up rules

A runaway leader is a design problem: the player ahead uses the lead to get further ahead, and the last hour is a formality. Power Grid answers it with player order. The player with the most cities goes first in the plant auction, but then buys fuel and builds last, when fuel is dearer and the good cities may be gone. The player in last place gets the cheapest fuel and first choice of cities.

Every catch-up rule creates a new incentive, and designers have to check it too. If falling behind is rewarded, some players will choose to fall behind for a turn. That is not a flaw if the choice is interesting, but it is a payoff the designer created and must test. Our article on [catch-up mechanics](/articles/catch-up-mechanics-board-games/) compares the main approaches.

## Hiding the score

Lesson 14 showed why tables turn on the leader and why kingmakers cause arguments. One design answer is to keep the lead uncertain. In Ticket to Ride, destination tickets stay secret until the end, so nobody can be sure who is winning, and it is harder to gang up on the leader or to crown one.

## A designer's checklist

When you design a game or a house rule, run each rule through these questions:

1. **What do I want players to do?** Name it in one sentence.
2. **What is each player's best reply under this rule?** Use lessons 4 to 6.
3. **Is there a dominant strategy?** If one move is always best, the choice is not a choice.
4. **Does the rule reward something nobody enjoys,** like stalling, hiding, or doing nothing?
5. **What does it reward that I did not intend?** Only playtesting will tell you. Our guide on [how to playtest a board game](/articles/how-to-playtest-a-board-game/) shows how to find out without fooling yourself.

## At the table

House rules are mechanism design. A group that picks games by "loudest voice wins" gets the same three games forever. Try a rule where one person proposes three games and the next person chooses: the proposer now has a reason to offer three good options. If your table has kingmaking trouble, a norm agreed before play changes the payoffs before anyone is tempted.

## At work

A bonus scheme, a sales target, and a review process are all mechanisms, and people play them as written, not as intended. Before launching one, ask what a smart, self-interested person would do under it. If the answer is not what you want, fix the rule rather than blaming the players.
