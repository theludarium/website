Two Power Grid players who both burn coal hurt each other every round. Each marker costs the price on its space, the cheap ones go first, and every coal one player buys makes the next coal dearer for the other. The fix is not to buy faster. It is to burn something else. That is business strategy in one picture: some fights cannot be won, only avoided, and the best move is often to change which game you are in.

## Price wars are a prisoner's dilemma

Two firms sell similar products. Each can hold its price or cut it. Here are the results, in the same shape as lesson 9.

| | They hold price | They cut price |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **You hold price** | 3, 3 | 0, 5 |
| **You cut price** | 5, 0 | 1, 1 |

Cutting is the dominant strategy for each firm (lesson 4), so both cut and both end up at 1. That is the price war. Lesson 9 showed why repeated games often avoid this outcome: firms that will face each other for years have reason not to start a fight they will both lose.

One line you must never cross: **do not agree on prices with competitors.** The US Federal Trade Commission defines price fixing as an agreement among competitors that raises, lowers, or stabilizes prices, whether it is written, spoken, or inferred from conduct. It calls price fixing a major concern of antitrust enforcement, and violators can face fines and prison. The legal way out of a price war is the one the coal players found. Compete on something else: quality, service, speed, a different customer.

## Threats to newcomers

A company in a market often threatens to crush any newcomer with a price war. Work it back from the end, as in lesson 6. Say a newcomer can enter or stay out. If it enters, the incumbent can fight, which leaves the incumbent with 1 and the newcomer losing 1, or share the market, leaving 3 and 2. If the newcomer stays out, the incumbent keeps 5 and the newcomer gets 0.

Once the newcomer is in, the incumbent's best reply is to share, because 3 beats 1. The newcomer can see that, and 2 beats 0, so it enters. The threat was empty.

Reinhard Selten's 1978 paper "The Chain Store Paradox" pushed this further. A chain facing newcomers in town after town might want to fight early to build a reputation, yet the backward induction logic says share every time. Selten argued the gap tells us something about how real decision makers reason. Lesson 13 gives the other half: commitment. Avinash Dixit's 1980 paper showed how an investment made before a rival arrives, such as capacity already built, can change the incumbent's own payoffs so that fighting becomes its best reply, and the threat becomes believable.

For a newcomer, the lesson runs the other way. Enter in a way that gives the incumbent little reason to fight: small, in a niche, or as a partner rather than a rival.

## Co-opetition and the value net

Adam Brandenburger and Barry Nalebuff's book *Co-opetition* (1996) argued that business is not only war and not only peace. They drew a **value net** around any company with four kinds of player:

- **Customers** and **suppliers**, along one line.
- **Competitors** and **complementors**, along the other. A complementor is a player whose product makes yours more valuable to customers.

Complementors are the ones companies forget. A game store and a game cafe can be complementors: one sells games, the other gives people a place to play them, and each makes the other more useful. The same company can be a competitor in one moment and a complementor in the next.

Brandenburger and Nalebuff's 1995 Harvard Business Review article "The Right Game" listed five things you can change, which they called PARTS: the **players**, the **added values** each brings, the **rules**, the **tactics** that shape what others believe, and the **scope** of the game. Every one of them is a lesson in this course seen from the boardroom.

## Using the worksheet

The map the game worksheet from lesson 16 works for a company too. List the players around your value net, the choices each has, and what each wants. Find the equilibrium. Then spend most of your time on line 7: what could you change so that a better outcome becomes everyone's best move? A new partner, a different price structure, a product that competes on a new dimension, or a clearer signal of quality (lesson 10) are all ways to change the game.

## At the table

Notice when you are in a fight nobody can win. Two players racing for the same route in Ticket to Ride can both spend turns hoarding the same cards while a third player builds freely. Two players burning the same fuel in Power Grid pay more every round. The strong move is usually to find the space nobody is fighting over.

## At work

Use these tools to compete harder and more fairly: better products, better deals, and partnerships that help customers. Do not use them to collude, mislead, or hide.
