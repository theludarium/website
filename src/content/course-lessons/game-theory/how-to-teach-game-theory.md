Explaining game theory in words takes a while. Playing it takes ten minutes and a stack of scrap paper. This lesson is a host script for one activity, the Two Thirds game, that shows the core idea of the whole field: your best move depends on what you think others will do, and on what you think they think you will do.

For general habits that make any teach go better, see [how to teach a board game](/articles/how-to-teach-a-board-game/).

## What you need

- **Players:** 5 to 20 is ideal. Three or four works. Do not run it with two (see the host notes).
- **Paper:** one small slip per player per round, and a pen each.
- **A calculator:** any phone.
- **A host** who reads the rules, does the math, and does not play.
- **A prize**, if you like: first pick of snacks, or choosing the first game.

## The rules, word for word

Read this aloud and answer only questions about the rules, never about strategy:

"Write a whole number from 0 to 100 on your slip, with your initials on the back. Fold it and hand it to me. I will add up all the numbers, find the average, and take two thirds of that average. That is the target. The closest number to the target wins. Ties share the win. We will play three rounds."

## The ten minutes

| Minute | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 0 to 1 | Read the rules. No strategy talk. |
| 1 to 2 | Round 1. Everyone writes in silence and hands in a slip. |
| 2 to 4 | Read every number aloud, without names. Add, divide by the number of players, multiply by 2, divide by 3. Announce the target and the winner. Write the numbers where everyone can see them. |
| 4 to 5 | Round 2, same rules. |
| 5 to 6 | Reveal and announce. |
| 6 to 7 | Round 3, then reveal. |
| 7 to 10 | Debrief. |

**A worked example.** Five players write 50, 40, 33, 20, and 7. They add up to 150. The average is 150 divided by 5, which is 30. Two thirds of 30 is 20. The player who wrote 20 wins.

## What usually happens

In round one, numbers spread out, but they tend to bunch in a few places. Someone reasons: "If people pick at random, the average is about 50, so the target is about 33." Someone else goes one step further: "If everyone thinks that, the target is about 22." In Rosemarie Nagel's 1995 experiments with this game, first-round guesses clustered near those steps, and they fell toward zero round after round as players learned from the results.

The theory says the only answer nobody can improve on by changing alone is everyone writing 0. (With whole numbers and five or more players, everyone writing 1 also holds up, a fun wrinkle for the sharpest player in the room.) Yet the person who writes 0 in round one almost never wins. Being right about the theory and being right about the room are different skills. The economist John Maynard Keynes made the same point in 1936, comparing the stock market to a newspaper beauty contest where you win by picking the face other people will pick.

## Debrief questions

Ask these in order. Let people answer before you explain.

1. **What did you write in round one, and what were you guessing about the rest of us?** Listen for the steps: "I thought you would pick around 50," then "I thought you would think that."
2. **Why did the numbers fall each round?** Seeing the last round changed everyone's guess about everyone else.
3. **Was 0 a smart first answer?** It is right in theory and usually loses. What does that say about using theory with real people?
4. **Where in a game we own do you have to guess what others will guess?** Good answers: a Codenames clue, a card you pass in a draft, a bid in an auction game.
5. **Where does this happen outside games?** Good answers: setting a price when a competitor is setting theirs, picking a launch date, guessing what a manager expects in a report.

Close with one sentence: "Game theory is the habit of asking what everyone else will do, and what they think you will do, before you choose."

## Host notes

- **Keep it anonymous.** Read numbers without names. The winner claims their slip by the initials.
- **Keep the math fast.** One calculator, one person. Slow math kills the energy.
- **Why not two players?** With two, the target is always closer to the lower number, so the lower guess always wins and 0 can never lose. It stops being a guessing game.
- **Stop at three rounds.** End while people still want a fourth.

## At work

The same ten minutes opens a team session well, because it is quick, fair, and nobody needs to know any games. Run it as written, then add one question to the debrief: "Where on this team are we each guessing what the others will do, instead of asking?" The answers point to real coordination problems, such as unclear priorities or everyone waiting for someone else to raise a risk. The fix is rarely cleverness. It is usually saying the plan out loud so nobody has to guess. Teams that want a guided session can look at our [corporate events](/corporate-events/).
