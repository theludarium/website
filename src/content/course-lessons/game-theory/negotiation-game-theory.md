Welcome to Level 3. So far you have mostly played against the rules and against moves. Now you play against people who can talk back. Catan trades, Cosmic Encounter deals, and Twilight Imperium votes are all bargains. Game theory has three big ideas for them: your walk-away, the split, and the fairness that real people demand.

## Your walk-away is your power

Roger Fisher and William Ury's book *Getting to Yes* (1981) gave negotiators a name for it: your **BATNA**, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. It is what you will do if this deal falls through. Any deal worse than your BATNA is a deal to refuse.

Catan shows it clearly. On your turn, every trade must involve you, the active player. If nobody offers what you need, you can still trade with the bank at 4 to 1, or 3 to 1 or 2 to 1 with the right port. That bank trade is your BATNA. A player with a good port can say no to a bad offer, and everyone can feel it.

Cosmic Encounter shows the other side. When both players reveal Negotiate cards, they have one minute to make a deal. If they fail, each loses three ships to the warp. The designers made the walk-away painful on purpose, so deals happen.

## How the gains get split

John Nash's 1950 paper "The Bargaining Problem" asked how two sides should share what a deal adds. His answer, in its simplest form: each side first gets its walk-away, and then they share the extra, the **surplus**, evenly when nothing else sets them apart.

Say a deal is worth 10 points between you. Your walk-away is worth 2 and theirs is worth 4.

| Case | Your walk-away | Their walk-away | Surplus (10 minus both) | You get | They get |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Before | 2 | 4 | 4 | 2 + 2 = 4 | 4 + 2 = 6 |
| You improve your walk-away | 4 | 4 | 2 | 4 + 1 = 5 | 4 + 1 = 5 |

Look at what moved your share. Not a better speech. A better alternative. Building toward a port in Catan does more for your trades than any pitch.

Patience matters too. Ariel Rubinstein's 1982 model of offers and counteroffers showed that when waiting costs both sides, the side that minds the wait less gets a bigger share. In a game, that is the player who does not need the trade this turn.

## The ultimatum game

In 1982, Werner Güth, Rolf Schmittberger, and Bernd Schwarze ran a famous test. One person proposes how to split a pot. The other can accept, or reject, and then both get nothing. Work it back from the end, as in lesson 6: any offer above zero beats nothing, so the responder should accept it, and the proposer should offer the smallest amount possible.

Real people do not play that way. Reviewing the many experiments that followed, Colin Camerer and Richard Thaler reported that offers usually average 30 to 40 percent of the pot, an even split is often the most common offer, and offers under 20 percent are often rejected. People will pay to punish an offer they see as unfair. Lesson 18 looks at why.

At the table, this explains the Catan offer that would help the other player, and still gets refused. "One wool for two of your ore" may beat paying the bank four cards for that wool, but if it feels like a squeeze, they will say no, and they will remember it.

## At the table

- **Name your walk-away before you speak.** You cannot judge an offer without it.
- **Build your alternatives.** Ports, a second source of a resource, or a plan that does not need this trade.
- **Look for trades that create value.** The best trade moves a card from someone who cannot use it to someone who can.
- **Leave them a reason to say yes.** A fair offer accepted beats a greedy offer refused. Our [Catan table talk lesson](/academy/catan/table-talk-and-negotiation/) has more on reading the table while you deal.

## At work

The same three ideas carry into a salary talk, a vendor contract, or a project handoff. Know your walk-away, and if it is weak, improve it before you negotiate, not during. Strengthen it with real options, never invented ones: a made-up offer is a lie, and it falls apart the moment someone checks. *Getting to Yes* also urges negotiators to focus on interests, not positions: timing, scope, and support can create value that a fight over one number never will. And remember the ultimatum game. People reject deals that feel unfair, even when the deal helps them, so a fair split is often the one that closes.
