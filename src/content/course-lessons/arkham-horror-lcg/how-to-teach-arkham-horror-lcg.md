Arkham Horror: The Card Game looks dense on the table: five card types, four skills, a bag of tokens, and a book of story text. The trick to teaching it is that new players only need about a third of it to take a first turn. The rest can arrive as the cards do.

This script follows the 2026 core set and its rulebook. It takes about ten minutes, plus a first round played together. Print the [teach sheet](/academy/arkham-horror-lcg/teach-sheet/) and put a copy in front of each player.

## Before anyone sits down

Set the whole first scenario up yourself, following the rulebook's "First Game Setup." Lay out the agenda and act decks, the scenario reference card, the encounter deck, the starting location, and the chaos bag. Let each player choose an investigator, but have the starter decks already shuffled.

The rulebook recommends starting with the first scenario and the starter decks. Custom decks can wait.

## The script

**1. The story (one minute).** "We are a team of investigators, and this is a horror story we play through together. Everyone wins or loses as a group. This game is one chapter of a longer campaign, and what happens tonight carries into the next game."

**2. The race (one minute).** Point at the two decks. "This is the act deck: our progress. We move it forward by finding clues and spending them together. This is the agenda deck: the bad guys' progress. Every round it gains doom, and when it fills up, things get worse. We want to finish the act deck first."

**3. Your investigator (one minute).** Have everyone look at their investigator card. "These four numbers are your skills: willpower, intellect, combat, and agility. You also have health and sanity. Run out of either and you are knocked out of this game." Then point to the ability text: "This is what makes you special. Read it now."

**4. A round (two minutes).** "Each round has four phases. In round one we skip the first." Walk the teach sheet's list:

- Mythos: doom goes on the agenda, then everyone draws a card from the encounter deck. Bad things.
- Investigation: everyone takes three actions. We choose who goes first.
- Enemy: monsters move and attack.
- Upkeep: everyone readies, draws a card, and gets a resource.

**5. Actions (two minutes).** "On your turn you get three actions. You can draw, take a resource, play a card from your hand, move, investigate to find clues, or fight, evade, or engage enemies." Point out the player reference card each investigator comes with.

**6. One skill test, out loud (two minutes).** Do a real investigate. "My intellect is 4. This location's shroud is 2. I can add cards from my hand with this icon to boost it. Now I pull a token." Pull it, apply it, and say whether you passed. Then put it back in the bag. That single example teaches more than any explanation.

**7. One warning (thirty seconds).** "If a monster is engaged with you, almost any action except fighting or evading gives it a free attack. We call that an attack of opportunity." Say this before the first enemy appears, not after it hurts someone.

That is enough to start.

## What to leave out

Do not teach these up front. Explain each one the first time it appears:

- Keywords on enemies, such as hunter, retaliate, alert, or aloof.
- Peril and surge on encounter cards.
- Weaknesses. When someone draws one, read it together.
- Experience, trauma, and the campaign log. Explain them at the end of the scenario.
- Deckbuilding. Nobody needs it until after the first game.

## Running the first round

Play the first round with every hand face up, or with each player reading their cards aloud. Talk through each turn as a group. The rulebook's own tip for the opening turn is sound: play a useful asset or two, and look for clues, because the first act needs them.

Fantasy Flight's rulebook also asks players to stay in character when they talk, and not to name exact cards in their hands. That keeps one player from running everyone's turn, the classic co-op problem. For a first game, relax it: name your cards so people learn them. From the second game on, try the in-character style. Our article on [the quarterback problem](/articles/quarterback-problem-cooperative-games/) has more ways to keep every player in charge of their own turn.

## When the game ends

When a card sends you to a resolution, stop and read it from the campaign guide together. Then show the campaign log and explain experience and trauma in two sentences each. People remember those rules far better once they have earned something.
