Arkham Horror: The Card Game plays well alone. The box says 1 to 4 players, and the rules do not change for solo play. The game simply scales: clue values and many act and enemy values are printed per investigator, so one investigator faces a smaller version of the same scenario.

There are two ways to play by yourself, and they feel quite different.

## True solo: one investigator

You play one investigator and one deck. You draw one encounter card each mythos phase, and every clue value with the per-investigator icon is multiplied by one.

**What gets harder.** You must do every job: find clues, deal with enemies, and pass treacheries. No one can commit a card to help your test. And if you are defeated, every investigator has been defeated, so the scenario ends right there with the "no resolution" outcome.

**What gets easier.** Planning is simple, and setup is fast. Each round you face one encounter card instead of several.

**Who to play.** The 2026 rulebook recommends Joe Diamond for solo. His skills (willpower 2, intellect 4, combat 4, agility 2) cover both clues and fights, and his ability keeps cards flowing while he investigates. Isabelle Barnes is a good second pick: no skill below 2 and a deck of skills she can reuse.

Investigators with one great skill and three low ones struggle alone. Dexter Drake can work once his spells are out, but his slow start is riskier with no teammate to cover it.

## Two-handed: two investigators

You run two investigators as if you were two players. Each gets their own deck, hand, resources, and turn. Two encounter cards come out each mythos phase, and per-investigator values double.

**Why many solo players prefer it.** You can split the jobs, the way a two-player table does: one fighter, one clue-finder. Each investigator can commit one card to the other's test if they share a location. And if one is defeated, the other can still reach a resolution.

**What it costs.** It takes longer and asks you to manage two hands. Keep the hands physically separate, and do not shuffle cards between them in your head; the rules treat them as two people.

**Good pairs from the core set.** Joe Diamond and Daniela Reyes are the cleanest split: clues and fighting. Trish Scarborough and Daniela also work, with Trish evading her way to clues. Dexter Drake pairs well with anyone who can protect him while he sets up.

## Rules to keep straight

- **Turn order is yours to choose.** The investigators pick who takes the next turn, so with two-handed play you choose each round.
- **The lead investigator** settles ties. With two-handed play, you are both, but pick one token for the lead.
- **Encounter draws are in player order,** starting with the lead.
- **The per-investigator count is fixed at setup.** If one of your two investigators is defeated or resigns, values stay doubled.
- **Hidden information.** The rulebook encourages players not to name exact cards in hand. Solo, this does not matter; you know everything. That is part of why two-handed can be easier than two real players.

## Tuning the challenge

Solo players often adjust difficulty more than groups do. The Arkham Grimoire's optional Boons and Ultimatums make that easy: for example, Boon of Hades gives each investigator 2 extra starting resources, and Ultimatum of Dread makes you play the mythos phase in the first round. The [standalone and difficulty lesson](/academy/arkham-horror-lcg/arkham-horror-lcg-standalone-difficulty/) covers them.

## A solo routine

1. **Pick a style per campaign,** not per scenario. Switching from one investigator to two mid-campaign changes the math.
2. **Start on Standard.** Easy is for story; Standard is the game as designed.
3. **Say your plan aloud each round.** Talking through the turn catches the same mistakes a teammate would, especially attacks of opportunity.
4. **Keep a log.** A notebook with each scenario's result, experience, and what went wrong builds skill faster than replaying blind.
