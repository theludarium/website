You can learn Sushi Go! in five minutes and keep finding new things in it for years. This last lesson is about getting more games in: online when nobody is around, at public game nights, in a small tournament of your own, and as the game that starts every evening.

## Play online

[Board Game Arena](https://boardgamearena.com/gamepanel?game=sushigo) has Sushi Go! in your browser, and it also has [Sushi Go Party!](https://boardgamearena.com/gamepanel?game=sushigoparty).

Online play is the fastest way to practice the skills from Levels 2 and 3. The game handles the scoring, so your attention goes to the picks. Try these drills:

- **One type per game.** Track only squid, or only pudding, for a whole game, as in lesson 11.
- **Name your pass.** Before every pick, say what your left neighbor wants most. Then check what they take.
- **Score your sets.** After each game, count the sets you started and the sets you finished. Aim to finish more of what you start.

## Official organized play

Gamewright does not list an official Sushi Go! tournament series or organized-play program on its site, and Sushi Go! has no event at the World Boardgaming Championships as of this writing.

That is fine. A light game like this lives at home tables, in classrooms, and at public game nights more than in tournament halls.

## Find a public game night

Sushi Go! is one of the easiest games to bring to an open game night: one small tin, a five-minute teach, and room for up to five, or eight with Party.

- **Your local game store.** Many run open game nights. Gamewright's [store locator](https://gamewright.com/wheretobuy/) lists shops near you that carry its games.
- **Libraries, cafes, and community centers.** Our guide to [finding a board game night near you](/articles/board-game-night-near-me/) covers where to look and what to expect.
- **Indianapolis.** Our [guide to public game nights in Indianapolis](/articles/indianapolis-game-nights/) lists where to play now. And join the [Indianapolis list](/lounges/indianapolis/) for first word on local learn-to-play nights as The Ludarium grows.

## Run a Sushi Go! tournament at home

You do not need an official event to crown a champion. Sushi Go! is short enough to play several games in an evening.

1. **Set up tables of three to five,** each with its own copy and a scorekeeper.
2. **Play one full game at each table.** Use the official rules, including the pudding tiebreaker.
3. **Give tournament points by finish:** for example, 3 for first, 2 for second, 1 for third.
4. **Reseat and play again.** After two or three games, the highest total wins. If you have enough players, finish with a final table of the leaders.

A few tips that keep it fair:

- **Agree on rulings up front,** like whether chopsticks can take a wasabi and a nigiri on the same turn.
- **Rotate seats** so nobody sits to the left of the strongest player every game.
- **Try pass both ways** for a final, since it spreads the blocking around.

For more on running events like this, see our guide to [running a board game tournament](/articles/how-to-run-a-board-game-tournament/).

## Make it your opener

Sushi Go! works best as the first game of an evening. It is short, everyone plays at once, and nobody is knocked out. It tells you who is in the mood for strategy and who just wants to laugh. Our pieces on [hosting a game night](/articles/how-to-host-a-game-night/) and [gateway board games](/articles/gateway-board-games/) cover how to build a whole evening from an opener like this.

## Keep teaching

The best thing you can do with a course like this is pass it on. Teach a friend, a grandparent, a coworker. Seat them to the left of a patient player. Score the first round together. Let them win a maki race.

## What you can do now

Look back at the four levels. You can teach and score a full game, value every card, time wasabi and chopsticks, read the hands coming your way, play the set and maki odds, win the pudding race, track every card in a round, and adjust for every player count and for Sushi Go Party!. That is a lot of depth for a game in a small tin.

The game is the reason. The people around the table are the point.
