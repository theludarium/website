Sushi Go! is listed for 2 to 5 players, and it changes more across that range than its five-minute teach suggests. Hand size, how quickly hands return, how many cards are in each round, and how fierce the competition is all move with the player count. This lesson goes count by count, then covers the two official variants printed in the rules.

## What changes with the count

| Players | Hand size | A hand returns after | Cards left in the deck after 3 rounds |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 10 | 2 turns | 48 |
| 3 | 9 | 3 turns | 27 |
| 4 | 8 | 4 turns | 12 |
| 5 | 7 | 5 turns | 3 |

The last column matters for planning. At five players, almost the whole deck is used, so counting cards over the game is nearly exact. At two players, close to half the deck never comes out, so any card might simply be missing.

## Two players

With two, you and your opponent swap hands every turn. You see both hands from turn two on, and every pick is revealed, so the game is close to full information.

What changes:

- **Pudding has no penalty.** Only most pudding scores, so pudding is a 6-point race for one prize.
- **Maki is a small race.** First scores 6 and second scores 3, a 3-point gap between the two of you.
- **Sets are scarce.** Only about 2.6 sashimi are dealt in a round of 20 cards, so a sashimi set needs nearly all of them.
- **Blocking is direct.** Every card you leave goes straight to your only opponent.

## The official two-player variant

The rules include a variant credited to Peter Kinsley that adds a third "dummy" player controlled by each of you in turn:

1. Deal three hands of 9 cards, as for three players.
2. Put the dummy's hand face down between you as a draw pile.
3. One player controls the dummy first. On your turn in control, draw the top card of the dummy pile into your hand, then choose one card for yourself and one for the dummy. Your opponent picks one card as usual.
4. Reveal, then swap hands, leaving the dummy pile in place.
5. Your opponent now controls the dummy the same way. Keep alternating until all cards are played.
6. Play three rounds and score as usual.

The dummy scores like a player, which brings back the pudding penalty and a real second place in maki. It also gives each of you a way to dump cards the other wants: what you give the dummy is gone.

## Three players

Three is a sweet spot for planning. Hands of 9 come back every three turns, so you see each hand three times in a round. Sets are reasonably available, and maki and pudding races have enough players to matter but few enough to read.

## Four players

The most common count at many tables. Hands of 8 return on turn five with four cards left. Competition for sets is real, and maki second place is often won cheaply because one player runs away with first.

## Five players

Hands of only 7 return on turn six with two cards left, so you get one real look at most hands and a last small choice from your first. Expect fewer finished sets, more shared ties in maki, and tight pudding counts. Favor sure points: nigiri, second tempura, dumplings that are already started.

## Variant: pass both ways

From the rules: in rounds one and three, pass hands to the left; in round two, pass to the right.

This changes who feeds you. In round two, your old left neighbor now passes to you, and the player you have been watching all game becomes the one who sees your leftovers. It spreads the blocking around, which helps when one seat at the table is a much stronger player than the rest.

Tips for playing it:

- **Re-check your neighbors in round two.** The player who wants your cards is now on your right.
- **Pudding plans stay the same.** Pudding is decided over three rounds regardless of direction.
- **Teach it as a house rule.** Say it at the start, so nobody is surprised when round two begins.

## Choosing a count

For learning, three or four players gives the clearest view of how drafting works. For two players who want a sharper game, try the dummy variant. For a crowd of six or more, the original Sushi Go! tops out at five, so look at Sushi Go Party!, covered in lesson 12.
