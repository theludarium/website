Sushi Go! teaches in five minutes, so most tables start playing before every odd case has come up. Your first three games are where habits form. Some mistakes are rules slips nobody catches because the game moves so fast. Others are legal picks that quietly cost points. This lesson covers both, checked against the official Gamewright rules.

## Rules slips

**Discarding pudding between rounds.** Pudding is the one card you keep. At the end of rounds one and two, everything else goes to the discard pile, but pudding stays in front of you until the game ends. A table that throws pudding away has quietly removed a 12-point swing from the game.

**Scoring maki by cards instead of icons.** Maki cards show one, two, or three rolls at the top. You compare total icons, not the number of maki cards. One 3-roll card beats two 1-roll cards.

**Giving out second place for maki after a tie for first.** If two or more players tie for the most maki icons, they split the 6 points, rounding down, and no one scores second place that round.

**Putting a nigiri on a wasabi taken later.** Wasabi only works on nigiri you take after it. The rules say the nigiri goes on the wasabi when you "already have" one in front of you. A salmon you took two turns ago stays a 2-point salmon.

**Stacking two nigiri on one wasabi.** Each wasabi holds one nigiri. A second nigiri needs a second wasabi.

**Keeping the chopsticks.** After you use chopsticks to take two cards, the chopsticks card goes back into your hand before you pass. It travels on, and someone else may grab it.

**Using two chopsticks in one turn.** You can hold more than one, but you can use only one per turn.

**Forgetting the last card.** When one card is passed to you, it is yours. Put it face up and score it. Sometimes it is a gift.

**Pudding with two players.** With two, no one loses points for having fewer puddings. Only the most pudding scores.

## Picking habits that cost points

**Starting sets you cannot finish.** A lone tempura scores nothing. So do one or two sashimi. New players love starting sets because a pair of tempura looks like 5 points. It is only 5 points if the second one reaches you. Before you start a set, count how many hands are left to pass through you.

**Taking the biggest number on the card.** A sashimi card says 10, but it is 10 for three cards. Per card, a finished sashimi set is worth about 3.3 points, and an unfinished one is worth zero. A plain squid nigiri is worth 3 points the moment you take it. Lesson 4 lays out the numbers.

**Ignoring what you pass.** Every card you leave in a hand goes to your neighbor on the left. If they have one tempura and you pass a tempura, you just gave them 5 points. Sometimes the best pick is the card that hurts them most.

**Chasing maki every round.** Maki is a race with only two prizes. If two players are already ahead, more maki earns you nothing. Lesson 8 covers when to race and when to quit.

**Forgetting pudding until round three.** Pudding is decided over the whole game. A player with zero pudding going into the last round often cannot catch up, and fewest pudding costs 6 points with three or more players.

**Taking chopsticks late.** Chopsticks score nothing by themselves. They pay off only on a later turn, when a hand holds two cards you want. Taken with two cards left in the round, they almost never get used.

## A three-game plan

Use your first three games to build one habit at a time.

1. **Game one: score right.** Score together, slowly, card type by card type. Keep pudding out between rounds. Count maki icons out loud.
2. **Game two: finish what you start.** Start a tempura or sashimi set only when enough turns remain for the rest to come around. Take note of every set you leave unfinished.
3. **Game three: watch what you pass.** Before each pick, look at your left neighbor's cards. Ask what they want, and whether you can afford to take it from them.

## Why these mistakes matter

Single picks swing big totals in Sushi Go!. A finished sashimi set is 10 points and an unfinished one is zero. The pudding race moves 12 points between the top and the bottom with three or more players. One slip in either place can decide a game. Fixing the rules slips makes the game fair. Fixing the habits makes you hard to beat.
