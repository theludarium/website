Welcome to Level 5, the top of the ladder. Cube has no official championship to aim for. The highest levels of the hobby are about craft instead: drafting with real skill, and building a Cube that people remember.

At Level 4 you learned to build, balance, curate, and host. This lesson is about the step after that: giving your Cube a character of its own.

## Why identity matters

Many first Cubes are lists of the strongest cards the owner can afford. They draft well, and they feel like every other Cube. Drafters enjoy them, but rarely talk about them.

A Cube with identity is different. Drafters say "that Cube" instead of "a Cube." They know its quirks, its best decks, the card everyone fights over. They come back for it. Our piece on [Cube identity](/articles/cube-identity/) explores where that character comes from.

## Start with purpose

Every strong identity starts with a clear answer to "what is this Cube for?" Our piece [What Is Your Cube For?](/articles/what-is-your-cube-for/) asks the question directly. Some common answers:

- **Teaching:** a Cube where new players can learn Magic, with simpler cards and clear decks.
- **Nostalgia:** cards from the era your group started playing. Our piece on [nostalgia in Cube](/articles/nostalgia-in-cube/) makes the case.
- **One set, forever:** a [set Cube](/articles/set-cube/) that keeps a favorite draft format alive.
- **A design idea:** a Cube built around a mechanic or a single type of card.

Once you know the purpose, every card decision gets easier. A card that does not serve the purpose goes, even if it is strong.

## Themes and restrictions

Some of the most memorable Cubes use a restriction: a rule that every card must follow. Commons only. Only cards from before a certain year. Only creatures of certain types. The restriction forces creative choices and gives drafters a story.

Restrictions come with costs. A commons-only Cube may lack sweepers. A creature-type Cube may struggle for removal. Name the cost and design around it. Our guides to [themed Cube ideas](/articles/themed-cube-ideas/) and the [themed Cube primer](/articles/themed-cube-primer/) explain how, and [five famous themed Cubes](/articles/famous-themed-cubes/) shows what restrictions have produced.

## Packages and modules

A more advanced tool is the module: a small group of cards that together support one deck or effect, which you can swap in or out as a unit. One night the Cube has a graveyard package; next month it has an artifact package instead.

Modules keep a Cube fresh for regulars without a full redesign. They also make curation cleaner, since you test a whole idea at once. Our piece on [modular Cube design](/articles/modular-cube-design/) shows how to size a core and build modules that fit it.

## Borrow from the best

Many of the best ideas in Cube design come from other owners. Our piece on [ideas to borrow from great Cubes](/articles/ideas-to-borrow-from-great-cubes/) collects ten of them, and [Lucky Paper's approach](/articles/lucky-paper-cube-design/) shows how one group turned taste into method. Borrowing an idea is not copying a Cube. It is how a design community works.

## Teach the identity

A Cube's identity only works if drafters can see it. Three tools help:

- **A primer** that explains the purpose, the theme, and the decks. Our guide on [how to write a Cube primer](/articles/how-to-write-a-cube-primer/) covers the structure.
- **Signposts** that point to the decks you built for. The [design lesson](/academy/cube-draft/cube-design-color-balance/) covers them.
- **A short pitch** before each draft for newcomers: one sentence about what makes this Cube different.

## Keep the story going

Identity grows over time. The card that won the most memorable game. The deck someone drafted for the first time. The changes your group argued about. Share those moments in your changelog and your group chat. They are part of the Cube now.
