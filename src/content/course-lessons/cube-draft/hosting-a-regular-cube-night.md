One great draft night is an event. A night that happens every week, or every month, is a community. The difference is mostly logistics: people need to know when to come, how the evening runs, and that they will play real matches whether they are new or have drafted a hundred times.

The [teach lesson](/academy/cube-draft/how-to-teach-cube-draft/) covered running one night. This lesson covers running them again and again. Our host's guide to [running a Cube draft](/articles/how-to-run-a-cube-draft/) has the full checklist for a single evening.

## A start time that means it

A draft cannot begin until every seat is filled. The most useful rule for a regular night is a published start time and a clear cutoff. Something like: "We seat at 6:30 and draft at 6:45. If you are not here by 6:45, you can join for the next draft."

It feels strict once. After that, people arrive on time, and the night ends at a reasonable hour.

## Rounds and timing

The official Magic Tournament Rules set the time limit for each match at no less than 40 minutes, with 50 minutes recommended for limited events like drafts. They also allow about 25 minutes to build and register a draft deck.

For a casual night, those numbers work well:

- **Deckbuilding:** 20 to 25 minutes.
- **Rounds:** 50 minutes each, best of three games.
- **Two or three rounds,** depending on how late your group can stay.

Decide what happens when time runs out. In tournaments, a match unfinished at time goes to a few extra turns and can end in a draw. Many casual groups simply let the current game finish. Either works if you say it beforehand.

## Pairing methods

**Random each round.** Draw names, and avoid repeat matches. No bookkeeping, but strong decks may never meet.

**Swiss.** Round one is random. After that, players with the same record play each other. In an eight-player pod with no draws, three Swiss rounds leave exactly one player undefeated. It finds the best decks, but it can feel more like a tournament.

**A blend.** Random round one, then pair by record without announcing standings until the end. It keeps the good matches of Swiss without a scoreboard in the room.

For scoring, the tournament rules give 3 points for a match win, 1 for a draw, and 0 for a loss. A bye counts as a win, 3 points.

## Byes and odd numbers

With an odd number of players, someone sits out each round. Under the tournament rules, that player gets a bye, which counts as a win. Many casual groups prefer to avoid byes altogether:

- Let the extra player play a three-player free-for-all, or a casual match against whoever finishes first.
- Run a second, smaller draft for the extra players, such as a two-player Winston draft. The [small tables lesson](/academy/cube-draft/cube-draft-small-tables/) covers the formats.

## Tiebreakers, if you want them

If you want a single winner from a Swiss night, use the tournament tiebreakers in order: match points, then opponents' match-win percentage, then game-win percentage, then opponents' game-win percentage. Each opponent's match-win percentage counts as at least 33%, so one bad night does not sink the people who played them.

Most casual groups never need this. Calling it a shared win is also fine.

## Keep one simple record

A little record keeping helps a group grow:

- **Who came,** so you know your regulars and can reach them.
- **Which decks went well,** for your [curation notes](/academy/cube-draft/cube-curation-playtesting/).
- **One story from the night,** posted in a group chat the next day.

Skip lifetime win rates and public rankings. They tend to make the night more competitive than most groups want.

## Make newcomers welcome

A regular night lives or dies on new people coming back. A few habits help:

- **Seat a newcomer next to a patient regular,** not next to another newcomer.
- **Pair them thoughtfully in round one,** with someone happy to explain as they play.
- **Ask them at the end** what their best card was, and invite them to the next one.

Our guides to [Cube house rules](/articles/cube-house-rules/) and [house rules examples](/articles/cube-house-rules-examples/) show how other groups write all this down.

## Where to host

Living rooms work for one pod. As a group grows, local game stores often have tables for community play. Ask what they need from you, and support the store that hosts you.
