Welcome to Level 3. At Level 2 you learned to win combat and trade cards for time. Now the game gets precise. Most advanced Magic plays come down to timing: casting the right instant at the exact moment it does the most. That moment is defined by two rules, the stack and priority.

Everything here is checked against the Comprehensive Rules effective September 25, 2026, mostly rules 117 (priority), 405 (the stack), 603 (triggered abilities), and 608 (resolving).

## Priority: who may act now

**Priority** is the right to cast a spell or activate an ability. Only the player with priority can do either.

- The active player, whose turn it is, gets priority first in most steps and phases.
- When a player casts a spell or activates an ability, they get priority again afterward.
- When a player passes, the next player in turn order gets priority.
- When **all players pass in a row** without doing anything, the top item on the stack resolves. If the stack is empty, the step or phase ends.

That last rule is the heart of it. A spell does not resolve when you cast it. It resolves when everyone has declined to respond.

## The stack: last in, first out

When you cast a spell, it goes on the stack. If your opponent casts an instant in response, it goes on top. Responses can pile up. When everyone passes, the top item resolves first. Then the player whose turn it is gets priority again, and everyone gets a fresh chance to respond to whatever is now on top.

A simple example. You attack with a 3/3. Your opponent blocks with a 2/2 and casts a spell giving it +2/+2. In response, you cast a removal spell on the 2/2. Your removal resolves first, destroying the blocker. Their pump spell then tries to resolve, finds its target gone, and does nothing. Your 3/3 stays blocked, so it deals no damage unless it has trample.

## When a spell does nothing

When a spell or ability tries to resolve, it checks its targets. If **every** target is now illegal, it does not resolve at all. Players call this fizzling. If some targets are still legal, it resolves and does as much as it can to those.

This is why responding with removal to an Aura or a pump spell is so strong. You answer two cards with one.

## What does not use the stack

Some things happen immediately, and nobody can respond to them:

- **Playing a land.** It is a special action.
- **Mana abilities.** Tapping a land for mana resolves on the spot.
- **State-based actions.** A creature with lethal damage is destroyed, a player at 0 life loses, and the legend rule applies, all checked automatically every time a player would get priority.
- **Turn-based actions** like untapping and drawing for the turn.

The combat damage itself also does not use the stack. Once blockers are declared and everyone passes in the declare blockers step, damage happens. Your last chance for a trick is before that.

## Triggered abilities

A triggered ability, the "when," "whenever," and "at" kind, goes on the stack the next time a player would get priority after it triggers. It can be responded to like a spell.

If several abilities trigger at once, the active player puts all of theirs on the stack first, in any order they choose, then the next player. Because the last one in resolves first, the non-active player's triggers resolve before the active player's. If you control two triggers at once, think about which order you want them to resolve, and put them on the stack in reverse.

## Where the windows are

You get priority in every step except untap and, usually, cleanup. The most useful windows to know:

- **Your opponent's upkeep.** Tapping their creature here stops it from attacking this turn.
- **Beginning of combat.** The last moment to tap a would-be attacker.
- **After attackers are declared.** You know what is coming. Removal here takes an attacker out of combat.
- **After blockers are declared.** The classic window for combat tricks.
- **Your opponent's end step.** Casting an instant here lets you untap with your mana fresh and gives them the least information during their turn.

## Shortcuts at the table

In practice, nobody announces every priority pass. Players use shortcuts. When you say "go" or "your turn," the tournament rules treat your opponent as acting in your end step. When you cast a spell, you are assumed to pass priority unless you say you are keeping it. The [tournament rules lesson](/academy/magic/magic-tournament-rules/) at Level 4 covers these shortcuts in detail.

At a casual table, the kindest habit is to pause after casting something and let your opponent respond. Rushing through the stack is how arguments start.

Once these rules click, play a few games where you narrate every response. It feels slow for a game or two. After that, the stack becomes something you use instead of something you fear.
