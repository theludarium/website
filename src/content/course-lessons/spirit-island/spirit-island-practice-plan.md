Cooperative games have no rating and no ladder to climb unless you build one. The rulebook gives you the pieces: Difficulty numbers, adversary levels, and a scoring system made to compare your group's games. This lesson turns them into a plan.

## Keep a log

A paper notebook in the box works. So does a shared spreadsheet or note on everyone's phone. For each game, record:

- **Date and players.**
- **Spirits played.**
- **Adversary and level, scenario, and map.** Balanced or thematic.
- **Difficulty,** from the rulebook's chart, added up.
- **Result and score,** using the rulebook's scoring.
- **What ended it.** For a win: which Terror Level, and on which turn. For a loss: the Invader Deck, the Blight Card, or a Spirit with no Presence.

That last line is the one that teaches. After ten games, the pattern in it tells you what your group needs to practice.

## Read your losses

Each kind of loss points to a different fix:

- **The Invader Deck ran out.** You were too slow. Work on the [endgame lesson](/academy/spirit-island/spirit-island-endgame/): set a target turn earlier, and lean on Fear to reach an easier victory condition.
- **The Blight Card ran out.** Too many Ravages got through. Work on the [Blight lesson](/academy/spirit-island/spirit-island-blight-management/): count the budget every turn, clean lands before a second Blight, and steer cascades.
- **A Spirit lost all its Presence.** That Spirit spread too thin near the Ravages, or the team left it alone. Review where Presence goes in the [growth lesson](/academy/spirit-island/spirit-island-growth-and-energy/), and check the lands where each Spirit is exposed.
- **An adversary's extra loss condition.** Learn its rule cold. England's capital, for example, means counting buildings in one land every turn.

## The climbing rule

The rulebook's guidance is simple: raise Difficulty by 1 at a time, or by 2 if you are winning so easily that the Blight Card never flips. Turn that into a group rule:

1. **Win twice at a Difficulty** before you move up.
2. **Move up by 1.** Move up by 2 only if neither win flipped the Blight Card.
3. **Lose twice in a row,** and drop back by 1 for a game.

The rule keeps the challenge close to the edge of what your table can do, which is where games are most fun and you learn the most.

## A Spirit and adversary grid

Draw a grid: Spirits down the side, adversaries across the top. Each time a Spirit wins against an adversary, write the highest level it has beaten. The empty and low squares show you where your group is weakest.

The rulebook's own notes are a good place to start testing:

- Spirits that need time to develop, against Brandenburg-Prussia.
- Shadows Flicker Like Flame, and other Explorer-focused Spirits, against England.
- Spirits that cannot prevent Ravages, against Sweden.

A group that has filled the grid at level 6 has learned every core adversary from every angle.

## Keep the table happy

A practice plan should never make the game feel like homework. Two rulebook habits protect the fun:

- **Agree on pace.** Very fast and very careful players at one table can frustrate each other. Talk about it before you start, and use a timer for the Spirit Phase if your group wants one.
- **Do not rewind.** A rules mistake found mid-game gets noted and fixed going forward. Write it in the log too, so the next game starts right.

Celebrate milestones. A first win against an adversary, a first level 6, a first thematic win: these are the moments a play group remembers.

## The long game

The flywheel of a good Spirit Island group is simple. Play often, write it down, climb a step, and bring in new players at the bottom of the ladder while the veterans push the top. Our guide on [how to start a play group](/articles/how-to-start-a-play-group/) covers finding and keeping the people. The last lesson covers playing together once they are at the table.
