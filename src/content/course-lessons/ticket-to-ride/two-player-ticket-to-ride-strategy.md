Two-player Ticket to Ride is one of the most popular ways to play, and it is not the same game as a full table. Strategies that win at four or five players can lose at two, and a few moves that are rarely right in a crowd become strong when there is only one rival. This lesson is about those differences.

## The board is roomier than it looks

With 2 or 3 players, only one route of each double pair can be claimed. That closes 22 routes, about 53 train spaces of the board's 309, leaving about 256. <!-- VERIFY: space counts use the route list from the USA map lesson. -->

Now compare how much of the board the players can fill:

| Players | Trains in play | Share of claimable spaces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 90 | about 35% |
| 3 | 135 | about 53% |
| 4 | 180 | about 58% |
| 5 | 225 | about 73% |

At two players, two thirds of the board stays empty at the end. Detours exist almost everywhere. Long routes you would never get at five are often still there in the midgame. And your opponent is usually building somewhere else entirely.

That changes the main question. At five, it is "will my route survive?" At two, it is "how do I score more than one other person in the turns we have?"

## Every point is margin

In a multiplayer game, hurting one rival helps everyone else at the table too. At two, there is nobody else. A point you deny your opponent is worth exactly as much as a point you score.

That has three effects.

**The longest path bonus is a 20-point swing.** If you take it, you are 10 up. If they take it, you are 10 down. Tied, you both score it. At two players, watch both paths closely from the midgame on, and spend a short route to win or tie it when the gap is small.

**Blocking pays more often,** but the roomy board means most blocks only force a short detour. Block when your opponent's path runs into a true dead end, such as one of the [cities with only two or three ways in](/academy/ticket-to-ride/ticket-to-ride-usa-map/), and when the route also helps you. A block that costs you a turn and buys a one-train detour is a loss.

**Tie-breakers matter.** If you tie, the player with more completed tickets wins. That is one more reason to finish small tickets.

## Draw more tickets

With 33 tickets and only 8 dealt at the start, 25 unseen tickets sit on top of the deck. Your railroad has room to grow, and your rival takes fewer of the cities you need. Both make mid-game ticket draws better at two than at any other count.

The [ticket drawing lesson](/academy/ticket-to-ride/ticket-to-ride-drawing-tickets/) still applies: draw when your railroad is long and the end is not near. At two, that window is usually wider, and the player who finishes more tickets often wins.

## Read one rival completely

At five players, you can track a few opponents loosely. At two, you can track one closely.

- **Every face-up card they take.** Three blacks in a row means a black route soon. Look at the black routes near their trains.
- **Their hand size.** A big hand means a long route, or several claims in a row, is coming.
- **Their train count.** You know exactly how many turns the game can last.
- **Their tickets.** Watch where their railroad points and which cities it reaches. By the midgame, you can often name their tickets.

If you know their next route and it sits on your path too, take it first.

## Timing the end

At two players, the end is a duel of tempo. When either player drops to 2 or fewer trains, you each get one more turn.

- **If you are ahead on known points,** and your tickets are done, race to the end. Spend your trains fast on long routes. Each long route is efficient points, and it shortens the time your opponent has to finish their tickets.
- **If you are behind,** slow down only if you have something to build. Long routes and finished tickets are how you catch up.
- **Count both supplies every turn** from about 15 trains down. A single 6-train claim can end the game sooner than expected.

## Long routes go further at two

With less competition for space, the long routes in the West and South can still be open late. A 6-train route scores 15 points for one claim. At two, a plan built around a few of them, plus tickets they help finish, is strong. Level 3's last lesson looks at that plan in depth.
