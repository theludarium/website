New players watch the animals. That makes sense: the tokens are the bright, friendly part of the game, and the scoring cards are all about them. But in the rulebook's three-player scoring example, the player with the most wildlife points finished second. Lee scored 67 from wildlife and lost by one point to Jade, who scored 59. The difference was the tiles.

This lesson is about the other half of your score: habitat corridors.

## How a corridor forms

Each habitat tile shows one or two of the five habitats: mountains, forests, prairies, wetlands, and rivers. Two tiles join the same corridor when they share at least one matching edge. A forest edge against a forest edge connects. A forest edge against a river edge does not.

At the end, each habitat scores 1 point per tile in your **largest** corridor of it. Smaller groups of the same habitat score nothing. A big forest of 7 and a stray forest of 3 score 7.

That one rule shapes everything. Scattered habitats waste tiles. Connected habitats earn a point per tile, then compete for the bonuses.

## The bonuses are bigger than they look

With three or four players, the largest corridor of each habitat earns 3 bonus points and the second largest 1. With two players, the largest earns 2. That is up to 15 bonus points across five habitats at three or four players.

In the rulebook example, Jade won 7 bonus points: a 3, a 2 from a tie, two 1s, and a 0. Lee won 4. Lee led on wildlife by 8, and Jade won back 7 of those points in habitats: 4 from bigger corridors and 3 from bonuses. Her 2 unspent nature tokens did the rest.

You do not need to win every habitat. You need to win the ones you chose, and keep a few cheap 1s.

## Two-habitat tiles do double work

Most tiles show two habitats split across the hex. Placed well, one tile can grow two corridors at once: 2 points before any bonus. Placed badly, it grows neither.

When you look at a two-habitat tile in the display, ask which of its edges can touch your corridors. The only placement rules are that a new tile touches your environment and does not sit on another tile, so turn it before you place it: a tile that looks wrong often fits once you rotate it.

Single-habitat tiles are the keystones. They hold only one animal, but every one of their six edges is the same habitat, so they connect in every direction. A keystone in the middle of a corridor is easy to grow around.

## Choose your corridors early

You get twenty tiles. Spread them across five habitats and each corridor averages four tiles, which rarely wins anything. Pour them into two or three and those corridors can reach eight or ten.

A practical way to choose:

1. **Start from your starter tile.** Its three hexes already show three habitats. Pick the two that have the most open edges facing outward.
2. **Match your animals.** Elk and bears often want room to spread. A corridor that grows in the direction your animal shapes need serves both at once.
3. **Watch the table.** If a neighbor already has six wetlands by turn eight, your four wetlands are racing for second place at best.

## Grow outward, not inward

A corridor grows from its edges. If you surround your forest with prairie and river, the only way to grow it is to break through with a tile that has a forest edge in exactly the right place. Leave each main corridor at least two open sides where new tiles can attach.

The same idea helps your animals. An environment with open edges keeps giving you good places to put a tile and good places to put a token.

## When to concede a habitat

Not every bonus is worth chasing. Concede a habitat when:

- a rival leads it by three or more tiles and is still growing it,
- the tiles you would need keep coming with tokens you cannot use, or
- chasing it means breaking your main animal shapes.

Conceding does not mean ignoring. A habitat you are not chasing can still pick up a cheap second-place point at three or four players. Keep half an eye on it.

## A five-turn corridor check

At turns 5, 10, and 15, count your two or three main corridors and glance at every rival's. Then ask:

- Which habitat am I leading, and by how much?
- Which one am I about to lose?
- Which tile in the display would change a bonus?

That count takes thirty seconds, and it turns tiles from packaging into points. The [endgame lesson](/academy/cascadia/cascadia-scoring/) at Level 2 takes the same count into the last five turns.
