The [openings lesson](/academy/cascadia/cascadia-strategy-openings/) read all twenty cards. Each one asks for a shape. This lesson is about the ground those shapes stand on: the tiles. Strong players often place a tile three or four turns before the token that will sit on it, because the rules let a token land on any open tile in your environment.

## The rule that makes this work

When you place your token, it may go on the tile you just took **or on any other tile** in your environment, as long as that tile shows the animal and has no token yet. Each tile holds one token for the whole game.

So a tile is not just a tile. It is a set of future options. A tile showing salmon, bear, and hawk is three possible landing spots until something lands on it, then it is none.

## Count your landing spots

A landing spot is an open tile that shows a given animal. For each of your main animals, know roughly how many you have.

- **Two or more for each main animal:** healthy. Whatever token comes, you have a good place for it.
- **One:** fragile. If that tile is in the wrong place for your shape, the next token of that animal has nowhere useful to go.
- **None:** the token goes back in the bag, and your turn produces only a tile.

Counting spots also tells you which tiles you need. If your bears have plenty of spots but your elk have none, the right pick is often an elk tile with a token you do not even want, because it sets up three future turns.

## Place for the shape, not just the habitat

Each card's shape has a geometry. Your tiles should match it before the tokens arrive.

- **Lines** (Elk A): lay elk tiles along one of the three straight directions on the hex grid. Leave both ends open.
- **Runs** (Salmon A and B): lay salmon tiles in a winding chain, with no salmon tile touching the chain from the side, unless you will fill that side with something else.
- **Separated groups** (Bears A and B): leave a gap of one tile between the spots for each group. A bear tile in that gap is a hazard if a stray bear ever lands there.
- **Spread out** (Hawks A and B): hawk tiles far apart, at different corners of your environment.
- **Crowds** (Foxes A and Salmon D): a tile for your fox in the middle, with a ring of tiles for other animals around it.

When a tile serves both a corridor and a shape, it is a strong pick. When it serves neither, take it only if its token is worth the turn.

## Protect your flexible tiles

Tiles that show two or three animals are your most valuable landing spots, because they can serve whichever token comes. Use them last.

When you have a choice of where to put a token, put it on the tile with the **fewest** other options you care about. A keystone that shows only elk is the natural home for an elk; it also pays a nature token. A three-animal tile that could also hold your next bear should stay open.

## Avoid dead tiles

A dead tile is a tile that can never hold a useful token. It happens when every animal it shows would break a shape you are building, or belongs to an animal you have dropped.

Dead tiles are not wasted if they grow a corridor. But each one costs you the token that could have come with a better tile. A few per game is normal. If a third of your environment is dead by the end, your tiles and tokens were pulling in different directions.

## Think about the edges of your environment

Your environment can grow in any direction, and tiles never move. That makes the edges valuable.

- **Keep each main shape near an open edge** so it can keep growing.
- **Do not wall in a shape early.** An elk line with tiles on both ends that cannot hold elk is finished, whatever its length.
- **Leave a little space between shapes** that must not touch, such as two bear groups or two salmon runs.

## A three-question habit

Before each pick, ask:

1. Which animal is running low on landing spots?
2. Which tile in the display fixes that and grows a corridor?
3. Where will this turn's token land, and does it use up a flexible tile I need later?

This habit makes every turn about two turns: the one you are taking and the one you are preparing. Planning a turn ahead is one of the clearest habits that separates steady high scores from lucky ones.
