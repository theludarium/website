Cascadia games are often close. In the rulebook's own three-player scoring example, the final scores are 98, 97, and 95. Games that tight are decided by a habitat bonus, a single nature token, or one token placed in the right spot on the last turn. The good news is that nothing is hidden. You can count all of it before the end.

## Where the points come from

The winning player in that rulebook example scored 59 points from wildlife, 37 from habitats and habitat bonuses, and 2 from unspent nature tokens. Two lessons hide in that line:

- **Wildlife is the biggest column**, but not by as much as new players think.
- **The habitat bonuses alone were worth 7 points** to that player, far more than the 1-point margin of victory.

## Habitat math

Each habitat scores 1 point per tile in your largest connected corridor of it. A tile with two habitats can grow two corridors at once, so the right two-habitat tile is worth 2 points before any bonus.

Then the bonuses, by player count:

| Players | Largest corridor | Second largest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solo | 2 points for each habitat with a corridor of 7 or more | none |
| Two | 2 points, or 1 each if tied | none |
| Three or four | 3 points | 1 point |

With three or four players, ties change the payout. If two players tie for largest, each gets 2 and nobody scores second. If three or four tie for largest, each gets 1. A tie for second scores nothing.

The rulebook's example shows all of this in one game. Mountains of 6, 5, and 6: the two players at 6 tie and take 2 each, and the 5 gets nothing. Forests of 4, 4, and 4: a three-way tie, 1 point each. Wetlands of 8, 7, and 6: the 8 takes 3 and the 7 takes 1.

So one more tile in a habitat can be worth 1 point, or it can move a bonus by 2 or 3 points. Before the last few turns, write down every player's largest corridor in each habitat. The tiles that change a bonus are your most valuable tiles.

## What a token is worth on its card

Tokens are worth what their card says, and the jump from one shape to the next is where endgame points live. A few numbers from the rulebook and the cards:

- **Elk A:** a line of two scores 5, a line of three scores 9. That third elk is worth 4 points.
- **Salmon A:** a run of four scores 12 in the rulebook's example, and the table keeps climbing up to a run of seven. Per Dire Wolf's [salmon spotlight](https://news.direwolfdigital.com/cascadia-animal-spotlight-chinook-salmon), six salmon in one run score 20, while two runs of three score 16.
- **Bears B:** a group of exactly three scores 10. The third bear is worth all 10; the first two are worth nothing until it arrives.
- **Hawks C:** each line of sight between two hawks is worth 3, and one new hawk can create more than one line.

Compare that with a habitat tile, usually worth 1 to 3 points. Late in the game, a token that completes a shape almost always beats a tile that only grows a corridor. When a pair does both, take it.

The flip side: shapes with no time left are worth what they score right now. A bear pair waiting for a third bear on card B is worth nothing if no bear will come.

## The last five turns

You know exactly how many turns remain, because everyone gets twenty. Use that.

- **Finish what can finish.** A shape that needs one more token is worth chasing. A shape that needs three more, with three turns left, usually is not.
- **Prepare landing spots.** Tokens can go on older tiles, so a tile placed two turns early can be the spot your final token lands on.
- **Watch the corridor leaders.** Each rival's last tiles may push them past you in a habitat. If one tile protects a 3-point bonus, it may be worth more than a token.
- **Decline when it helps.** If the best tile comes with a token you cannot use, take the pair, place the tile, and return the token to the bag.

## Nature tokens at the end

Each unspent nature token is 1 point, and the first tiebreaker. That sets a simple rule for the endgame: spend one only when it gains you at least 2 points, so you come out ahead.

On your final turn, look again. A nature token that lets you take the best tile with the best token often clears that bar easily. If a rival is close and has more nature tokens, they win ties, so you need to pass them, not match them.

## A last-turn checklist

1. Which token placement adds the most wildlife points?
2. Which tile grows a corridor into a bonus, or protects one?
3. Will spending a nature token gain at least 2 points?
4. Who could tie me, and how many nature tokens do they hold?
