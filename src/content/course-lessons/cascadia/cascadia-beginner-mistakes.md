Welcome to Level 1. Your first three games of Cascadia are where habits form. Some of them are rules slips, quietly played wrong because nobody checked. Others are legal choices that cost a lot of points. This lesson covers both, checked against the current rulebook.

## Rules slips to catch early

**Skipping the overpopulation check.** Look at the display before every pick. Four matching tokens are wiped automatically. Three matching tokens can be wiped if the active player wants.

**Wiping three tokens twice.** The optional wipe of three matching tokens happens at most once per turn. The automatic wipe of four can repeat as often as it comes up.

**Thinking tiles must match.** A new tile only has to touch your environment on at least one side. Matching edges are about scoring, not about legality.

**Putting the token only on the new tile.** The token can go on any tile in your environment that shows that animal and has no token yet.

**Placing a token where its animal is not shown.** The tile has to picture that animal. One token per tile, for the whole game.

**Forcing a bad token.** You never have to place the token you took. Return it to the bag instead.

**Missing a nature token.** You earn one when you place a token on a keystone tile showing that same animal. Keystone tiles show a single animal.

**Underusing nature tokens.** There is no limit per turn. Each one does one thing: take any tile with any token, or wipe and replace any number of tokens.

**Sliding the display.** In a multiplayer game, refill the gap and leave the other pairs where they are. Solo mode slides; the regular game does not.

**Counting every forest.** Only your largest connected group of each habitat scores. A group of five prairies and a separate group of two score 5, not 7.

## Card slips with the A cards

The scoring cards hide a few rules that new tables miss.

- **Bears:** on every bear card, a group must have exactly the number of bears the card shows, and two bear groups may not touch. On card A, three bears together are not a pair plus one. They are a group of three, and they score nothing.
- **Elk:** each elk scores only once, for one line. When lines cross, count it the way that scores the most.
- **Salmon:** in a run, no salmon may touch more than two other salmon, and no other salmon may touch the run. One salmon in the wrong spot can break a long run.
- **Hawks:** on card A, a hawk scores only if no other hawk is next to it. Two hawks side by side both stop counting.
- **Foxes:** on card A, other foxes count as a neighboring animal type. On cards B, C, and D they do not, so read the card before counting.

## The big strategic mistake: chasing all five animals

You get twenty turns, so at most twenty tokens. Most cards need several tokens in the right shape before they pay well. A player who takes whatever animal looks nice ends the game with a little of everything and a lot of half-finished shapes.

The fix is to commit. Before your first pick, choose two or three animals to build around. Take the others when they come free, on tiles you wanted anyway.

## Forgetting the habitats

New players watch the animals and treat tiles as packaging. But every tile is worth points in a corridor. In the rulebook's own scoring example, the winning player scored 59 from wildlife and 37 from habitats and habitat bonuses, out of 98. Ignore that column and you give away more than a third of a normal score.

## Scattering your corridors

Two medium forests score less than one big one, because only the largest counts. Early on, decide which two or three habitats you will grow large, and place tiles so their matching edges connect. The rest can stay small.

## Burning your flexible tiles

Many tiles show two or three animals. Each one is a future landing spot. If you place a hawk on a tile that could also hold the bear you need, you may run out of good spots for bears later. When you have a choice, put a token on the tile that offers the fewest other options you care about.

## Spending nature tokens too fast

Each unspent nature token is a point, and it also breaks ties. Spending one to get a slightly better pair can cost you more than it earns. Spend them when they win a big turn, such as finishing a shape worth several points, or when every pair in the display is bad for you.

## A quick checklist for game three

Before each pick, ask:

1. Which two or three animals am I building?
2. Does this tile grow a corridor I care about?
3. Is this the best spot for the token, or am I using up a tile I will need later?

That habit alone fixes most of what goes wrong in the first three games. If your group wants games with this same calm, low-conflict feel, our list of the [easiest board games to learn](/articles/easiest-board-games-to-learn/) has a few more.
