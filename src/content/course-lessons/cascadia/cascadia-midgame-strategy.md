Cascadia looks like five people doing five separate puzzles. In the opening, that is mostly true. By the midgame, around turns 7 to 14, the puzzles start pulling on the same four pairs in the middle of the table. This is where good players turn a pleasant solo puzzle into a quiet contest.

## Count your turns

You get exactly twenty. Every turn adds one tile and at most one token, so twenty turns means at most twenty animals across five cards. By turn 10, half your game is gone.

Around the halfway point, look at each shape you started and ask: can it still finish in the turns I have left? A bear trio on card B with two bears placed is a strong bet. An elk ring with two elk placed and nine turns to go probably is not. Move your remaining turns to the shapes that can close.

## Read the display like a shared hand

The display is not your hand. It is everyone's. After you pick, one fresh pair arrives, and then every other player takes a pair before you pick again. In a four-player game, three pairs leave before your next turn.

That gives two habits:

- **Do not plan on a pair staying.** If a pair is great for you now, it may be gone later. Take the best pair today.
- **Look at your neighbors before you pick.** Their environments are open on the table. If the next player has two bears waiting for a third on Bears B, the bear in the display is worth 10 points to them.

## When to hate-draft

Taking a pair mainly to keep it from someone else is called hate-drafting. In Cascadia it is legal, normal, and usually small. You can still place the tile for a corridor, and you can return the token to the bag if it does nothing for you.

Do it when the gap between your best pick and the denying pick is small, and the denial is big: the elk that closes a ring, a fourth salmon for a long run, or a keystone the leader needs. In a two-player game, every pick is a little bit of this, because whatever you leave goes straight to your only opponent.

## Know what is in the bag

There are 20 tokens of each animal. Tokens on environments are out of the bag for good. Tokens that get wiped or declined go back in.

So you can estimate scarcity. If two other players are building salmon runs and twenty salmon are spread across the game, salmon will be scarce late. If nobody is taking foxes, foxes will keep showing up. Midgame is the time to notice this and lean toward the animals the table is ignoring.

## Overpopulation as a tool

When exactly three tokens match on your turn, the wipe is your choice.

- **Keep them** if you want that animal. Three of them means you will very likely get one, and so might your neighbors.
- **Wipe them** if you need something else. A fresh draw of three tokens may bring the animal you want.

Remember the wipe happens before you pick, and only once per turn for three matching tokens.

## Timing nature tokens

Nature tokens are where Cascadia hides its biggest midgame swings. Each one does one of two things: take any tile with any token, or wipe and replace any number of tokens.

Spend one when:

- it completes or nearly completes a shape worth several points,
- it lets you take a keystone with its matching animal, which pays the nature token straight back, or
- every pair in the display is poor for you, and a wipe is better than a wasted turn.

Do not spend one to turn a 1-point pick into a 2-point pick. A kept nature token is already worth 1 point at the end, and it breaks ties.

## Watch the corridor races

With three or four players, the largest corridor of each habitat earns 3 bonus points and the second largest earns 1. That makes some tiles worth far more than one point.

In the midgame, glance at every environment and note who leads each habitat. Say you and a rival tie for the largest river corridor, so you would each get 2. One more river tile gives you 1 point for the tile and lifts your bonus to 3, while theirs drops to 1. That is a 3-point swing between you from a single tile. If you are far behind in a habitat, stop feeding it and push your other corridors.

## When to pivot

Pivot away from an animal when the bag is running dry of it, when two rivals want the same shape, or when your environment has no open tiles left that show it. The forgiving cards from the openings lesson are your safety net: a lone hawk on Hawks A or a fox with a few neighbors on Foxes A still scores with no long setup.
