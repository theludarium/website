Cascadia at two is a different game from Cascadia at four. The rules barely change, but the math under them does. The display moves slowly, the bonuses shrink, and every pair you pass goes to exactly one person. This lesson covers what changes and how to win it.

## What the rules change

From the rulebook:

- **Tiles:** 43 habitat tiles, 20 per player plus 3. Each player still takes exactly 20 turns.
- **Habitat bonuses:** the largest corridor in each habitat earns 2. A tie earns 1 each. There is no second place.
- **Tokens:** at most 40 can land on environments, out of 100, so the bag stays full all game.

Everything else is the same: four pairs in the display, the same overpopulation rules, the same nature tokens.

## Every pick is a small hate-draft

At four players, three pairs can leave the display before your next turn, so you mostly ignore what you leave behind. At two, your opponent picks from the exact four pairs you leave, three old and one new.

So the question for every turn becomes: what does this pick gain me, minus what does the best remaining pair gain them? Call it the swing.

- If your best pair gains you 5 and leaves them a pair worth 6, the swing is minus 1.
- If a slightly worse pair gains you 4 but leaves them nothing better than 2, the swing is plus 2.

Take the second. In a two-player game the second pick is often right. The [midgame lesson](/academy/cascadia/cascadia-midgame-strategy/) called hate-drafting small; at two players it is part of nearly every decision.

## You can see further ahead

Because only one pair leaves between your turns, the display is far more predictable. Three of the four pairs you see now will usually still be there after your opponent picks, minus whichever one they take.

Use that:

- **Leave a pair you want, if they do not want it.** If a salmon pair helps only you, you may be able to take a better pair now and the salmon next turn.
- **Spot their pick.** Look at their environment and name what they want. Often it is obvious: the bear that closes a trio, or the elk that extends a line.
- **Plan two turns.** With a predictable display, a nature token spent this turn can set up a strong pick next turn.

## Corridors at two players

The bonuses are smaller and there is no second place, so the corridor game changes:

- **Each habitat is a straight race.** Winning gives you 2 and your opponent 0. That is a 2-point swing per habitat, and up to 10 across all five.
- **Ties are common and cheap.** A tie gives 1 each, no swing at all.
- **Pick your races.** Watch your opponent's corridors from the opening and race only where you can lead. Where they lead by three or more, let it go and grow your own corridors instead.

The points per tile in your largest corridor still count in full. A corridor of 9 is worth 9 even if you lose the bonus.

## The bag behaves

With at most 40 tokens leaving the bag, scarcity barely matters. The [odds lesson](/academy/cascadia/cascadia-wildlife-token-odds/) showed that your animal's share of the bag drives your chances. At two players, that share stays close to one in five for most of the game unless you and your opponent both chase the same animal.

That makes demanding cards a little safer at two. A Bears B trio that needs one more bear is more likely to find it.

## Reading one opponent

Everything your opponent does is in plain view. Before each pick, ask:

1. **Which shapes are they one token from finishing?**
2. **Which corridors are they racing me for?**
3. **How many nature tokens do they hold?** They break ties, and they let your opponent take any pair, so a big stack makes their next turn hard to predict.

When your opponent holds two or more nature tokens, assume they can take any tile and any token. Deny the one combination that would hurt you most.

## The last three turns

You know exactly how many turns are left, and you can see your opponent's whole environment. In the final three turns, count both scores. If you lead, deny. If you trail, chase the biggest swing you can find, even if it costs a nature token.
