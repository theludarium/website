Cascadia's solo mode is one of the best ways to get better at the game, because nothing hides your mistakes. There is no rival to blame for taking the elk. The [variants lesson](/academy/cascadia/cascadia-expansions/) explained how solo works. This lesson is about playing it well.

## How the solo display moves

Solo uses the two-player setup with a single starter tile, and the tile stacks sit to one side of the display. Each turn:

1. Take a pair as usual (or spend nature tokens as usual), and place both.
2. Before you refill, discard the tile and the token **farthest from the stacks**. They leave the game.
3. Slide the remaining tiles and tokens away from the stacks.
4. Draw new tiles and tokens to fill the display.

You play exactly 20 turns, as always.

## Use the drift

The solo display is a conveyor belt. Pairs enter next to the stacks and leave from the far end. That gives you two things the multiplayer game does not:

- **You know what will leave next.** The pair at the far end is gone after this turn unless you take it.
- **You know what will stay.** Pairs near the stacks will usually still be there next turn, one step closer to the far end.

So the core solo habit is simple. Before you pick, ask: is the pair at the far end better than the next best pair will be next turn? If yes, take the far pair now, and take the near pair next turn.

When you spend a nature token to take a tile and a token from different slots, the slide re-pairs what is left: the remaining tiles and tokens move away from the stacks as new pairs. Before you spend, check which token will end up beside which tile.

## The solo habitat bonus: aim for 7

Solo has no rivals to race, so the bonus is fixed: 2 points for each habitat where your largest corridor is 7 tiles or more. That is up to 10 points.

Seven in all five habitats takes 35 tiles' worth of corridor, more than your 20 tiles plus your starter can give, unless many tiles count in two corridors. Two-habitat tiles are what make it possible, so solo play rewards rotating every tile to grow two corridors at once.

A realistic target is three or four habitats at 7 or more. Decide which ones by turn five.

## The rating chart

The rulebook rates a single solo game by its score:

| Score | Rating |
| --- | --- |
| 60 or more | A good start |
| 70 or more | Getting there |
| 80 or more | Very good |
| 90 or more | Excellent |
| 100 or more | Elite |
| 110 or more | The top of the chart |

These are the rulebook's thresholds, in our own words. If you score in the 80s with random cards, the habits from Levels 1 and 2 are working. The jump to 100 usually comes from corridors and nature tokens, not from one more animal shape.

## The fifteen scenarios

The rulebook's scenarios give each game a fixed card set and one to four objectives, all of which must be met to pass. The rulebook suggests playing them in order, solo, from 1 to 15, as they get harder.

- **Scenarios 1 to 4** use one letter for every animal: all A, all B, all C, then all D. Pass lines are 80, 80, 80, and 85. These teach each card family on its own.
- **Middle scenarios** mix letters and add goals, such as fully scoring an animal a set number of times or earning a minimum score from one animal.
- **Later scenarios** add restrictions, such as two animals or two habitats that may not touch, and pass lines up to 100.

The scenarios are the closest thing Cascadia has to a structured training plan. They make you play the cards you would never choose, which is exactly what a random tournament draw will do to you.

## A solo practice routine

- **One scenario a session.** Replay it until you pass, then move on.
- **Keep a log.** Score, card set, and the one card that cost you most.
- **Review the habitats.** After each game, count how many of your corridors reached 7. If it is fewer than three, that is where the next 6 points are.
