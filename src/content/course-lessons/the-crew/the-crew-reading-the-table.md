The rules limit what you may say. They do not limit what you may notice. Every card your teammates play, every lead they choose, and every time they stay quiet gives you information. Strong crews read all of it, inside the rules.

## The line you must not cross

First, the rule. You may not show, say, or hint at your cards. That includes pointed looks, sighs, hovering over a card, or slowing down on purpose to send a message. Reading the table means drawing honest conclusions from legal plays, not inventing a code. If your crew starts using table talk as a signal, the game stops being The Crew.

## Silence is a signal

The rulebook's first tip says it outright: no communication is also communication. If nobody signals before a trick, it suggests that nobody sees a danger worth spending a signal on. Owners of tasks can go after them directly.

The flip side: when a teammate does signal early, they likely see something urgent. Read their signal before you play your first card.

## What a lead tells you

The player who leads chooses the color. That choice usually has a reason:

- **Leading a high card** often means "I am going for something in this color," or "I am drawing out cards for a teammate's task."
- **Leading a low card** often means "I want someone else to win this," perhaps a teammate with a task in that color.
- **Leading a rocket** clears rockets out of the game, or takes control of the next lead.

You cannot ask why. But you can guess, and a good guess shapes your own play.

## What a discard tells you

When a player does not follow suit, they are out of that color for the rest of the mission. That is a fact, not a guess. Remember it. It tells you:

- That player can trump that color with a rocket if they have one.
- That player cannot help win a task in that color by following high.

The card they chose to discard also says something. A player dropping a high card in another color may be clearing it out of the way so it cannot win a task card by accident later.

## Following low and following high

When you must follow, the card you choose matters. Following with your lowest card means "I am staying out of this one." Following with a high card means you are trying to win, or trying to beat a card so a task owner does not get stuck. Your crew will read your pattern, so be consistent and play for the mission.

## Dead zones

Some missions show a dead zone symbol. You still place a card in front of you to communicate, and it must still be your highest, only, or lowest in that color. But you do not place the token on it. You flip the token to red and set it beside the card. Your crew has to work out which of the three it is.

How to signal in a dead zone:

- **Pick a card where only one reading makes sense.** A 9 is always your highest in its color. A 1 is always your lowest. A card your crew can already place, because of earlier tricks, is clearer than a mystery.
- **Use the mission state.** If a task makes one reading far more useful, your crew will lean toward it.

How to read one: list the three possibilities, then cross out the ones that cannot be true from what you have seen.

## Disruption

Some missions show a disruption number. Nobody may communicate until that trick. A disruption of 3 means no signals before the first or second trick; you may signal again right before the third.

In those first tricks, everything depends on reading leads and discards. Play safe, low cards unless you are sure of a task, and save your strongest information for the moment the window opens.

## Commander's decision and distribution

Some missions let the commander ask simple yes or no questions, like whether each player feels fit to take on the tasks. These are the only answers allowed. Answer truthfully for your hand, and read the other answers the same way you read a lead: a fact about the player, not a guess about a card. A later lesson covers these missions in more detail.

## Practice: the replay question

After a failed mission, ask the crew one question before you reshuffle: "Which card first told us this was going wrong?" The answer is almost always a lead or a discard someone did not read. Asking it turns each failure into a lesson.
