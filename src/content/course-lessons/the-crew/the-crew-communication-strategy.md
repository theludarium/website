You get one signal per mission attempt. It is the only card information your crew will ever hear from you. That makes it the most valuable move you make all mission, and the easiest to waste.

This lesson is about reading a signal fully and choosing yours with care.

## What each token position really says

The rule is short: top means highest, middle means only, bottom means lowest. But each one quietly tells your crew about your other cards too.

- **Top (highest).** "I have this card, and nothing higher in this color." Every card above it in that color is in someone else's hand. If you signal the blue 6 as highest, the blue 7, 8, and 9 are all with your teammates.
- **Middle (only).** "This is my one card in this color." The other eight are spread among your teammates. You will also be out of that color as soon as you play it, which means you can drop off-color cards or rockets into that color's tricks afterward.
- **Bottom (lowest).** "I have this card, and nothing lower in this color." Every card below it is elsewhere. Signal the green 7 as lowest, and your crew knows the green 1 to 6 are not yours.

The token stays where you put it, even after your hand changes. If your "highest" becomes your "only" later, the token does not move. Everyone has to remember it as a statement about the moment you made it.

## The rulebook's best tip, and why it works

The rulebook gives one specific piece of advice: if you hold only the 8 and the 9 of a color, signal the 8 as your lowest, not the 9 as your highest.

Here is why. "9, highest" says almost nothing, because the 9 is always the highest card of its color. Your crew learns you hold the 9, and that is all. "8, lowest" says you hold the 8, and that nothing you hold in that color is below it. The only card that could be above it is the 9. If the 8 were your only card, you would have put the token in the middle, so you must hold the 9 too. One signal, two cards revealed. (That last step relies on a habit worth agreeing on as a crew: a single card always goes in the middle.)

The general lesson: a signal is worth more when the token position rules out many cards. Look for the version that lets your crew work out the most.

## Which card to signal

Ask what your crew needs to know to finish the mission. A few common answers:

- **"Who can win this task?"** If a task card is out for the pink 3 and you hold the pink 9 as your highest, signaling it tells the task owner you can cover that trick, or warns them you might win the 3 by accident.
- **"Who holds this task card's card?"** If a teammate owns the task for the yellow 2 and you hold it as your only yellow, signaling it as only tells them that the moment yellow is led, you will have to play it. They can plan to win that trick.
- **"Who is safe to lead to?"** A signal of "only" shows where a color will run out soon. That helps a teammate plan a trick you can't follow, where you can safely dump a card.

## When to wait

Signaling before the first trick is common, and often right when a task is urgent. But waiting has real value:

- After a few tricks, cards have fallen, and you know more about what your crew needs.
- Your hand has changed, so a signal now can be more precise. A color where you held three cards might be down to one, and "only" is a very clear message.
- Other players may signal first. Their information can change which signal of yours is the most useful.

The cost of waiting is that a trick might go wrong before you speak. If a task is at risk in the next trick or two, signal now.

## Reading the signal you are given

When a teammate signals, pause and work out everything it says. Ask three questions:

1. **What do I now know they hold?** The signaled card, plus anything the position forces, like the 9 in the 8-lowest example.
2. **What do I know they do not hold?** Every card above a top signal or below a bottom signal, and every other card in that color for a middle signal.
3. **Why this card, and why now?** Your teammate chose this out of all their cards. What problem in the mission does it solve?

The third question is where the real teamwork happens. Our article on [what The Crew teaches about communication](/articles/the-crew-card-game/) and our piece on [cooperative games and teamwork](/articles/cooperative-games-teamwork/) both dig into this.

## Signals in later missions

Some missions change the rules. A dead zone lets you show the card but not the token, so your crew has to guess which of the three it is. A disruption blocks all signals until a certain trick. The logbook explains each when it arrives, and a later lesson covers them.
