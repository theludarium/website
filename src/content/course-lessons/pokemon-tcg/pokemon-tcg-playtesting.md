Most players test by playing a lot of games. That helps, but slowly. A little structure makes the same games far more useful: you learn which matchups are close, which cards earn their slots, and which mistakes keep coming back.

## Choosing a deck

Weigh four things.

**1. Can you play it well?** The deck you know usually beats a stronger deck you just picked up. Every deck has small decisions, like which Pokémon to Bench first or when to use a key Supporter, that only practice teaches.

**2. How does it do against the decks you expect?** Use the [metagame lesson](/academy/pokemon-tcg/pokemon-tcg-metagame/). A deck with no hopeless matchups is often a better choice than one that beats half the room and loses to the other half.

**3. Is it legal?** Check every card against the current Standard format: regulation marks first, then the banned list.

**4. Can you build it?** Some decks need expensive cards. Trading at League, buying singles, and building around cards you already own are all fair ways to get there. The [budget upgrades lesson](/academy/pokemon-tcg/pokemon-tcg-deck-upgrades/) has tips.

Copying a decklist from a big event is normal and fine. Many strong players start from a published list and change a few cards for their local meta.

## Where to test

**Pokémon TCG Live.** The free official online game lets you build decks and play other people fast. It is the quickest way to get many games in, and it offers the Standard format. Keep in mind that online opponents may not play the decks your store plays.

**At the table.** Play in person too. Shuffling, tracking damage counters, and keeping a tidy board are skills, and they matter under a tournament clock. League nights and friends who also want to practice are ideal.

**Goldfishing.** Play your deck alone, with no opponent, for a few turns at a time. Note the turn your main attacker would first attack. Twenty quick goldfish games tell you more about consistency than a feeling ever will.

## Keep a testing log

After each game, write one line with:

- **Your opponent's deck.**
- **First or second.**
- **Result.**
- **The turn your main attacker first attacked.**
- **One sentence on why the game went the way it did.**

That last column is the important one. "Prized my only copy of a key card" and "opponent attacked before I could set up" point to very different fixes.

## Reading your results

A few games prove little. Win three of five against a deck and the next five could easily go the other way. Look for patterns over ten or more games against the same deck, and pay more attention to your one-sentence reasons than to the win count.

When a pattern appears, ask:

- **Is it the list?** You keep failing to find the same card. Add a copy or a search card.
- **Is it the matchup?** You set up fine and still lose. Look at the [tech cards lesson](/academy/pokemon-tcg/pokemon-tcg-tech-cards/), or accept the matchup and plan around it.
- **Is it your play?** The same misplay shows up in your notes. That is the easiest fix of all.

## Change a few cards at a time

Swap two or three cards, then test again. Change ten at once and you cannot tell which change helped. Keep your old lists. Sometimes the earlier version was better.

## Practice the match, not just the game

Tournament matches are usually best of three in about 50 minutes. Once a week, play a full match against a friend with a timer running, using the [tournament rules](/academy/pokemon-tcg/pokemon-tcg-tournament-rules/) for time and the extra turns. Note how much time is left after game two. If you often run out of time, practice playing faster on easy turns.

## Lock it in

Choose a date, a week or so before your event, after which you stop making big changes. Spend the last week playing the final list until every card feels familiar. Calm, practiced play with a good deck wins more than nervous play with a perfect one.
