The metagame, or "meta," is the set of decks people actually bring to events. It shifts with every new set, every banned list update, and every big event result. You do not need to chase it. But knowing it turns deck choice from a guess into a plan.

## Why the meta matters more here

Two rules from Bandai's Tournament Rules Manual raise the stakes:

- **No side decks.** In constructed events, you cannot swap cards between games. The 50 cards you register face every opponent. (Sealed is different; there your unused cards are a sideboard.)
- **Best of one in Swiss.** Qualifying rounds are usually a single game. A bad matchup cannot be recovered in game two.

So your deck has to be ready for the field as it comes, in one game, with no adjustments.

## Find out what the field plays

Start close to home and work outward:

- **Your store.** The regulars at your Store Tournaments are a small, real meta. Note which Leaders you face each week.
- **Big event results.** Organizers may publish deck lists, and the Tournament Rules Manual allows it once an event has begun. Top-cut lists from Regionals show what is winning at the highest level.
- **New sets and the banned list.** A new set or a banned list update can reshape the field overnight. The most recent Banned/Restricted update took effect October 12, 2026.

Look for the shape, not every card. Which three to five Leaders show up most? What does each want to do: attack early, build a board, or control the game?

## Matchups in plain terms

For each common Leader, ask two questions:

1. **How does my deck beat it?** Out-race it, out-last it, or remove its key Characters?
2. **How does it beat me?** What turn does it threaten to win, and what do I need in hand to survive it?

If you cannot answer the first question for a deck that a third of the room plays, that is a problem worth solving before the event.

## The best deck, or your best deck?

You will hear about "the best deck" before every event. It is worth considering. But a deck you have played fifty times usually beats a stronger deck you have played five times. You know its odds, its lethal turns, and its weak hands. You will also play it faster, which matters on a 30 or 35-minute clock.

A simple rule: switch to a new deck only if you have time to practice it seriously before the event. The practice plan in the next lesson assumes six weeks.

## Tuning the last few slots

Most of a good deck does not change from week to week. The last handful of cards are where you prepare for the field:

- **If the field attacks early,** lean toward cheaper Blockers, higher Counter values, and early plays.
- **If the field builds big boards,** lean toward removal.
- **If the field plays long games,** lean toward card flow and a strong finish.

Change a few cards at a time, then test. Every card you add pushes another one out, and the 4-copy limit and your cost curve still apply.

## Play the deck you registered

Once your deck list is in, the decision is made. Do not spend the event wishing you had brought something else. Use the rounds to learn the field for next time.

Keeping a record helps. After each match, away from the play area, write down your opponent's Leader and how the game went. Notes are not allowed during a match, and the manual asks players to keep paper away from the table while they play, so keep the notebook in your bag until the match is over. After a few events, those notes show the real meta at the events you attend, which is the one that matters to you.

## Respect the room

The meta is made of people. The player across from you brought the deck they love or the one they think is best, the same as you. Learn from every matchup, and thank them either way.
