You have reached the top of the ladder. You can play, teach, and run [Slugblaster](/academy/slugblaster/how-to-play-slugblaster/) through a whole season. This last lesson covers three ways to keep going: play alone, tune the rules, and find more Slugblaster to play and listen to.

## Playing solo

The book includes solo rules, written by Michael Elliott. They play like the normal game with a few changes.

**The Loner.** Solo players use a special personality made only for solo play. Its attitude lets you add boost or kick to any action, and it refills 1 whenever you fail. Traits include flashing back to how you prepared for a moment, an extra die on disaster rolls with an extra slam box, and an Autodidact trait with two blanks to fill instead of one. The Loner earns +2 style after every run, no matter what. The book also suggests trying solo with another personality for a harder game.

**No crew, so achievements.** Instead of crew fame, you chase achievements, a list of things like finishing a chase, running from Rescue agents, attending a party, or meeting another version of yourself. Every 7 achievements raises your fame and gives you a new perk.

**Two arcs.** With no crew to notice your problems, you have only the family arc and the Loner arc, plus the usual beats like Being Good, In the Lab, and Portal Discovery. The Loner arc moves from being alone toward a real connection, through a break and, if you get there, a reunion.

**You play the GM too.** Each run starts with a **solo bite roll**. Build a pool starting at one die: subtract a die for an unvisited world, for having slams, and for having enemies; add one for staying in boring old Null and for having allies. Roll it. A 6 gives you 1 bite, a 4 or 5 gives you 2, a 1 to 3 gives you 3. If the pool would be zero dice or fewer, roll two and keep the lowest. Spend each bite on a random problem from the solo problem table or one of the scene tables. The run does not end until all your bite is spent.

**You can nope snags.** Solo play uses the alternate rule that lets you refuse snags as well as slams.

**Ending.** Stop when you mark 4 doom, finish one or both arcs, or earn every achievement and reach the top fame level. Then roll your epilogue. Keep a journal, sketch each run like a video game level, or just keep score.

If you enjoy this, the site has more on [playing roleplaying games alone](/articles/solo-rpg/).

## Tuning the game

The book calls its alternate rules untested and invites you to use them at your own risk. Some of the most useful, and what they change:

**Softer:**
- **Afterburner.** Boost and kick may be spent after the roll.
- **No disaster rolls.** Removes the push-your-luck finale for a calmer game.
- **Crits.** Two or more 6s make an action more effective or add 1 style.
- **Hype generator.** Successful tricks add hype to the crew.
- **Noping snags.** More player control, at the risk of blocking fun.

**Harsher or more dramatic:**
- **Slams deplete you.** Taking a slam also costs some boost or kick.
- **Beats during runs.** Lets a parent's furious call land mid-chase. The book suggests making those slams super slams so they last.
- **Betting style.** Wager style on a big trick. Double it if you land it, lose it if you bail. Set a limit.

**For Blades in the Dark fans:**
- **Flashbacks.** Spend 1 kick to roll an action your teen could have taken earlier.

For simple difficulty changes, the book points to three dials: add or remove bite, add or remove hype, and lengthen or shorten tracks.

## Coming from D&D or Blades in the Dark

The book's quick guide is a good thing to read aloud at session zero. For D&D players: there are no skills, movement rates, initiative, or hit bonuses, and monsters do not roll. Impact is like damage, and progress tracks are like a health bar for any obstacle. For Blades in the Dark players: legacy is like stash, doom like trauma, trouble like stress, and style like experience and coin together. Slams are harm with no penalties, and resisting is called noping.
