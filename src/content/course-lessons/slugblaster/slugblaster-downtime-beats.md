Welcome to Level 3. Runs are the action half of [Slugblaster](/academy/slugblaster/how-to-play-slugblaster/). Downtime is the other half: the week back home in town after a run, with chores, parents, crushes, and tinkering in the garage. It can become the part of the night your group remembers most. This lesson covers how it works and how to make it sing.

## How beats work

A **beat** is a moment you buy to fuel the story. Each has a cost in style or trouble, and buying it clears that much from your sheet. Then you play out the scene it describes. The book's main rules:

- Pay the listed style or trouble. You can always buy a trouble beat even if you cannot afford it.
- **Trait beats** and **arc beats** are one-time buys.
- **Arc beats** go in order, and you can buy only one beat from each arc per downtime.
- Everything else has no limit. Buy three In the Labs at once if you want.

Players take turns describing their scenes. You are the director: set the scene, cast other players as the people in it, and say what your teen does and feels. No dice.

## The beats on your sheet

**Trait beats.** Each personality has its own, at 2 style, that plays out a scene in character and gives +1 trait: Grinding for The Grit, Swagger for The Guts, Research for The Smarts, Devotion for The Heart, Just Vibin' for The Chill. Two shared ones cost more: Origin Story (3 style, a flashback) and Sharpened (4 style, a teammate inspires you).

**The family arc** (trouble). Your family disapproves of slugblasting, then gives a final warning, then hits the last straw. Each step clears trouble and costs you a slam, doom, or both. (The 1.33 sheets price Final Warning in style instead; the [rules slips lesson](/academy/slugblaster/slugblaster-rules-mistakes/) has the details.) The last step, **Redemption**, costs style and clears doom.

**The angst arc** (trouble). The same shape for teenage stress: angst, struggling, darkness, then a style-cost **Catharsis** that clears doom and a fracture.

**Your personality arc.** Four steps, mostly style, with one trouble step in the third slot where things go wrong. The Grit's goes from setting a goal through setbacks and quitting to perseverance. The Heart's is a relationship. The Smarts' is a big idea that goes haywire. Each ends with a payoff: legacy, a new trait, less doom or fewer fractures.

**Crew beats.** Opportunity (2 style) rolls on the opportunity table for something good: a contest, a party invitation, a sponsor's free samples. Challenge (5 trouble) has the GM roll for something bad: a faction calling you out, impostors from another world, someone telling your parents. Fight (5 trouble) adds a fracture. Make Up (5 style) clears one.

**Other beats.** Being Good (1 style) clears 2 trouble. You Earned It (5 style) gives +1 legacy for a trophy or a trick named after you. Take an L (5 trouble) gives doom. In the Lab and Portal Discovery cover gear and new portals.

## How to plan a downtime

A simple plan for each player:

1. **Clear what threatens you.** If you sit on 4 or more trouble, buy a trouble beat or Being Good. The [disaster odds lesson](/academy/slugblaster/slugblaster-trouble-disaster-odds/) shows why.
2. **Move one story forward.** Buy the next step of an arc you care about.
3. **Grow.** If style is left, buy a trait beat or In the Lab.

Arcs are where the big rewards live. Every personality arc's final beat gives a new trait and clears doom or fractures, and most add legacy too. (The Guts is the exception: its finale gives up legacy to pour style into the crew.) But you only reach the end by walking through the hard middle step. That is the design: the teen has to fall before they rise.

## The crew beats are a dial

Opportunity and Challenge change what the next run is about. If your table is out of ideas for the next run, buy one. Two things raise the roll on these tables: certain fame tiers, and how many factions like you (for opportunities) or dislike you (for challenges). A crew with lots of enemies draws harsher challenges.

Fractures matter. The crew sheet has two fracture boxes. If the crew takes a fracture when both are full, you break up: everyone marks doom and at least one teen leaves the crew. Watch the Fight beat and the arcs that add fractures, and buy Make Up before it is too late.

## Running a good scene

The book's best advice for the GM is to ask questions rather than suggest answers:

- Where are we? What does the room look like?
- Who else is here? Should one of us play them?
- Is this a montage? What song is playing?
- If we watched through the window, what would we see?

Having a side character open with a question works wonders: "Is everything okay, sweetheart? You've hardly touched your dinner." Nothing gets a teen talking like a chance to lie.

Two more tips. Combine beats into shared scenes, as the book's own example does with a dad who sits on the bed while his kid solders. And keep scenes short. One strong moment beats a long conversation.

## When it gets heavy

The family and angst arcs can touch real pain. The book recommends checking in with each other and using a safety tool like Script Change. Anyone can steer a scene lighter, skip it, or fade to black.
