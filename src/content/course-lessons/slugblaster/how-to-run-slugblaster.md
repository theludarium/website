The best way to teach [Slugblaster](/academy/slugblaster/how-to-play-slugblaster/) is to teach almost nothing up front. The core rule fits in one breath, and the rest makes sense the first time it happens. This lesson is a host script for the person who will run the game, from the pitch to the end of the first run. Print the [teach sheet](/academy/slugblaster/teach-sheet/) and the free reference sheets from [slugblaster.com](https://slugblaster.com/) and keep them on the table.

## The one-minute pitch

Say something like this:

"We're teenagers in a tiny, boring prairie town that happens to sit on thin spacetime. Kids here have figured out how to ride hoverboards through portals into other worlds. It's dangerous, it's not allowed, and it's the coolest thing ever. We want to film tricks, go viral, and get famous. We also have chores, parents, and feelings. I describe the world and what goes wrong. You decide what your teen does."

Then the rule: "When you try something risky, roll one die. Six, you do it. Four or five, you do it but something goes wrong. One to three, you don't, and something goes wrong." Stop there. Everything else waits.

## Session zero, in order

Session zero is the stuff before the first run. The book's order works well:

1. **Sheets on the table.** Personality sheets, signature sheets, the crew sheet, dice, pencils, snacks.
2. **The pitch,** as above.
3. **Tone.** Talk through a few of the book's tone questions. Is this more Stranger Things, Into the Spider-Verse, or Adventure Time? How hurt is someone who falls 40 feet into a bush? Is it okay to blast a giant bug that isn't attacking you? Agree on topics you will skip. The book recommends Script Change, a free safety toolkit by Beau Jáger Sheldon, and its movie terms (pause, rewind, fast forward) fit this game well.
4. **Characters.** Each player picks one of the five personalities, a look, two family details, a bond with another teen, some vibes, one trait, a signature device with one starting mod, and two extra pieces of gear.
5. **Introductions.** Each player describes their teen and their bond. A tip from the book: have each player pick the bond with the character of the player to their right, so everyone is connected.
6. **The crew.** Pick two brands, two hangout spots, a starting perk, faction relationships, and draw a few portals on the map. The book gives one personality the final say on most of these choices (The Guts on brands, The Chill on hangouts, The Grit on the perk), which keeps it moving.

The book notes you can wait on crew creation, and even brand choices, until after the first run. That is a good call for a new group. The run teaches them what kind of crew they are.

## Teach the rest as it happens

Keep a short list in your head and introduce each rule the first time the fiction calls for it:

- **First risky action:** the action roll, and "you keep the highest die."
- **First mixed success:** problems, snags, and slams.
- **First slam:** noping, and what trouble is for.
- **First player who wants it badly:** boost and kick. Point at the boxes on their sheet.
- **First player who wants to show off:** "check it," and how tricks earn style.
- **Near the end:** disaster rolls, then clean-up and downtime.

This is how the book's own sample of play runs, and it works because every rule shows up with a reason attached.

## Start in the action

The book offers two ways to begin. You can open in town, with each teen's daily life, which is slower and builds the emotional core. Or you can start mid-run, near the first obstacle, which teaches the rules fastest. For a first session, start in the action. Mundane teenage life will show up in downtime anyway.

The book's first-run outline is a good skeleton:

1. Open near the first obstacle. Exciting, but not deadly.
2. Ease off. Let them explore, talk to characters, maybe reach the goal.
3. Add a twist: a monster, Rescue agents, the world starts collapsing.
4. End with a big chase or fight. You can call for disaster rolls near the climax.
5. Head home, run any remaining disaster rolls, then clean-up and downtime.

A good first goal is small and concrete: get candy from an abandoned store, film a trick at a famous spot, deliver a package. The prize can be 3 to 5 crew style.

## Prep less than you think

The book is clear that you should not plan a plot. The players drive. Prep a handful of problems, two or three characters with voices, a few vivid details for the place they are going, and a list of names you find hard to invent on the spot. Then react.

## Wrap up with downtime

Leave 30 to 45 minutes for clean-up and downtime the first night. It is where players fall for their characters. If someone freezes, ask questions: "Where is this scene? Who else is there? What song is playing?"
