Welcome to Level 4. The rest of this course is mostly for the person running the game. If you play, read on anyway: knowing what the GM is weighing makes you a better teammate.

The [Slugblaster](/academy/slugblaster/how-to-play-slugblaster/) book lists the GM's jobs as setup, pacing, tone, decisions, and danger. It calls danger the most important of the five, and puts it bluntly: you are a consequence generator. This lesson is about that job.

## The mindset

The book gives the GM five attitudes, and they are worth keeping on a card behind your screen:

- **Be curious.** Ask questions. Play to find out what happens.
- **Be bold.** Paint the multiverse in slime and neon. Make everything a skatepark.
- **Be passionate.** Be a fan of these teens. When they roll badly, the world is dangerous. They are not incompetent.
- **Be tough.** If you say the slug eats them when it catches up, follow through. They have plenty of ways to save themselves.
- **Be chill.** Prep less. Say "yes, and," and when you cannot, "no, but."

## Obstacles come first

Before the run, know at least one **obstacle**: the thing the crew has to overcome to get the prize. Obstacles are known or hinted at in advance. The prize is hidden. The path is guarded. The window closes at midnight. A rival crew wants the same spot. Problems come later, from the dice.

## Snag or slam?

When a player rolls a 4, 5, or 1 to 3, you have three choices.

**A snag** makes the situation worse and creates more story: a new threat appears, the task needs more steps, the area gets more dangerous, the scene twists, or an asset is lost. Snags cannot be noped, because they add fun.

**A slam** is a lasting hurt to body, mind, or gear: bruised, humiliated, drained powercell, pinned down. The player can take it or nope it. Slams are clean one-time stings, and they work best when the scene is ready to end and you do not want to extend it.

**Bite.** Or you simply gain 1 bite to spend later.

A rough rhythm: snags early in a scene, slams near the end. And mix the kinds of slams. Physical hurt is the obvious one, but the book also points you to stress, embarrassment, exhaustion, and broken gear, which fit a game about teenagers.

## Worse problems

When a player does a trick or refuses to run from something huge, problems are **worse**. That can mean two problems, a slam for everyone, or a **super slam**. A super slam, like "super bruised," only loses its intensifier at clean-up, so it lingers into the next run. It takes two nopes to avoid fully. The book's sample of play shows the right energy: a trick rolled as a 1 earns both a hard fall and a slime puddle full of eggs.

## Bite

Bite is the GM's currency for surprises that would otherwise feel unfair. You gain 1 per player at each refresh, 1 whenever you choose bite over a problem, more from risky runs and some opportunities and challenges, and 1 whenever a player pays for a dare with it.

- **1 bite:** a surprise problem, cutting to the action with the crew in over their heads, or suddenly advancing a threat and its danger track.
- **2 bite:** force an extra disaster roll on one teen, or bring in a random challenge (pick a specific one for a little more).

The book's reasoning is the useful part: limiting your power makes the run feel winnable, and it frees you to be wild without worrying you overstepped. Unspent bite can be spent in downtime on challenges or looming threats.

## Judging impact

Players say what they do, dice say whether it works, and you say how much it accomplishes. The book's questions: Is it clever? Did they look for an edge? Are they slammed in a way that matters? Is it the right tool? Could you picture it in a movie? Are they adding kick?

When in doubt, **say yes and ask for a roll**. Say no only to protect the tone, keep a scene honest, or stop someone copying a teammate's special ability. Climbing a ladder needs no roll. Climbing it during a laser fight needs one. Jumping over a house without special boots is a no.

Skip perception checks. Either give the players the information, or give them a thread and let them pull it: "The bushes just moved. What do you do?"

## Tracks

**Progress tracks** measure how close the crew is to finishing something big: killing a giant cockroach, hacking a door, impressing a sponsor. **Danger tracks** measure something closing in: a storm, a search party, a portal shrinking.

The first track you set in a scene sets its pace. Should this door take one roll or a whole scene? The book suggests 4 to 6 marks as a good default. A success marks 1, a very effective one marks 2 or more. Danger tracks tick with time, with problems, or with bite. Draw them as circles, a row of boxes, or spell a word like in a game of S.K.A.T.E.

## Player against player

Teen drama can set two characters against each other. The book says not to use dice. Ask both players to shape the scene together. If that stalls, settle it with a coin flip, rock paper scissors, or a trouble bid, where whoever will mark more trouble wins.
