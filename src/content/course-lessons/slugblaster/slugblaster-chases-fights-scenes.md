The book's advice for players ends with one rule: cheese it. The best sessions of [Slugblaster](/academy/slugblaster/how-to-play-slugblaster/) end in a chase, and running away is a tactic, not a failure. This lesson walks the four kinds of scenes most runs are built from, with the tools the book gives the GM for each.

## Chases

A Slugblaster chase is not about distance or speed numbers. The book describes it as a side-scroller obstacle course the crew runs through as a tight group while something breathes down their necks.

**The checklist:**

1. **Get things moving.** Players hesitate to flee. So skip the debate: "It starts chasing you! Where are you headed?"
2. **Set the course.** A finish line (a thin-zone, somewhere safe) and 3 to 5 checkpoints. The players know how many, even if each one is a surprise.
3. **Set the stakes.** Say clearly what happens if they lose: two slams, losing the prize, disaster rolls. Set a danger track of 3 to 5 marks. If it fills, they lose.
4. **Run it.** Each checkpoint takes an action. On a 1 to 3 they still get through, but slowly, and the danger track advances. Problems happen as usual.

Good checkpoints have hazards, choices, or things to use: a chain-link fence, a soccer practice, a sofa being loaded into a moving truck. Each world in the book lists its own. Players can spend kick or extra actions to slow the pursuer and clear danger marks.

**Group rolls** suit chases well. One player rolls for everyone, anyone can boost or kick that roll by describing how they help, and the result applies to the whole group. If the crew splits up on purpose, the pursuer picks one teen and the rest are free.

The same checklist works for chasing someone or racing another crew. A slow, tense version works for stealth too, which the book calls cat and mouse.

## Fights

Fights are usually the run's big action scene. Tools from the book:

- **More than one progress track, more than one way to win.** Blast the bug to bits, or bring the cave down on it.
- **Immunities.** A monster that shrugs off lasers makes players scramble for a better idea.
- **Evolve early, slam late.** At the start, use problems as snags: the monster mutates, more arrive, it swallows someone. Later, use problems as slams.
- **Move the fight.** Shift the battle through several locations, using checkpoints as stages.
- **Big monsters hit harder.** They can deal extra slams or super slams.

Monsters do not roll. They act when it makes sense and when players roll problems.

## Infiltration

Sneaking into a lab, past club bouncers, or into your mom's email. Make it a meal: give the players objects and terrain to use, set progress and danger tracks, and turn up the heat one problem at a time without boiling over into a fight too soon. Typical problems: sentries you did not expect, a guard taking a break by your hiding spot, a creaking floorboard, an alarm, a booby trap, nerves fraying into a slam.

## Social scenes

Parties, interviews, photo shoots, and high-stakes texting are real encounters with real danger. The trick is to make the social goals visible as tracks. "Impress the Sponsor" as progress. "Sponsor Leaves the Party" as danger. Add "The Cool Kids Get Annoyed" and you have a minefield. Slam freely: embarrassed, flustered, over-caffeinated.

## Getting there

When the trip is not the point, the book has a quick travel roll. One player rolls for the group, everyone can help. On a 6, smooth sailing, and each player describes a fun moment. On a 4 or 5, they arrive but take slams or trigger a looming threat. On a 1 to 3, a big problem stops them or sends the run in a worse direction. For a long trip, roll once per world crossed.

## Pacing it all

Jump cuts are your strongest tool. After the first party in another world, start the next one mid dance battle. Smash cut from the heist plan to the alarms. But the book warns: jump to the fun, not past it. If your players love planning outfits, let them. And remember that cutting to a desperate situation costs 1 bite.

## Keep the teens alive

The book is firm on two things. The teens should not kill people, and they should not die. Remind players about stun settings, let them run from Rescue agents, and give them androids or pod people to blast instead. When a situation stretches too far, peelback, being yanked painfully back home, is the ejection seat. It is still a harsh consequence, so it works where death would in other games.
