Welcome to Level 1. At Level 0 you learned what a die can do. Now you learn what a die is likely to do, and how much control the game really gives you. The answer surprises most new players: a lot. The Castles of Burgundy uses dice, but it hands you the tools to steer them, and a few plain numbers show how far those tools go.

Every figure here comes from counting the 36 ways two dice can land, using the rulebook's worker rule: each worker changes a die by 1, up or down, and 1 and 6 wrap around.

## Wrapping makes the die a circle

Because a 6 can become a 1 and a 1 a 6, the six faces sit in a ring. No number is ever more than 3 steps from another. That means:

- **0 workers:** a die reaches 1 number, its own face.
- **1 worker:** it reaches 3 numbers, its face and the two beside it.
- **2 workers:** 5 numbers.
- **3 workers:** all 6. Three workers can turn any die into anything.

## The odds of hitting one number

Most turns, you need one specific number: the depot with the tile you want, or the space that tile goes on. You have two dice, so you need just one of them to get there.

| Workers you can spend | Chance to hit one number |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | 11 in 36, about 31% |
| 1 | 27 in 36, 75% |
| 2 | 35 in 36, about 97% |
| 3 | Certain |

Read the jump from the first row to the second. One worker more than doubles your chance. On average, hitting a single number costs just under one worker. That is why players who keep a worker or two in hand seem to roll well all game.

## The odds of hitting two numbers

The best turns use both dice on a plan: take a tile from depot 4 and place it on a 2 space, say. Now you need both numbers, one on each die. That is much harder.

| Workers you can spend | Two numbers next to each other (like 1 and 2) | Two numbers across the ring (like 1 and 4) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 2 in 36, about 6% | 2 in 36, about 6% |
| 1 | 8 in 36, about 22% | 10 in 36, about 28% |
| 2 | 18 in 36, 50% | 26 in 36, about 72% |
| 3 | 28 in 36, about 78% | Certain |

A two-number plan with no workers almost never happens. With two workers it is a coin flip or better. On average, landing two numbers next to each other costs about two and a half workers.

The lesson: do not build a turn around needing two exact numbers. Build around one, and let the other die do whatever it can.

## How to make one number enough

Since one number is easy and two are hard, split your plans so each needs just one die:

- **Take a tile this turn, place it next turn.** Your storage holds three tiles. A tile waiting in storage turns next turn's plan into a one-number problem.
- **Keep tiles for several numbers in storage.** A stored ship that fits a 2 space and a stored building that fits a 5 space give you two ways to use your dice.
- **Use the free actions.** Taking 2 workers works with any number. So does a black depot buy, which needs no die at all.

The rulebook warns that discarding from full storage is usually a bad idea, so this works best with one or two tiles waiting, not three.

## Tools that change the math

Several monastery tiles bend these numbers further:

- **Monastery 8:** a worker may change a die by 2 instead of 1. The rulebook's own example: a 3 becomes a 6 with 2 workers instead of 3. Your workers go a lot further.
- **Monasteries 9 to 12:** change a die by 1 for free when you place certain tile colors, or when you take a tile.
- **Monastery 14:** the take-workers action gives 4 workers instead of 2.

And a castle is the strongest tool of all. Once you place it, it gives an extra action with any number you choose, no worker needed. A castle waiting in storage is a turn that can grow when the dice line up.

## Putting a price on a plan

Before you commit to a plan, price it in workers. If it needs one number and you hold a worker, it is very likely. If it needs two numbers and you hold none, it is a hope. Choose the plan you can pay for.
