At home, finishing second in a close game feels good. At most tournaments, it does very little. This lesson looks at how a well-known Castles of Burgundy tournament scores its heats, and what that means for the choices you make in the last phases of a game.

## How the WBC scores heats

The [2026 World Boardgaming Championships event preview](https://www.boardgamers.org/wbc26/Previews/cob.html) uses a format called Heats, Most Wins, Points:

- **Wins come first.** Players are ranked by how many heats they won.
- **Then points.** Each heat earns 1,000 for first place, 100 for second, 10 for third, and 1 for fourth.
- **Sixteen players reach the semifinal.** The preview says more than one win will probably be needed.
- **Only semifinal winners reach the final,** a single four-player game for the title.

In that system, wins are counted first, so one heat win outranks any number of second places. And in the semifinal and final, only first place counts at all.

## What that means at the table

Most of your game, playing well and playing to win are the same thing. The difference shows up late, usually in phase D or E, when you can count the scores well enough to know where you stand.

### When you are behind

If the leader's likely final score is out of reach with safe moves, safe moves only lock in a finish that barely counts. Look instead for the plan that could still win:

- **A big region you can close in phase E.** Size points do not shrink. An 8-space region is still 38 points in the last phase.
- **A color race** that would also close a region.
- **A bridge move** that puts you before the leader in the final round, so you take the tile they need.

These plans can fail and leave you lower than a safe line would. In a wins-first format, that trade is often worth it.

### When you are ahead

Now reverse it. Choose the line with the least risk. Close what you can close for sure. Take the tile a chasing player needs when it also helps you. Keep a worker or two, so a bad final roll cannot strand you.

### When it is close

Count the leader's end points, not just the track. Unsold goods, silver, and pairs of workers each add up, and so do end-game monasteries in their duchy. A leader with five silver, three goods, and monastery 25 placed has more points coming than the track shows.

## Know the tiebreakers

The rulebook breaks a tie by the most empty spaces in the duchy, then by who is furthest behind on the bridge. The WBC preview uses the same first tiebreaker, and then the tied player who moved last in the final round.

In practice: in a very close game, scoring the same points with fewer tiles placed wins. If two plans score the same, the one that fills fewer spaces is better.

## Play your own game

Two cautions keep this from going wrong.

**Do not play to spoil.** If you cannot win, play your best game for your own result anyway. Deliberately handing a game to one player over another is poor sportsmanship, and it spoils the game for everyone at the table.

**Do not panic early.** Risky plans belong late, when you can count. In phases A to C, the best path to a win is simply good play.

## The semifinal and final

In single-game rounds where only first counts, everything above applies from earlier in the game. The WBC preview says the semifinal and final use two championship boards, chosen at random, with or without monasteries.
