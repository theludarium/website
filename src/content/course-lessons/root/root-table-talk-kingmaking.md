In most games, you win by playing your own position well. In Root, that is only half the job. Four factions score in four different ways, the game ends the instant someone reaches 30, and one faction often cannot stop a runaway leader alone. The table has to talk, and it has to act together. That is what makes Root feel alive, and it is also where most of its arguments start.

This lesson covers what the rules allow, how to read who is winning, and how to keep the talk fair.

## What does the Law say about deals?

The Law of Root keeps it short:

- Players may discuss the game and make agreements, but **agreements are not binding**.
- Players may give each other cards **only when a rule says so**, such as the Vagabond's Aid or the Alliance's Outrage.
- **Actions never require consent.** Nobody can refuse to be attacked, and nobody needs permission to move into a clearing.
- The **number of cards** in each hand is public. The cards themselves stay private unless a rule reveals them.
- Face-up piles such as the discard pile can be searched. The shared deck cannot.

So you can promise anything, and so can everyone else. A promise in Root is worth exactly as much as the person making it.

## Why does Root need table talk?

Because the leader is not always obvious. A Marquise on 18 points and an Eyrie on 15 look close, but the Eyrie may score 4 every Evening while the Marquise has run out of room to build. The game also ends the moment a player hits 30, even mid-turn, so the last few turns before that moment are where games are decided. Asked why players do not get equal turns, Leder Games' FAQ answers that seating order is part of the game's asymmetry.

Leder's own guidance builds in a duty to police, too. The Learning to Play guide warns the Marquise in a four-player game not to chase a few extra points while the map fills with rivals. The Alliance, by Leder's own description, can rise late only if it keeps the other players in check. Somebody always has to be the one hitting the leader.

## How do you read who is winning?

Every faction shows its next turn on the table, if you know where to look.

- **Marquise:** count the buildings left on her faction board, her wood, and the clearings she rules where she could build next. Each building scores, and late buildings score the most.
- **Eyrie:** count the roosts on the map. More roosts mean more points every Evening, turn after turn. Then look at the Decree: a long Decree with few birds is close to turmoil.
- **Alliance:** look at the sympathy track and their supporters. A stack of supporters plus a sympathetic clearing full of enemy buildings means a revolt is coming.
- **Vagabond:** check the relationship chart and the quests. A faction one aid from Allied, or a third quest of one suit, is a big turn waiting to happen.
- **Dominance:** any player with 10 or more points can trade their score for a dominance goal. Watch for dominance cards sitting by the map, which any player can take by spending a matching card.

Saying this out loud helps everyone. "The Eyrie score 4 next Evening" is a fact about the board, not an accusation. Our article on [Commander politics](/articles/commander-politics/) makes the same point about another four-player game: threat is easier to discuss when it is visible.

## Deals that keep friends

Deals are part of Root, and the walkthrough even suggests one: as the Vagabond, give a player cards that make items if they promise to craft those items for you. The rules will not enforce it. Your reputation will.

A few habits keep deals friendly:

- **Make deals in the open.** Side whispers make the rest of the table nervous.
- **Say how long a deal lasts.** "I won't attack your roost this turn" is clearer than "we're friends."
- **Announce a broken deal before you break it.** If you change your mind, say so before you act, not after.

## Kingmaking in Root

Kingmaking happens when a player who cannot win decides who does. In Root it shows up in the final turns: a player far behind chooses whether to attack the Eyrie or the Marquise, and that choice picks the winner. The Vagabond's coalition is a built-in version, since he ties his fate to the last-place player.

You cannot remove it from a game this interactive, but you can make it fair. Agree on a norm before the first turn. One option: if you cannot win, play for your own best score, and if your score does not change either way, hit whoever is closest to 30. Our article on [kingmaking in board games](/articles/kingmaking-board-games/) covers more ways to handle it calmly.

Tournaments make their own choices here. The 2026 Root event at the World Boardgaming Championships did not allow coalitions at all. If your group finds coalitions cause more arguments than fun, that is a reasonable house rule to borrow.

## Talk about the board, not the person

The best Root tables talk constantly and stay friends. Point at pieces and numbers. Leave out grudges from the last game. When the game ends, talk about the turn it was decided, and who could have stopped it.
