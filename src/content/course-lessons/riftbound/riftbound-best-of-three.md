Most competitive Riftbound is played as best-of-three matches: the first player to win two games takes the match. Between games, a few decisions happen that never come up in a single casual game. Strong players make them on purpose, before the match starts.

## Battlefields across a match

In a best of three, you choose your battlefield for each game instead of picking at random. The rules from the Core Rules' match mode:

- Each player chooses one of their three battlefields, and both are revealed together.
- **After a game someone won, both battlefields used in it are removed** for the rest of the match. You must choose from your remaining ones.
- **If a game is a draw,** the same battlefields are used again.
- If a game ends in a Game Loss penalty after battlefields were revealed, players must use different battlefields for the following games.

So if a match goes to three games, you will almost always use all three of your battlefields once. The question is the order.

**Rank your battlefields for each matchup.** Before the event, decide which of your three is best against each common deck, and which is worst. Then:

- **Game 1:** you know little about your opponent unless the store runs open decklists, so play the battlefield that helps your own plan most in general.
- **Games 2 and 3:** you now know their deck. Play your best remaining battlefield for that matchup, and remember your opponent's choice also shrinks. Notice which of theirs are still left.

A battlefield that is excellent for your plan but weak against one popular deck is fine. You can save it for a game where it shines.

## Going first or second

For the first game, a randomly chosen player decides who goes first. In playoffs at competitive events, the higher-ranked player from the Swiss rounds decides. After each game, the loser of that game chooses for the next one. If a game was a draw, the turn order stays the same.

Which should you pick? In its October 15, 2026 post on testing start-of-game rules, Riot said that going first currently wins more often than it would like, and it is testing changes aimed at a closer split. So under today's rules, going first is the sensible default. Consider going second only if your deck badly wants the extra rune on turn one, and test that choice in practice before relying on it.

## Sideboarding, by the rules

At events that allow one, you register a sideboard of up to 10 cards. The Tournament Rules set how you use it:

- **Not in game 1.** You start every match with your registered Main Deck.
- **One for one.** In constructed, swap sideboard cards with Main Deck cards one for one. Your deck stays at exactly 40.
- **You may change your Chosen Champion** to another that matches your Legend, from your Main Deck or sideboard.
- **You may not change** your runes, Legend, or battlefields after registration.
- **Not after a draw.** If a game was drawn, neither player may sideboard for the next game.
- **Reset after each match.** Before the first game of your next match, put your deck back exactly as registered.
- **Counting is allowed.** You may count the cards in your opponent's sideboard at any time.

## Building a sideboard

A sideboard is a set of answers to specific problems. Good candidates:

- **Answers to a popular deck's key cards,** such as extra removal against a deck of a few big units.
- **Speed for slow matchups,** cheaper units that let you take battlefields before a slow deck sets up.
- **Durability for fast matchups,** units with Tank or Shield, or cards that defend a battlefield well.

Every card in should replace a card that is weak in that matchup. If you can't name the card it replaces, it does not belong in.

## Write the plan down

The Tournament Rules let you use notes you made before the match during sideboarding and battlefield selection, until opening hands are drawn. Use that. For each common matchup, write:

1. **Battlefield order:** your preferred choice in games 2 and 3.
2. **In and out:** the exact cards to swap.
3. **Turn order:** first or second, if it ever differs from your default.

Three lines per matchup, made calmly at home, beat a rushed decision at a table with the clock running.

## Pace across the match

The Tournament Rules recommend 60 minutes for a Swiss round, and that covers all three games. Sideboarding and battlefield choice take time from that clock. With a written plan, they take a minute instead of five, and the minutes you save could decide whether game three finishes.
