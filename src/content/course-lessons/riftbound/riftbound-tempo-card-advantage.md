Welcome to Level 3. You can manage runes, count the race, build a deck, and handle a sealed pool. Advanced play is about seeing the trades underneath every decision. Two ideas from card games in general explain most of them, and Riftbound gives each a twist of its own.

## Card advantage

**Card advantage** means having more useful cards than your opponent: in hand, on the board, or both. You gain it when one of your cards deals with two of theirs, when you draw extra cards, or when your opponent spends cards that do nothing.

In Riftbound, the cleanest source is combat. Because damage is assigned as lethal blows, one well-placed unit or trick can win a fight that kills two enemy units while losing one of yours. Tank, from the [combat lesson](/academy/riftbound/riftbound-combat/), works the same idea from the other side: it forces the attacker to spend damage on it first, so the defender's other units are more likely to survive.

Riftbound has a quiet card advantage rule built into scoring, too. When a conquer would give you the final point but you have not scored every battlefield that turn, you draw a card instead. That card is not a consolation prize; it helps you win the hold next turn.

There is no maximum hand size, so card advantage keeps. A full hand late in the game means options in every showdown.

## Tempo

**Tempo** is about time: acting before your opponent and making each turn count. A player with tempo forces the other to react.

Riftbound's tempo twist is that units enter exhausted. A unit played on your turn 2 moves on turn 3 at the earliest. So every turn you spend without putting units on the board is a turn your opponent's units are a step ahead of yours. That is why cheap units matter so much, and why Accelerate exists: it costs an extra 1 energy and 1 matching power, and the unit enters ready, so it can move the turn you play it.

Ask whether a tempo play leads to points. An Accelerate unit that takes an empty battlefield this turn and holds it next turn has bought you 2 points. One that moves into a fight it can't win has bought nothing.

## Points are the third resource

Unlike many card games, Riftbound does not end when someone runs out of life. It ends at 8 points. That changes how you value cards and turns:

- **A card is worth a point** when it lets you hold a battlefield one more turn or stops your opponent's hold.
- **A turn of tempo is worth a point** when it gets a unit to an empty battlefield first.
- **A two-for-one is worth less** if both units you killed were sitting at base, doing nothing for the race.

The best trades turn cards into points, and points are what win.

## Damage does not last

All units heal at the end of every turn, and surviving units heal after each combat too. So damage that does not kill is usually wasted. A spell that deals 2 damage to a 4 Might unit outside combat leaves it at full health by the end of the turn.

Two lessons follow:

- **Damage spells work best when they finish the job.** Kill a unit outright, or combine damage with a fight in the same turn.
- **Removal is most valuable on units holding battlefields.** Killing the last unit at a battlefield ends your opponent's control there. Killing a unit at their base mostly costs them a card.

## Efficiency: energy for effect

Compare what you pay with what you remove. If a 2 energy spell kills a unit that cost 5, you gained tempo: you spent less of your turn than they spent of theirs. If you used 5 energy to remove a 2 energy unit, the trade went the other way, even if it was necessary.

Necessary trades are fine. The point is to notice them. If you are often spending more than your opponent to answer their cards, your deck may need cheaper answers or more pressure of its own.

## When to choose which

- **When ahead on the board,** play for tempo. Keep adding units and keep them on battlefields. Your opponent has to spend cards to catch up.
- **When behind on the board,** play for card advantage. Make trades that kill more than you lose, and look for the turn to retake a battlefield.
- **Near the end,** play for points. A card that does nothing for the last two points can wait.
