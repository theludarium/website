At Level 1 you learned how a showdown works. Now it is time to play it well. A showdown is the one part of Riftbound where both players act back and forth, and that makes it the part where reading your opponent pays the most.

## Hidden cards, exactly

Cards with **Hidden** have a second way to be played. On your turn, you can pay 1 power of any domain to put a Hidden card facedown at a battlefield you control. A few rules govern it:

- **One per battlefield.** A battlefield can hold only one facedown card.
- **Wait a turn.** Starting on the next turn, including your opponent's turn, the card gains Reaction and you can play it without paying its normal cost.
- **It stays local.** A facedown unit or gear must be played to that battlefield, and a facedown spell's targets must come from that battlefield, unless the card makes that impossible.
- **Keep the battlefield, keep the card.** It stays there only while you control the battlefield. Lose control in a fight and the card is removed and revealed.

You can always play a Hidden card normally instead, for its full cost at normal timing.

## Why hiding is strong

For 1 power now, you get a card you can play later for free, at Reaction speed, right where the fight will happen. That is efficient. But the facedown card also does something before it is ever played: it makes your opponent worry.

A facedown card at your battlefield says "attacking here may cost you." Your opponent can't see what it is, only that it is there. Some opponents will attack the other battlefield instead, or wait a turn to bring more Might, or hold back runes. Each of those is a small win for you, and the card has not been played yet.

That also tells you when to hide. Hide at the battlefield you most want to keep, especially one you will be holding when your opponent reaches 6 or 7 points.

## What you can see

The Core Rules and Tournament Rules make most of the game public, and almost all of it helps you read a showdown:

- **The number of cards in each hand.**
- **Each player's ready and exhausted runes,** and what is in their rune pool.
- **Every card on the board,** including that a facedown card exists at a battlefield.
- **Every trash,** so you can see what has already been used.
- **The score,** and who has focus and priority.

Players do not have to help each other work out what this means, but you can always ask about public information, and you can always ask to read a card on the board.

## Reading a showdown

Before you move into a fight, run through four questions:

1. **How many ready runes does my opponent have?** Zero means no paid tricks at all. Two or three means a real chance.
2. **How many cards in hand?** One card and two runes is a different threat from five cards and two runes.
3. **Is there a facedown card at that battlefield?** If so, assume a free Reaction is possible there.
4. **What did they skip?** A player who passed on playing a unit last turn with runes to spare may be holding a trick.

Then decide how much Might you need to win even if they have something. If you can bring that much, attack. If you can't, consider the other battlefield, or wait a turn.

## When to act and when to pass

The attacker gets focus first, and players take turns until everyone passes in a row. Some timing habits:

- **As the attacker, pass first when you can.** If your Might already wins, make the defender spend a card to change that. Then answer with yours.
- **As the defender, save your trick for after theirs.** A pump played early gives the attacker a chance to answer it with another.
- **Remember that passing is not final.** If your opponent plays a card after you pass, you get focus again.
- **Count the Chain.** When a card is played, Reactions can answer it before it resolves. The last card played resolves first.

## Bluffing fairly

Leaving runes up and placing a facedown card are honest bluffs: the game lets your opponent see the threat but not its contents. Lying about public information is not. The Tournament Rules require players to tell the truth about the state of the game, and pretending to change your mind to draw a reaction out of your opponent counts as angle shooting.
