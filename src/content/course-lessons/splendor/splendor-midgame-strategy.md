By the middle of a Splendor game, every player has a handful of cards and a direction. The opening was about your own engine. The midgame is about turning that engine into points, while watching everyone else's.

## From bonuses to points

There is a shift once you own several cards in your colors. Cheap level 1 cards stop moving you forward much, and level 2 cards in your colors start to cost only a few pieces.

The best midgame buys do two jobs at once: they score points and add a bonus in a target color. A level 2 card worth 2 points in a color your noble wants is often worth more than a 3-point card in a color you will never use again.

## Read the table first

Before your turn, look at each other player:

1. **Their bonuses.** Count their cards by color. That tells you which cards are cheap for them.
2. **Their pieces.** What can they buy right now, with what they hold?
3. **Their hand.** How many reserved cards? A reserve taken face up tells you exactly what they want.
4. **Their noble.** Which noble is closest for them, and how many cards away?

You will often find that the card you want and the card they want are the same one. That changes your turn.

## Denial without wasting turns

Splendor has no attacks, but it has ways to get in someone's way:

- **Buy the card they need.** The best denial helps you too. If it fits your colors, buying it is a full step for you and a lost plan for them.
- **Reserve it.** If you cannot buy it, reserving takes it off the table and gives you a Gold. Only do it when the card matters: it costs your turn and fills a slot.
- **Drain a color.** When a color is low, taking it can leave an opponent short. This works best when you need that color anyway.

Pure denial, a turn that does nothing for you, is a last resort. It is worth it mostly when an opponent is one turn from a big score.

## Racing for nobles

A noble is 3 points to whoever gets there first, and 0 to everyone else. When two players build toward the same noble, the race matters as much as the cards.

Count it: how many more cards of which colors does each of you need? If you are two cards behind, switching to a different noble may be smarter than racing. If you are ahead, buy the card that closes it before they catch up.

Nobles arrive at the end of your turn, after your action. A purchase that completes a noble scores the card and 3 points in one move.

## Gold is your most flexible piece

Gold stands in for any gem. In the midgame, that matters most when a color runs dry. Save Gold for the color you cannot get, rather than spending it on a color sitting in the supply.

There are only 5 Gold pieces in any game. When they run out, you can still reserve, but you get no Gold for it.

## The supply is shared

With fewer players, the supply shrinks: 5 of each gem at three players, 4 at two. At two players, taking two of a color needs all four in the supply, so once either of you holds even one, that option is gone for both of you until it comes back.

This is also why bonuses matter more as the game goes on. When the color you need is gone from the supply, your cards still pay it.

## Two players versus four

At two players, anything that slows your one opponent is a direct gain for you, so denial is worth more. At four players, the table can change three times before your next turn. Long plans built around one specific card are fragile. Flexible plans, and a reserve to protect a key card, work better.

## A midgame checklist

1. Which card scores and builds my colors at once?
2. What can each other player buy right now?
3. Am I winning the race to my noble, or should I switch?
4. Am I holding Gold for a color that is actually scarce?
