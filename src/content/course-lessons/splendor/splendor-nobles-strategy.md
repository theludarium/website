A noble is 3 points that arrive without an action. That is worth as much as a level 2 card costing 6 gems, and you earn it with cards you were buying anyway. At Level 1 you learned to read the nobles at setup and pick colors. This lesson shows the pattern hiding in the noble tiles, and how to use it to pick a race you can actually win.

## Two shapes

The base game has 10 nobles. Each is worth 3 points, and each asks for one of two shapes:

- **Four and four:** 4 bonuses in each of two colors. Five nobles look like this. That is 8 cards.
- **Three, three, three:** 3 bonuses in each of three colors. The other five look like this. That is 9 cards.

Only card bonuses count. Pieces never do.

## The hidden wheel

Put the five colors in a circle in this order: White, Blue, Green, Red, Black, and back to White.

Now look at the nobles. Every four-and-four noble asks for two colors that sit side by side on that circle: White and Blue, Blue and Green, Green and Red, Red and Black, Black and White. Every three-three-three noble asks for three colors in a row: White, Blue, Green; Blue, Green, Red; and so on around the circle. The ten nobles are exactly every pair and every run of three on the wheel.

This is a pattern in the card data, not a rule. But it has real uses:

- **Each color sits on 5 of the 10 nobles,** two of the four-and-four shape and three of the three-three-three shape.
- **Every three-three-three noble contains two four-and-four nobles.** White, Blue, Green overlaps with White and Blue, and with Blue and Green.
- **Colors across the wheel never share a noble.** White and Green, for example, never appear on the same tile.

## Building the middle

Say you own 3 White, 3 Blue, and 1 Green bonus. Look at what is in reach:

- White, Blue, Green (three each): 2 more Green cards.
- White and Blue (four each): 1 more White and 1 more Blue.
- Black, White, Blue (three each): 3 more Black cards.

Two nobles are each two cards away, and they want different cards. If a rival grabs the Green you need, you pivot to White and Blue without losing a turn. That is the power of building two neighboring colors: they sit inside several nobles at once.

Compare a player with 3 White and 3 Green. Those colors are not neighbors, so no single noble wants both. They need the Blue between them to make that work.

At setup, mark which nobles came out on the wheel. Usually one stretch of two or three neighboring colors covers more nobles than the rest. That stretch is your first guess at target colors, and it often lines up with what the openings lesson told you to look for.

## Counting your distance

For any noble, your distance is the number of cards you still need. Count each required color, take away your bonuses in it, and add up what is left, ignoring colors where you already have enough.

Do the same count for every rival on every noble you care about. Then compare:

- **You are closer:** buy toward it now. Prefer the card that both scores and closes the gap.
- **You are tied:** turn order decides. If you move first, you get there first. If not, look for a card that moves you two steps, or deny the one card they need.
- **You are two or more behind:** switch to a neighbor noble on the wheel, or let nobles go and buy points.

## One per turn, and timing

You can take only 1 noble at the end of a turn. If one card would complete two nobles for you, you get one now and the other next turn, if it is still there. When a rival is also near one of them, take that one first.

A purchase that completes a noble scores both in one turn: the card's points plus 3. Plan those turns as scoring turns. In the final round, that combined jump is often the difference.

Nobles are tiles, not cards. They do not count toward the fewest-cards tiebreaker, which makes a noble the cheapest 3 points in a close finish.

## How many nobles to chase

Our [Splendor strategy guide](/articles/splendor-strategy/) puts it simply: one noble is realistic, two is a great game. The wheel explains why two is possible: neighboring nobles share colors, so the second one often costs only a few extra cards. Planning for three usually spreads you too thin to reach 15 in time.

## The noble checklist

1. Which stretch of the wheel covers the most revealed nobles?
2. How many cards is each player from each noble?
3. Which card closes my gap and scores at once?
4. If I lose this race, which neighbor noble is my fallback?
