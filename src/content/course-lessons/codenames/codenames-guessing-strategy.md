Codenames is often taught as a spymaster's game. It is not. Teams lose more games at the guessing end of the table than at the clue end, usually one hopeful touch at a time. This lesson is about the midgame from the operative's seat: reading clues, managing risk, and keeping your team's pace up.

Our [Codenames clue tips](/articles/codenames-clue-tips/) include a short table of guessing habits. Here we go further into why they work.

## Read your spymaster, not their face

The rules ask operatives to focus on the cards and avoid eye contact with their spymaster. Reading a face is off the table. Reading a person is not.

You know your spymaster. You know what they like, how they talk, and which words they reach for. When the clue is "ocean: 2," ask which two words this person would link to ocean. A sailor and a surfer will mean different words.

## Ask what the clue avoided

A clue is shaped by what the spymaster had to avoid. If an obvious word was left out, there may be a reason.

Say the clue is "pet: 2," and the grid shows DOG, CAT, and MOUSE. Why not "pet: 3"? Maybe one of the three is not yours. Maybe it is the assassin. The clue that was not given is information too.

This is also how the zero clue works under the expert rules. A clue like "feathers: 0" says do not touch the feathered word, and invites you to ask why your spymaster needed to say so.

## Guess the surest word first

Start with the word that fits the clue best. If it turns out wrong, the clue meant something you did not see, and the rest of your guesses are built on sand. Stop.

If it turns out right, you have confirmed your reading. Your next guess can be a little bolder.

## Keep a list of leftovers

Every clue has a number. Every time your team finds fewer words than the number, a word is left over. Keep track.

- "Ocean: 2" found one word. One ocean word is still out there.
- "Music: 3" found three. Nothing left.

The rules say your spymaster cannot remind you of missed words. They can only repeat an old clue if you ask. Your team has to remember. One player can keep a quiet mental list, or just repeat the leftovers out loud at the start of each turn.

You may guess toward an old clue at any point in a turn, even with your first guess.

## Use the plus one guess on purpose

The plus one rule lets you make one more guess than the number, as long as every guess so far was right. It is not a free swing at a word that might fit. It is the way you collect a leftover.

A good turn might look like this: the clue is "night: 2," you find two night words, then use your third guess on the ocean word you missed two turns ago. That is three words from one clue, and no risk taken on a hunch.

If there are no leftovers, the extra guess is usually not worth it.

## When the right word is not the one meant

Sometimes your team touches a word that is yours, but not one your spymaster meant. The rulebook is clear about what the spymaster does: cover it and act as though it was intended all along. That is good luck, and your turn continues.

It also means the word your spymaster wanted is still on the grid. Add it to the leftover list in your head.

## Talk, then touch

The rulebook asks operatives to discuss a bit, then guess, and not let debate stall the game. A good rhythm for a team:

1. Everyone names the words they think fit.
2. The team agrees on the surest one.
3. Someone touches it.

Big teams can slow down here. If the debate goes in circles, the player who touches the card makes the call. Our article on [analysis paralysis](/articles/analysis-paralysis-board-games/) has more on keeping a table moving.

## Tempo: you are in a race

Codenames is a race. Each turn your team spends on one word is a turn the other team can spend on two. That pressure is why stopping early feels bad. It is still often right.

The question to ask before every extra guess: what does it cost if we are wrong? Early in the game, a bystander costs you one turn. The other team's word costs you a turn and gives them a word. The assassin costs the game. Weigh the guess against the worst word it could be.

When you are behind, the math changes. Level 2 works through it: the [race math](/academy/codenames/codenames-odds-and-tempo/) first, then the [endgame](/academy/codenames/codenames-endgame/), where it changes most.
