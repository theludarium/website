Your first three games of Codenames are where a table's habits form. Some of those habits are rules slips, quietly played wrong because nobody checked. Others are legal choices that lose games. This lesson covers both, checked against the current 2025 rulebook from Czech Games Edition.

If you need a refresher on the rules first, start with [how to play Codenames](/academy/codenames/how-to-play-codenames/).

## Rules slips to catch early

**Extra words with the clue.** A clue is one word and one number. "Ocean: 2, but think older" is not a clue, and neither is a long sigh before it. The rulebook puts it neatly: Codenames is always a bit of a stretch, so there is no need to say so.

**Reacting to guesses.** Spymasters keep a straight face while their team talks. They also wait to reach for a tile until a card has been touched. A hand hovering over the blue pile tells the table plenty.

**Watching the spymaster.** Operatives look at the cards, not at their spymaster. Reading a face is not a skill this game rewards. It breaks the game for everyone.

**Reminding the team of old clues.** A spymaster may not point out a missed word or bring up earlier clues. They may repeat an old clue only when someone asks.

**Forgetting the plus one rule.** As long as your team keeps guessing right, it may make one more guess than the clue's number. New tables often stop at the number every time and never use the extra guess.

**Stopping before guessing.** A team must make at least one guess each turn. You can stop after any right guess, but not before the first.

**Saying a form of a visible word.** With BREAK on the table, "broken" and "breakdown" are out. With HORSESHOE on the table, "horse" and "shoe" are out. Once a card is covered, its word is fair game.

**Arguing over the penalty.** An invalid clue ends that team's turn with no guesses, and the other spymaster may cover one of their own words. The other team can also wave the clue through. If nobody noticed until later, it stands.

## The big mistake: greedy clues

New spymasters see four words that sort of fit together and say "four." The team gets two, then touches the other team's word, and the turn is gone. Worse, the stretch that reaches the fourth word often reaches the assassin too.

A clue for two that lands both words is worth more than a clue for four that lands one. It keeps your turn clean, keeps the other team's words covered by them and not by you, and builds trust. Our [Codenames clue tips](/articles/codenames-clue-tips/) explain the math behind that.

## Guessing too far

The mirror mistake belongs to operatives. The clue was for two, the team found two, and now everyone feels lucky. The third guess is a coin flip, and a wrong one can hand the other team a word or end the game.

A third guess on a clue for two is not a bonus round. It is a tool for picking up a word you missed earlier. If no earlier word is left over, stop.

## One voice making every guess

Big teams drift toward one confident player who reads every clue and touches every card. Quieter players stop talking, and the team plays worse, because the spymaster gave the clue for the whole team, not for one person.

The rulebook asks teams to make sure everyone gets to play. A simple fix: pass the touch around. Whoever touches the card has to say why first.

## A frozen spymaster

The first clue of a game can take a couple of minutes, and the rulebook says so. That is normal. What hurts is a spymaster who keeps searching for the perfect clue on every turn. A good clue for two, said now, beats a brilliant clue for three, said five minutes from now.

If your spymaster stalls, nobody should rush them out loud. Agree before the game on a rough pace instead, or try a timer from the rulebook's suggested app once the table knows the game.

## Treating a wrong word as a total loss

Touching the other team's word feels terrible, and new teams often tilt after it. They guess faster, argue more, and make it worse. A wrong word ends one turn. It does not end the game. The assassin is the only card that does that.

## A quick checklist for game three

Spymasters, before each clue:

1. Does this clue reach the assassin in any way?
2. Does it reach the other team's words?
3. Is the number honest?

Operatives, before each touch:

1. Is this the word we are surest of?
2. Do we have a leftover from an earlier clue?
3. Is the next guess worth the risk?

That habit alone fixes most of what goes wrong in the first three games.
