Clues for two are the backbone of Codenames, and Level 1 told you to give them most of the time. That advice still holds. But a team that never gives a three leaves a word on the table, and the [next lesson](/academy/codenames/codenames-odds-and-tempo/) shows why the starting team almost always needs at least one bigger clue to win the race.

This lesson is about how to give that clue without losing the game for it.

## The fit count

The test for any clue, and a strict one for clues for three, is the fit count. Before you speak, list every word on the grid that the clue could reach. All of them, not just yours.

Then compare that list with your number:

- **Fit count equals your number, all yours.** A clean clue. Give it.
- **Fit count is one more, and the extra is a bystander.** Playable. The worst case is that your turn ends on the bystander.
- **Fit count is one more, and the extra is the other team's word.** Risky. Your team may find two and hand them a third.
- **The assassin is anywhere on the list.** Drop the clue, however good it is.

Spymasters who skip the fit count are counting words they want. Operatives count words that fit. Those are different lists, and the gap between them is where clues for three go wrong.

## Finding the overlap

Clues for three rarely come from a category that holds three of your words and nothing else. They usually come from overlap: one clue that reaches each word through a different kind of link.

Say your words include PHONE, BELL, and BOXER. No category holds all three. But "ring" reaches PHONE through what it does, BELL through what you do to it, and BOXER through a second meaning: the ring a boxer fights in. Three links, one word.

That is the craft: hold each of your words in mind and ask what each one could be called, used for, or paired with. Where those lists cross, a bigger clue lives. The [kinds of clues lesson](/academy/codenames/codenames-clue-types/) gives you the vocabulary for this.

## Narrow beats clever

A clue that is clever often reaches through a long chain of thought. Your team will follow chains too, and their chains will not match yours. A three built on one plain link per word is safer than a three that needs a story to explain.

The test from Level 1 still holds: if you have to explain the link to yourself, it is a two.

## Plan the board, not the turn

A bigger clue changes the shape of the rest of the game. Before you give one, look at what it leaves behind.

- **Does it strand a word?** If your three uses the only partner your fourth word had, that fourth word may need a clue for one later. That can be fine, but plan for it.
- **Does it clear danger?** A three that covers the words sitting near the assassin makes every later clue easier.
- **Does it use your easy words?** Your clearest pair might be better saved as a clean two, with the three built from harder words that need each other.

With nine words, one clue for three and three clues for two finish in four turns. With eight, four clues for two do it. Map your words to that kind of plan in the first minute, and the opening clue becomes a step in it.

## Clues for four

Fours happen. They are safest when:

- The grid has shrunk and the danger words are already covered.
- Your team has read this kind of link right all game.
- The fit count is exactly four, and you checked it twice.

Remember the plus one rule. A clue for four allows up to five guesses, so a confident team may take a fifth swing at a leftover. If there are no leftovers, that is not your worry, but if there is a word nearby that fits loosely, the fifth guess may find it.

## When three goes wrong

Some threes will find two words and a bystander. That is not a disaster. Your team found two, which is what a clue for two would have done, and the third word is now a leftover your team knows about. On a later turn, the plus one guess can pick it up.

The real loss is a three that finds one word and the other team's word. That is why the fit count matters more than the link. If the other team's word is on the list, the clue is not a three.

## Practice

Try this away from the table. Lay out a grid of 25 cards and pick nine at random as yours, plus one as the assassin. Give yourself five minutes to find the best three on the grid, then run the fit count with care. Most of the time you will find a strong two and a weak three. Learning to tell them apart is the skill this lesson is about.
