Random setup is part of Scythe's charm, and the rulebook says the higher-numbered mats get slightly better starting tracks to balance turn order. But serious players want a fair start, and a random deal will now and then hand one player a pairing everyone else would have chosen. The answer in competitive Scythe is an auction. This lesson covers what Stonemaier's guidelines say, then how to price a pairing.

## What the tournament guidelines say

Stonemaier publishes suggested guidelines for Scythe tournaments on its site. It is clear that there is no official Scythe tournament organization, so the guidelines are for fans, clubs, stores, and conventions. For setup, they suggest:

- **Pair at random.** Deal faction mats and player mats into random pairs for each game.
- **No banned pairs.** Rusviet with Industrial and Crimea with Patriotic are not allowed. Reshuffle if one comes up.
- **Auction the pairs.** Players bid coins for the pairing they want, and the winning bid comes off their final score.
- **No repeats.** A Gen Con rule the guidelines recommend: once you have played a faction, you cannot bid on or use it again in later rounds.

The guidelines also rule out in-game trading for tournament play.

The guidelines do not set a bidding order, a minimum bid, or a timer. A comment from Jamey Stegmaier on that page suggests a simpler home version: deal each player a random player mat, then bid on the faction mats, one bid per player per faction, with the starting bidder changing each time.

## Running an auction at home

Because the procedure is open, pick one and agree on it before you start. Two that work:

**Round robin.** Lay out the pairings. Starting with a random player, each player in turn either raises the bid on one pairing or passes. When everyone passes on a pairing, it goes to the high bidder. Keep going until everyone has one.

**Sealed bids.** Each player writes a bid for every pairing on paper. Reveal together. The highest single bid takes its pairing, then repeat with what is left. Agree on a tiebreaker first, such as the lowest player mat number.

Either way, write the bids down. They come off at the end, after scoring.

## Pricing a pairing

A bid is a promise to give up coins at the end. So the question is: how many coins will this pairing earn me that the others will not?

The cleanest way to answer is in turns. Most of the value of a strong pairing is speed: it reaches six stars, or a strong position, a turn or two sooner. Work out what one of your turns is worth:

> coins per turn = your usual final score / your usual number of turns

If your recent games ended around $70 after about 20 of your turns, a turn is worth about $3 to $4 to you. A pairing you expect to save one turn is worth about that much. A pairing that saves two is worth twice that. Use your own numbers. Strong players earn more per turn, so their bids can be higher.

## What makes a pairing worth more

Look at the same things the [player mats lesson](/academy/scythe/scythe-player-mats/) covered, plus the table:

- **Turn order.** A low mat number acts sooner, and in a race that is worth something.
- **A section that takes both actions early.** The sooner you can take a top and bottom action every turn, the faster your engine.
- **A cheap bottom action that feeds your faction.** Polania with cheap encounter travel, Crimea with Bolster paired to a bottom action it wants, Saxony with a path to its objectives.
- **Neighbors.** A pairing that sits between two passive neighbors is worth more than one between Saxony and a fast Rusviet.

## Bidding mistakes

- **Bidding on the faction you like.** Bid on the pairing you can play best, not the one with your favorite mech.
- **Forgetting the bid at the end.** Track it on paper, not in memory.
- **Bidding to deny.** Pushing up a rival's price is legal, but if they pass, you own a pairing at a price you did not want.
- **Ignoring the cheap seats.** A pairing nobody wants often goes for $0, and a sound plan on a weaker pairing can beat an overpriced strong one.
