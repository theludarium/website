Kingdomino has no attacks, no stealing, and no way to touch another kingdom. Its only interaction is the draft. That makes the draft the place to read the table. Every kingdom sits face up in front of its owner, and every need shows. A player who looks only at their own kingdom is leaving half the game on the table.

## What to look for in a rival's kingdom

Glance at each kingdom before every pick and note three things:

1. **Their biggest crowned territory.** That is where a new crown or square is worth the most to them. A rival with a 9-square forest and 3 crowns gains 3 points from every plain forest square and 13 from a crowned one.
2. **Their open space.** A rival whose 5 by 5 is nearly full needs pieces that fit. A domino that cannot fit their gaps is no threat in their hands.
3. **Their turn position.** Who picks before you and who picks after. You can only take a domino away from someone who picks after you.

## Pricing a hate draft

Taking a domino mostly to keep it from someone else is called a hate draft. It is a sound move when the numbers say so, and a vanity move when they do not.

Compare two things in points:

- **What the domino is worth to the rival** who would otherwise get it.
- **What you give up** by not taking your own best domino.

An example. Your best option is worth 6 points to you. Another domino in the line is worth 2 points to you, but its crowned lake square would join a rival's 10-square lake that has 1 crown: 10 + 1 + 1 = 12 points for them, as lesson 4's shortcut shows. Taking it costs you 4 points and denies them 12. In a two-player game, that is an 8-point swing in your favor. In a four-player game, the math is softer: you spent 4 points to slow one of three rivals, and the other two gained nothing either way.

So hate drafting is strongest with two players, reasonable with three, and only worth it with four when one rival is clearly leading or the denied domino is a big one.

## Read the order, not just the tiles

Who picks after you matters as much as what is in the line.

- **The last player in a four-player round** gets no choice at all: they take the one domino left. If you know which domino they need, and you pick just before them, you can leave them the one that does not fit.
- **The player who picks first** next round is the one sitting on the lowest number now. If the leader is about to pick first, the rich domino in the next line is probably theirs. Plan around it.
- **You cannot deny anyone who picks before you.** Do not waste effort worrying about what they will take.

## What rivals are reading in you

Your kingdom is just as public. A few habits make you harder to read and harder to block:

- **Keep two growth options.** A kingdom with one big territory and nothing else is easy to starve. One with a strong forest and a promising swamp makes every rival guess.
- **Avoid the single-domino need.** If only one domino in the box completes your plan, a careful rival will take it. Plans with several possible pieces survive.
- **Do not announce.** Saying "I need that mine" out loud invites a hate draft. Some tables enjoy table talk; know yours.

## Reading the tiebreaker

Ties are broken by the biggest single territory, then by the most crowns. In a close game, look at your largest territory against the leader's. Adding one plain square to your biggest territory can win a tie you were about to lose, even when it scores less than another option.

## Keep it friendly

Kingdomino is often a family game, and a hate draft against a child or a new player can sting. At a teaching table, play your own kingdom and let the draft teach itself. Save the sharp reads for tables that want them. Our article on [family game night rules](/articles/family-game-night-rules/) has more on matching your play to your table.
