Every Carcassonne player gets the same seven meeples. What changes from game to game is how well those seven get used. A strong player and a new player can draw similar tiles all game and finish thirty points apart, and most of that gap is meeples: where they went, how long they stayed, and how many were sitting in the supply at the end doing nothing.

This lesson treats meeples as what they are: a budget.

## How many turns do you get?

The base game has 71 land tiles to draw after the start tile. Each turn uses one, so the number of turns you get depends on the player count. If no tile is ever returned to the box, it works out like this:

| Players | Turns per player |
| --- | --- |
| 2 | 36 for the first player, 35 for the second |
| 3 | 24 or 23 |
| 4 | 18 or 17 |
| 5 | 15 or 14 |

<!-- VERIFY: the 71 drawable tiles assume the 2021 box has the same 72-tile base set as earlier editions; confirm against the tile overview in the box. -->

Now compare that to your seven meeples. In a two-player game you have about five turns for every meeple. In a five-player game you have about two.

That ratio is the heart of meeple management. **The more turns you have per meeple, the more each meeple has to cycle.** In a two-player game, a meeple that gets stuck early costs you many future placements. In a five-player game, you will rarely run out, but each of your few turns matters more, so a turn spent placing nothing hurts.

## What does a stuck meeple cost?

A meeple on a feature that never finishes still scores something at the end: half value for a city, full value for a road or monastery. The real cost is the turns it could have spent working.

Think of it this way. A knight that sits in a big open city for twenty turns is a knight that could have closed four small cities in that time. If each of those would have scored 4 to 6 points, the stuck knight cost you far more than its city is worth at the end.

So when you place a meeple, ask two questions, not one:

1. How many points will this score?
2. How long will this meeple be away?

## The habits that keep meeples cycling

**Prefer features with one or two open edges.** A city with one open side has many tiles that can close it. A city with four open sides needs four good draws.

**Use the free placement.** If the tile you place completes a feature that nobody has claimed, put a meeple on it. It scores in step 3 and comes straight back. That is points for no meeple cost at all.

**Keep a reserve.** Two meeples in the supply is a comfortable midgame. It means that when a great tile appears, such as an early monastery or a chance to join a rival's big city, you can take it.

**Finish your own features first.** When your supply runs low, the next few tiles should close things you already own rather than start new ones.

**Count farmers as spent.** A farmer never comes back during the game. Each one shrinks your budget for every remaining turn, which is why the [Carcassonne strategy guide](/articles/carcassonne-strategy/) suggests no farmers in the first quarter of the game.

## How the count changes over a game

Meeple management has three phases.

- **Early:** cycle fast. Short roads and small cities, finished quickly, keep most of your seven in play and returning.
- **Middle:** commit carefully. This is when a larger city, a monastery, or a first farmer makes sense, because you know where the board is going.
- **Late:** empty the supply. When the last tile goes down, meeples in your supply score nothing. In the final few turns, the question flips from "will this come back?" to "will this score anything at all?"

That last phase is easy to miss. A player who managed meeples well all game can still leave three in the supply at the end. Each of those could have been a farmer on a small field or a traveler on an open road.

## A quick self-check

Count these at the end of your next game:

- **Meeples left in your supply.** Ideally zero.
- **Meeples stranded on features that never finished.** Fewer is better, and each one is a lesson about which features to avoid.
- **Turns you placed no meeple because you had none.** This is the number that hurts most, and the one these habits fix.

Over a few games, those three numbers improve together. When they do, your scores rise even when your tiles do not.
