Carcassonne with two players is a different game from Carcassonne with four. The rules are identical, but the math underneath changes, and it is the version tournaments play. The [Carcassonne World Championship](https://carcassonne-meisterschaft.de/en/tournamentrules.htm) is played at two-player tables, and for 2026 the World Boardgaming Championships event switched to [two-player games only](https://www.boardgamers.org/wbc26/Previews/car.html).

If you want to compete, this is the game to learn.

## What changes with two players

**You get many turns.** With 71 tiles to draw, the first player places 36 and the second places 35, if no tile is returned to the box. That is about five turns for each of your seven meeples, so meeples cycle more and plans can run longer.

**The starting player places the last tile.** With an odd number of tiles and nothing returned to the box, the last draw falls to the first player. Both players should know this going into the final turns, because it decides who gets the last word on a contested field or city.

**Every point is relative.** With four players, scoring 10 while one rival scores 10 still puts you ahead of two others. With two, it changes nothing. Only the gap matters.

## The swing test

Judge each placement by its swing: what it does for you, minus what it does for your opponent.

- **Closing your own city for 12:** a swing of 12.
- **Closing a city you share equally:** a swing of 0. You both score the same. It can still be right, because both knights come home, but it gains you nothing on the scoreboard.
- **Joining a city your opponent would have scored alone for 20:** a swing of 20. They still score 20, but now you do too.
- **Taking that city with a second knight:** a swing of 40. You score 20, and they score nothing.
- **Blocking their 14-point city so it can never close:** it scores 7 for them at the end instead of 14. A swing of 7, and their knight stays stuck all game.

That last line explains why blocking is stronger in a duel. In a multiplayer game, a turn spent blocking one rival helps the other players as much as you. With two players, every point you deny is a point gained in the only comparison that counts.

## Never close a rival's feature for free

In a multiplayer game, closing someone else's city is sometimes fine if it also helps you. In a duel, closing your opponent's feature hands them points and returns their meeple. Do it only when the swing still favors you, for example when the city you close also raises a field you hold by more than it pays them.

The same thinking applies to farms. Closing a city adds 3 points to every field it touches. If your opponent holds those fields, you are feeding them.

## The starting player's edge

Going first is a small advantage. The Catalan community site Carcassonne a Catalunya looked at [599 two-player games](https://www.carcassonne.cat/2020/12/11/does-the-starting-player-have-an-advantage-in-carcassonne/) from the 2020 European team online championship, played with the base game. The starting player won 52.25% of them, lost 44.74%, and drew the rest, averaging about 2 points more than the second player. Average scores were a little over 90 points each.

The World Championship balances this in its own way: under its rules, a tied game is a loss for the starting player.

For you, the lesson is practical. Two points is small. Over a long event, it evens out as you start some games and not others. Do not play differently because you went second; play the swing.

## How long plans change

With 35 turns, plans that would be reckless in a five-player game become reasonable:

- **Bigger cities** are more likely to close, because you get more draws.
- **Monasteries** have more time to fill, and you will usually draw about three of the six yourself.
- **Field fights** develop slowly, so you have time to set up a second farmer or cut a gap.

But your opponent has the same time. A big open city that you can wait for is also one they can wait to join. The longer a feature stays open, the more chances they get.

## Tempo with one opponent

Meeple tempo still matters, perhaps more. Your opponent's supply is visible, and so is yours. When you have meeples and they have none, they cannot place a meeple on their next turn, so nothing you start now can be contested right away. That is the moment to open a new city or start a farm.

When the situation is reversed, close things. Get your meeples home before your opponent can use the gap.

## A quick duel checklist

Before each placement:

1. What is the swing of my best move?
2. Does this help my opponent anywhere, including their fields?
3. Is there a block worth more than my best build?
4. Who places the last tile, and does that change my plan?
