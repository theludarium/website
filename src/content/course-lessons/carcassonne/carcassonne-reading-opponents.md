Carcassonne has very little hidden information. The map, every meeple, both supplies, and the score track are all in plain view. The only secret is the next tile. That means a careful player can know most of what an opponent wants, and much of what they are planning, before they do it.

This lesson is about reading the board through your opponent's eyes.

## Read the supply first

Your opponent's meeple supply tells you what they can do this turn and next.

- **Zero meeples:** they cannot place a meeple on their next turn. Anything you start now cannot be contested right away.
- **One meeple:** they will spend it only on something important. Expect it to go to a big city, a key field, or a block.
- **Four or more:** they are free to contest anything. Be careful about leaving a big feature open beside empty space.

Also watch their supply over several turns. A player who stops placing meeples while holding several is usually saving them for something, often a field fight late in the game.

## Read their open features

For each of your opponent's open features, ask what it needs.

1. **Which spaces would close it?**
2. **What shape fits each space?**
3. **How many of those tiles are left?** The [tile counting lesson](/academy/carcassonne/carcassonne-tile-counting/) shows how.

Now you know which tiles they are hoping for. That changes how you play your own draws. When you draw a tile that would close their feature, you get to decide where it goes. Place it somewhere it helps you, and you have quietly removed one of their fits from the game. Every fit you draw and use elsewhere is one fewer for them.

## Read their setups

Most attacks in Carcassonne take two steps, and the first step is visible.

**A separate city piece next to yours, with their knight on it.** That is a steal in progress. The next tile that connects the two makes the city shared. Your answers: close your city before the merge, or fill the gap with a tile that keeps the two cities apart, or prepare a second knight of your own.

**A farmer on a small field next to your big field.** The same idea, for fields. If a later tile joins the two fields, you share the field or lose it. Your best answer is often to cut the gap with a road or city wall while you still can. The [farming lesson](/academy/carcassonne/carcassonne-farmer-strategy/) covers this in detail.

**A tile placed beside your open feature with no meeple on it.** This is often a block. Look at the space it leaves next to your feature. If it now needs a rare shape, recount your fits before you invest any more in that feature.

The earlier you spot the first step, the cheaper the answer.

## Read what they did not do

Placements tell you something, and so do the moves an opponent passed up.

- **They had a chance to close their city and did not.** They may be trying to grow it for more points, or they may be keeping it open so it does not feed your field.
- **They left a strong spot for a farmer empty.** They may be short on meeples, or waiting to place it late.
- **They placed a tile far from everything.** Sometimes that is the only legal spot. Sometimes it is a tile that would have helped you anywhere else.

You will not always know which reason is true. But asking the question keeps you from being surprised.

## Read the score

The score track tells you how your opponent will play the rest of the game.

- **A player ahead** tends to close things, avoid risk, and defend.
- **A player behind** has to take chances: contest the biggest field, join the biggest city, and block whatever the leader needs.

In a two-player game, this is especially clear. If you are ahead late, expect an attack on your largest feature or field, and get there first.

## In tournament play

Under the [World Championship rules](https://carcassonne-meisterschaft.de/en/tournamentrules.htm), a player may choose and secretly look at their next tile during the opponent's turn, and supplies must stay visible at all times. So your opponent often arrives at their turn with a plan already made. Your read of their needs, done during your own turn, is how you stay a step ahead of it.

## The reading habit

Each turn, before you look at your own tile:

1. How many meeples does my opponent have?
2. What does each of their open features need?
3. Is any two-step attack already half done?
4. Where are they on the score track, and how will that shape their next move?

The best players do this so often it becomes background. Start by doing it on purpose.
