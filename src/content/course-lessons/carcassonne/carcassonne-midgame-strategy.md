By the middle of a Carcassonne game, the map is crowded with half-built cities, roads that wander, and monasteries with gaps around them. The opening was about your own tempo. The midgame is about everyone's. This is where Carcassonne turns from a puzzle into a contest.

## Read the table first

Before you look at where your tile fits, look at the board and ask three questions:

1. **What does each player need?** Which of their features is close to finishing, and what shape closes it?
2. **Where are the big points?** Which city is largest? Which field touches the most finished cities?
3. **Who is short on meeples?** A player with an empty supply cannot contest anything this turn.

Now look at your tile. The best spot is often not where it fits your own feature best, but where it changes the answer to one of those questions.

## Joining a city that isn't yours

You cannot place a knight on a city that already has one. But the rules let separate features merge. When they do, the player with the most meeples on the combined feature scores it, and tied players all score the full points.

So the classic move works like this:

1. An opponent has a big, open city.
2. You place a tile with a separate city piece next to it, and put a knight on that piece.
3. A later tile connects the two. Now you each have one knight in the same city.

That turns their 20-point city into a 20-point city for both of you. If you can bring a second knight in through another merge, you take it alone.

Sharing is not a loss. Look at it from the leader's view: a shared city gives the trailing player the same points the leader gets. That is the cleanest way to close a gap.

## Defending your own city

If you own a big city, look at every open edge. Each one is a door.

- **Close it fast.** A finished city cannot be joined. Taking 10 points now can beat hoping for 16 later.
- **Fill the gap with your own tiles.** A tile that touches the opening without leaving room for a rival city piece shuts the door.
- **Accept the share.** If someone has already joined, help the city finish. Shared points are still points, and your knight comes home.

## Blocking with awkward tiles

Some draws do nothing for you. Those are your blocking tiles.

Place them next to an opponent's open feature so that the gap they leave is a shape that rarely appears, or a shape no tile has. A city opening boxed in on several sides by fields and roads may never close, and their knight stays stuck there all game.

Blocking is legal, normal, and part of the game. It also has a cost: you spent a turn not building. Block when the feature is big, or when the player is leading.

## Your first farmer

The midgame is when farmers start to make sense. Look for a field that:

- touches several cities that are finished or close to finishing,
- will be hard for others to reach without a merge, and
- is not already claimed.

One farmer on the right field can be worth 12, 15, or more points at the end. Watch for rivals doing the same thing: fields merge just like cities. A rival can start a separate farmer nearby and connect the fields later.

## Managing your meeple budget

A comfortable midgame usually means two or three meeples in your supply. Fewer than that and you start missing good tiles. More than that and you may be too cautious.

If you are at zero, your next few tiles should finish your own features, not start new ones. If you are at five or six, you can afford a farmer or a merge attempt.

## The two-player game is different

With two players, every tile you draw is the other player's tile too, in a sense: anything you leave open, they can use. Blocking and joining matter more, because every point you take from your one opponent is a swing. With five players, the board changes more between your turns, so long plans are riskier and fast features are worth more.
