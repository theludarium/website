The rules of Carcassonne do not change at a tournament. The procedures around them do. A clock runs. Tiles cannot be taken back. Your meeples sit where your opponent can see them. And at most events, you cannot write anything down.

This lesson walks through the [World Championship tournament rules](https://carcassonne-meisterschaft.de/en/tournamentrules.htm), the procedures for the final itself, then the main differences at the World Boardgaming Championships. Always read the rules for your own event; organizers can and do vary them. <!-- VERIFY: check the current US National Carcassonne Championship rules on its Board Game Arena group for its timer and format, and add a line on how they differ. -->

## The game itself

- **Base game only,** played from the standard box. No River, no Abbot, no expansions.
- **Two players** at every table.
- **Current scoring.** The World Championship rules point out that a completed two-tile city scores 4 points, not the 2 that some older editions gave. Farmers score 3 points per completed city for each field, and one city can pay out for several fields.

## The clock

Each player has **15 minutes for the whole game** on a chess clock. If you run out, you lose, even if you are ahead. Both players record the score at the moment of the timeout, and the sheet is marked "timeout."

You press the clock **after** your move is complete, including scoring. That means you count your own points and your opponent's on your own time.

What 15 minutes means in practice: a two-player game gives each player about 35 turns. Fifteen minutes across 35 turns is roughly 25 seconds a turn, scoring included. That is plenty for most turns and very little for a hard endgame. The habit that saves you is spending a few seconds on easy turns early, so you have minutes left when the last fifteen tiles arrive.

## Your next tile

During your opponent's turn, you **may choose and secretly look at your next tile**. It must stay above the tabletop the whole time, so it is clear which tile you chose. At the start of your turn, show it to your opponent before you place it.

This is the clock's best friend. Use your opponent's turn to plan your own. When your turn starts, you should already know where your tile is going.

## Placing tiles and meeples

- **A tile is final once you let go of it.** So is a meeple. There are no take-backs.
- **If your tile scores and you want a meeple on it,** place the meeple from your supply before you count the points. That shows your opponent you had one available.
- **Your supply stays visible at all times.** No holding meeples in your hand or hiding them.

## Before and around each game

- **Count the tiles** before every game, to make sure none went missing between rounds.
- **The non-starting player chooses the color.** You play with the meeples at the table unless both players agree otherwise.
- **No private notes** during a game, including counting tiles on paper. Recording your game on video is allowed only with your opponent's agreement.
- **Do not talk to visitors** during a game. You may talk with your opponent and the referees.
- **Disputes:** stop the clocks, talk it through, and if you cannot agree, raise a hand for a referee.

## How the event runs

The World Championship final runs six Swiss rounds, pairing players with similar records, then a top eight into quarterfinals, semifinals, a final, and a game for third. The starting player in each Swiss game is the one who has started fewer games so far; in the knockout rounds, it is the player with the better preliminary result.

Two rules shape how you play:

- **A tie is a loss for the starting player,** in every round. If you start, a tie is not good enough.
- **Ties in the standings** are broken by the number of wins your opponents had, with the weakest opponent discarded, then the same count with the best and weakest discarded, then point difference across your six games. Big wins help only as a last tiebreak.

## How WBC differs

The [2026 WBC event](https://www.boardgamers.org/wbc26/Previews/car.html) was also two-player, but it ran differently:

- **Single elimination,** six rounds, a semifinal, and a final, with one-hour rounds.
- **No notes,** but you may look at the tile manifest that comes with the game, or an equivalent tile list, as long as you write nothing down.
- **The first player is chosen randomly,** and a meeple may be placed on the start tile.
- **Ties are broken** by points before final scoring, then most unused meeples, then fewest farmers, then a dice roll.

That last list changes endgame thinking. At WBC, a tie is not a loss for anyone by default, and the tiebreaks reward points scored during the game and meeples kept in reserve.

## Before your first event

Read your event's rules a week ahead, and play a few games under them. The clock, the visible supply, and the no-notes rule all feel strange the first time. They should feel normal before the games count.
