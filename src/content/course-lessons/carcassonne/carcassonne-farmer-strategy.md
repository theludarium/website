Farmers are where Carcassonne turns from a tile puzzle into a territory game. A single field can be worth more than any city on the board, and it is often decided by one well-timed meeple lying on its back. This lesson goes past the basics in the [first lesson](/academy/carcassonne/how-to-play-carcassonne/) and into how fields are won.

## A field has borders, and borders move

A field is a connected area of green. Roads and city walls are its borders. The edge of the map is not a border; it is an open side where the field can keep growing.

That last point matters. Until a field is sealed by roads and cities on every side, any tile placed at its open edge can grow it, connect it to another field, or cut it off from one. Fields change shape all game, which is why reading them takes practice.

Some tiles split fields more than others:

- **A straight road** splits the green on a tile into two sides.
- **A curved road** splits it into a small inner corner and a larger outer side.
- **A junction with three roads** splits it into three parts, and the four-way crossroads into four.
- **A monastery** does not split a field. The field wraps around it.

Trace a field before you place a farmer, every time.

## What is a field worth?

At the end, each field scores 3 points for every completed city it touches, to the player with the most farmers on it. Unfinished cities add nothing.

So a field's value has two parts:

- **What it touches now:** count the completed cities.
- **What it could touch:** count the open cities on its border that are likely to close.

A field touching four completed cities is 12 points today. If three more cities on its border look likely to close, it may be worth 21 by the end.

One more thing to see: every small city completed next to a field adds 3 to that field. A two-tile city scores 4 points for its knight and then adds 3 to every field it touches. Players who hold the big field love small cities.

## When to place a farmer

The [Carcassonne strategy guide](/articles/carcassonne-strategy/) gives a good rhythm: no farmers in the first quarter of the game, one around the midpoint on the field touching the most completed or nearly completed cities, more only to defend or steal, and any spare meeple in the last few tiles.

Within that rhythm, look for a field that is:

- touching several cities that are finished or close,
- likely to keep growing toward more cities, and
- hard for a rival to reach with a separate farmer.

## Taking a field that is not yours

Fields merge just like cities. You cannot place a farmer on a field that already has one, but you can place a farmer on a separate field next to it. When a later tile connects the two, both farmers are on one field.

- **One farmer each** is a tie. You both score the full value.
- **Two farmers to one** takes the whole field.

So the steal is a two-step move. First get a farmer into a separate field that borders the big one. Then connect it, ideally after you have also brought a second farmer in the same way.

## Defending your field

If a rival starts a separate field next to yours, you have three answers:

1. **Cut it off.** Place a tile so that a road or city wall seals the gap between the two fields. Once sealed, they can never join.
2. **Match them.** If the fields will join, bring in a second farmer of your own through another separate field.
3. **Shrink what you are fighting over.** If you are going to lose the field, stop closing cities that feed it.

Cutting is the strongest of the three when it is available, because it costs you a tile but no meeple.

## Closing cities is a farming decision

Every time a city closes, it pays its knights and then raises the value of every field it touches. Before you close a city, look at who holds the fields around it.

- Closing a city next to your own field raises your farm.
- Closing a city next to a rival's field raises theirs.
- Sometimes the best play is to leave a city open so a rival's field stays smaller.

## The checklist

Before placing a farmer, and every few turns after:

1. Where are this field's borders, and which are still open?
2. How many completed cities does it touch, and how many could it?
3. Is there a separate field next to it where a rival could start a farmer?
4. Can I cut that gap with a tile instead of a meeple?

Farmers decide close games. The [area majority explainer](/articles/area-majority-vs-area-control/) covers the thinking behind them if you want the wider view.
