Many Everdell games are played by two: couples, roommates, two friends on a weeknight. The rules barely change at two players, but the strategy does. This lesson covers what is different and how to win the duel.

## What changes at two players

From the rulebook setup:

- **3 Forest cards** come out instead of 4, and each holds one worker. Fewer strong spaces, and only one rival for each.
- **Starting hands are 5 and 6 cards.** The first player starts with one fewer card.
- **Everything else is the same:** 4 basic Events, 4 special Events, 8 Meadow cards, and the same seasons.

What follows from that:

- **The Meadow turns over more slowly.** With one opponent, a card you leave in the Meadow has a better chance of still being there on your next turn than at four players.
- **Every Event is a two-way race.** You only have one city to watch.
- **Blocking is personal.** Every exclusive space you take is a space your one opponent cannot use.

## The swing test

At four players, a move that gives one opponent 2 points is a small cost; the other two opponents gain nothing. At two players, that same 2 points closes the gap between you by 2. Only the gap matters.

So run a swing test: what does this move do for you, minus what it does for your opponent?

**Visiting an Open card.** When you place a worker on an opponent's Open Destination, they gain 1 point token. At two players, that space has to be worth more than a point to you. It often still is, but count it.

**The Monk.** Give up to 2 berries to an opponent for 2 points each. At two players, your opponent gets those berries, which they will turn into cards. Four points for you against 2 berries for them is usually still a good swing, but only if they can't use the berries well right now.

**The Post Office.** You give an opponent 2 cards, then discard any number and draw back to your hand limit. At two players, choose cards your opponent cannot use, because they will get them.

**The Teacher.** Draw 2, keep 1, give the other away. Give your opponent the card that fits their city least.

## Events: race or deny

With one rival, you can see exactly how close they are to each Event. Count their colors after every card they play.

If you both need the same Event, the swing is big: 3 points for a basic Event to one of you, nothing to the other, and the tiebreaker on top. Claim the turn you qualify.

If only they can reach an Event, look at the Meadow for the card they need. Buying it is fair when it also fits your city. Pure denial, a card bought only to keep it from them, costs you a turn and resources, so it is worth it only when the swing is large: the last card of a special Event, or the fourth green card for their Harvest Festival.

## The Meadow as a shared hand

At two players, the Meadow is mostly a hand you share with one person. Track what they want. When you prepare for summer and draw 2 Meadow cards, you can take one you want and one they want. That is a summer draw doing double work.

## Worker spaces

With 3 Forest cards and one worker each, the best Forest card is the most contested space in a two-player game. If you both want it, getting there first each season is worth thinking about, including when you prepare. An early prepare frees your workers so you can take it again next turn, possibly before your opponent has prepared at all.

## Tempo at two

With only two players, you can see every turn your opponent takes. Use it. When they have every worker out, they are about to prepare, and every space they hold is about to open. When they reach autumn, the 5 space on the autumn trail is in play for them. Being one turn ahead of them into autumn can win the trail.

## Practicing two-player

The solo game against Rugwort, covered in the expansions lesson, is set up as a two-player game, and his workers block a Forest card and a basic location. It is a good way to practice two-player spaces without a partner. Year 3 is a real test.

For couples who love the theme, [Everdell Duo](https://tycoongames.com/products/everdell-duo) is a separate standalone game for 1 or 2 players, built for the head-to-head game.
