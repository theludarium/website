Many players run Jaws of the Lion as a solo campaign, and the box supports it: the box says 1 to 4 players, and the scenario book makes setup quick enough for a weeknight. The Learn to Play Guide gives the solo rule in one line: if you play by yourself, you control two separate characters (p. 3). It also warns that this is more complex. This lesson makes it simpler.

## The solo rules

Everything works exactly as in a two-player game. The map uses the two-character row for monsters (p. 4). Each character has its own hand, modifier deck, hit points, items, gold, and battle goal. Gold still cannot move between them, even though you own both (p. 18).

The card selection rule about not sharing numbers no longer matters, since you are both players. That is the one real solo advantage: perfect coordination. The cost is that you have to think for two.

## Picking a pair

Two characters means two of the four roles go missing. Pick a pair whose jobs fit together:

- **Red Guard and Hatchet:** the classic front line and back line. The Red Guard pulls focus and moves monsters into place; the Hatchet throws from safety and loots. This is the easiest pair to learn solo.
- **Demolitionist and Voidwarden:** a damage engine with a healer and action-granter behind it. The Voidwarden can hand the Demolitionist extra attacks and patch it up, which stretches the Demolitionist's 9-card hand.
- **Red Guard and Voidwarden:** durable and slow. Strong when scenarios are long, weaker when you need to kill fast.
- **Demolitionist and Hatchet:** all offense. Fun, fast, and fragile. Better once you know the monsters.

Lesson 4 covers each character's plan in detail.

## A routine for card selection

Running two hands is mostly a bookkeeping problem. A fixed routine solves it:

1. **Read the board first.** For each monster, decide who it will focus on (Lesson 7). Usually you want that to be your front-line character.
2. **Choose "early or late" for each character.** Often the tank goes early to pull focus and the damage dealer goes late to finish.
3. **Pick the front-line character's cards first,** then the second character's, so the second plan fits the first.
4. **Count both clocks.** Note each character's rounds left (Lesson 5). The character with fewer rounds should not spend lost actions early.
5. **Set the cards in two separate rows,** initiative card on top, so you never mix them up.

## Using perfect coordination

Solo play lets you combine turns in ways a group rarely does:

- **Elements on purpose.** Infuse with the first character, consume with the second later in the same round (p. 26).
- **Focus on purpose.** Place the tank where it is the cheapest character to reach, and the second character just behind.
- **Rests on different rounds.** Do not let both characters long rest in the same round. One of them should always be acting.
- **Battle goals together.** You see both goals, so you can set up turns where both are met.

## Solo difficulty

Solo games can feel harder, because two characters have half the actions of four and lose a whole role. The map scales the monster count down, but Normal can still feel steep early. The scenario level dial from Lesson 9 is your friend: drop to Easy for a scenario that stalls you, and climb to Hard once both characters have their perks and items.

## Practice on a screen

The digital Gloomhaven game sells Jaws of the Lion as downloadable content. It came to PC and Mac first, and Cephalofair announced it for PlayStation, Xbox, and Nintendo Switch with the console release in 2023. The app runs the monsters for you, which makes it a handy way to practice a pair and test your focus predictions. It is an adaptation, so treat the printed rules as the final word at the table.
