In Jaws of the Lion you do not lose because a monster rolled well. You lose because you ran out of cards. Hit points matter, but most exhausted characters in a careful group run dry on cards first. This lesson treats your hand as a clock and shows how to read it.

## How a hand runs down

Every round you play two cards. They go to your discard pile, or your lost pile if the action you used has the lost icon. When your hand is low, you rest to take the discards back, and every rest loses one card for the rest of the scenario (p. 12 and p. 22).

The guide's exhaustion rule is short: if at the start of a round you cannot play two cards because you have fewer than two in your hand, your character is exhausted and out of the scenario (p. 12).

## The math: your maximum rounds

Assume only short rests and no lost actions. A hand of H cards plays H divided by 2 rounds, rounded down, then rests and loses a card. The new hand does the same, and so on until one card is left.

| Hand size | Most rounds, short rests only |
| --- | --- |
| 9 | 20 |
| 10 | 25 |
| 11 | 30 |
| 12 | 36 |

Your hand limit is in the top right corner of your character mat (p. 24). The Demolitionist has the smallest hand of the four, 9 cards, per Cephalofair.

Here is the 10-card count: 5 rounds, rest to 9 cards, 4 rounds, rest to 8, 4 rounds, rest to 7, then 3, 3, 2, 2, 1, and 1. That adds up to 25. For an even hand the total is H times H divided by 4; for an odd hand, take one away from H times H first, then divide by 4.

These are ceilings. Every lost action and every card lost to block damage takes a chunk off. On a 10-card hand, losing one card in the first round costs you about five rounds, because it shrinks every later cycle too.

## Short rest or long rest?

**Short rest** (end of round, at least two cards in your discard): free in time, but the lost card is random. Once per short rest you can suffer 1 damage to lose a different random card instead (p. 12).

**Long rest** (your whole turn, at initiative 99): you pick the card to lose, heal 2, and refresh spent items. The cost is a turn with no attacks or moves, while monsters still act (p. 22).

A simple rule of thumb that most experienced players follow:

- **Long rest** when you hold a card you cannot afford to lose to chance, when you need the heal, or when no monster can reach you this round.
- **Short rest** when you are in the thick of the fight and cannot spend a turn standing still.

The guide says it well: long rests extend your life and reduce the randomness of short rests, but monsters still get to act while you rest (p. 22).

## Lost actions: spend them like savings

Many of your strongest actions are lost after use (p. 15). They are not a trap. They are savings you are meant to spend. The questions are when and on what.

- **Spend them on rooms, not single monsters.** A lost area attack that kills three monsters saves the party several rounds of cards.
- **Spend them late, when the clock allows.** If the scenario goal is one room away and you have rounds to spare, burn the big card.
- **Do not hold them forever.** A lost card still in your hand when you win did nothing.

If you play a lost card's half as a basic action (Attack 2 or Move 2) instead, the card goes to the discard pile, not the lost pile (p. 15). That is a quiet way to keep a big card in your hand for a later round.

## Losing cards to stop damage

When any single source of damage would hit you, you can negate all of it by losing one card from your hand or two from your discard pile (p. 16). Two rules of thumb:

- **Do it when the damage would exhaust you.** A lost card is better than leaving the scenario.
- **Prefer two discards over one hand card** when the discards are weak, since cards in your discard are already spent for this cycle.

Every card lost this way is about a rest cycle off your clock, so do not do it to save 2 or 3 hit points that a heal can restore.

## Pacing a scenario

Before the first round, look at the map. Count the rooms between you and the goal. One simple way to pace it: divide your maximum rounds by the number of rooms, then keep a few back for the last fight, which is usually the hardest. If you are past halfway through your clock and not past halfway through the map, slow down the lost actions and lean on the party. If the [quarterback problem](/articles/quarterback-problem-cooperative-games/) shows up here, remember that card selection rules already limit what players can tell each other: share plans, not cards.
