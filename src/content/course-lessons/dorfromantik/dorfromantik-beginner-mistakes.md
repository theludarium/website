Your first three games of [Dorfromantik](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BQ3W9GRL?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) are where a group finds its habits. Some points slip away because a rule was played a little wrong. Others slip away through legal choices that close doors ten tiles later. This lesson covers both, checked against the English rulebook. If you need the rules first, start with [how to play Dorfromantik: The Board Game](/academy/dorfromantik/how-to-play-dorfromantik/).

## Rules slips to catch early

**Drawing the wrong kind of tile.** After the first 3 turns, count the Task tiles on the map that still carry a marker. Fewer than 3 means a Task tile, every time. Groups that forget draw Landscape tiles instead, the stack runs down faster, and fewer tasks ever come into play.

**Counting a territory through a mismatched edge.** A territory is tiles of one type joined by matching edges. Two forests on neighboring tiles whose touching edges are Forest and Meadow are two separate territories, not one.

**Forgetting the Task tile counts.** A Forest Task tile adds a Forest tile to its territory. Place one with a 4 so its Forest edge meets a forest of three connected tiles, and it completes on the spot.

**Placing a Task tile where it cannot work.** You may not place a Task tile so that its area is already bigger than its new marker, or so that it closes the area off below the marker's number. Turn it, or find another spot.

**Keeping a dead marker.** If a Landscape tile closes off an area that is still too small for its marker, that marker goes back to the box right away. So does any marker whose area grows past its number. Leaving it on the map means drawing a Landscape tile when you should draw a Task tile.

**Losing a banked marker.** The reverse slip. Once a task is complete, its marker leaves the map. Growing that area later does not take it back.

**Breaking the Track and Stream rule.** Track edges only meet Track edges, and Stream edges only meet Stream edges. A Stream edge against a Meadow is illegal, even if it looks fine.

**Scoring open Flags.** A Flag territory with even one open edge scores nothing.

**Ending too early.** If the last Landscape tile completes or cancels a task, the game goes on. Keep revealing Task tiles until one does not complete or cancel anything.

## The placement habits that cost points

**Placing for beauty.** Dorfromantik's tiles are lovely, and it is tempting to build a pretty village. But every tile is also a count. Before you place, ask which open task this tile helps, and which it might break.

**Growing every area at once.** New groups spread tiles everywhere, so five half-built territories wait for tiles that may never come. A few focused areas finish tasks faster, and every finished task brings a new Task tile.

**Joining areas by accident.** A common way to lose a marker: a forest needs 5, has 4, and a tile with two Forest edges joins it to another forest of 2. Now it is 7. Before you place, count both sides of every edge you are joining.

**Leaving Flags for later.** Closing a territory means surrounding it with tiles whose touching edges are not that type. That takes several tiles, and late in the game you may not have them. Lesson 5 covers how to close a Flag on purpose.

**Ignoring the Track and the Stream.** Your longest Track and longest Stream score a point per tile. A tile with a stream on it placed far from your main stream can still earn its point later, but only if you leave room to join them.

## Don't open the boxes early

It is tempting to read ahead or peek in a box after a good game. The campaign sheet decides when each box opens. If you want to know how the sheet works without spoilers, see [the Dorfromantik campaign guide](/academy/dorfromantik/dorfromantik-campaign-guide/).

## A checklist for game three

Before each placement, someone says three things out loud:

1. How many tiles does each open task still need?
2. Does this tile join two areas, and what is the new total?
3. Does it close anything off, on purpose or by accident?

Those three questions catch most lost markers. If you like games where the group thinks out loud like this, our list of [the best cooperative board games](/articles/best-cooperative-board-games/) has more.
