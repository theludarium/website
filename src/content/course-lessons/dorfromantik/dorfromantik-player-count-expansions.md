Dorfromantik: The Board Game says 1 to 6 players on the box, and it means it. There is no special setup for a big group or a solo game. This lesson covers how player count changes the feel, then the other games in the family and how they relate to the one you own.

## How many players

| Players | What changes |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | You make every call. Lesson 13 covers solo play. |
| 2 | Fast and quiet. Each of you places about half the tiles and sees the whole map. |
| 3 to 4 | The sweet spot for many groups: enough voices to catch mistakes, few enough to stay calm. |
| 5 to 6 | The same tiles shared among more people. Each player places fewer tiles, and the talk does more of the work. |

The tile supply never changes. With 45 Landscape tiles and up to 25 Task tiles, the map is the same size at any count. More players means fewer turns each, not a longer game, though more discussion can stretch the play time toward the 60 minutes on the box.

The rule for decisions is the same at every count: talk it over, then the active player decides. At 5 or 6, that rule matters most. Our piece on [the quarterback problem in cooperative games](/articles/quarterback-problem-cooperative-games/) has habits that keep a big table fun for everyone.

## Variants

The base rulebook describes one way to play: the campaign. Two simple ways to bend it are both within the rules:

- **Run more than one campaign.** The rulebook lets you pause a campaign and start another, so a family table and a friends table can each have their own sheet.
- **Play a single game for fun.** Use a Score sheet, skip the Campaign sheet, and play the base game as a puzzle with a score to beat.

## Dorfromantik: The Duel

[Dorfromantik: The Duel](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CQ6WS3VP?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) turns the idea into a competition. Pegasus describes it as a game for two players or two teams, each building their own landscape from identical tiles, and two copies let four players or teams compete. It keeps the board game's core: hex tiles, villagers' tasks, rivers, and railway tracks.

Pegasus also says it includes material that can be added to the cooperative board game. If you want a two-player game that feels like Dorfromantik but with a winner, this is it.

## Dorfromantik: Sakura

[Dorfromantik: Sakura](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DF4BGSKK?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) is a standalone cooperative game for 1 to 6 players, set in a landscape inspired by Japan. Pegasus says that instead of railway tracks you build roads, and cherry blossoms add a new element of play. It has its own campaign with its own boxes to open.

It is a good next box for a group that finished the base campaign and wants the same feeling with new puzzles. Because it stands alone, you do not need the base game to play it.

## Dorfromantik: Light Luggage

[Dorfromantik: Light Luggage](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0G8W1CNV7?tag=cubecommonw0a-20) is the small box. Pegasus describes a compact, portable cooperative game for 1 to 4 players with its own campaign, where beating score thresholds adds new components. It plays in 20 to 40 minutes and, according to Pegasus, also includes new material for owners of the board game.

## Small extras

Pegasus has also released mini expansions, such as Wetterau, which adds three special tiles with their own rules. Small promos like this come and go; check the [Pegasus Dorfromantik pages](https://pegasus.de/en/Dorfromantik-The-Board-Game-Game-of-the-Year-2023/51240E) for what is current.

## The board game and the video game

The board game is based on the Dorfromantik video game from Toukana Interactive, available, according to Toukana, on PC, Nintendo Switch, PlayStation, and Xbox. They share a look and an idea: hex tiles of forest, fields, villages, rivers, and rails, and quiet satisfaction in fitting them together.

They play differently:

| | Video game | Board game |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Players | One player | 1 to 6, cooperative |
| Tile supply | Quests and perfect placements add tiles to your stack | A fixed stack: 45 Landscape tiles a game |
| Matching every edge | A perfect placement earns an extra tile | Not scored in the base rules |
| Goals | Quests appear as you play | Task markers needing an exact count |
| Long-term play | Grows as you play | A campaign of sealed boxes and Achievement cards |

The biggest shift is the clock. In the video game's classic mode, the game ends when your stack is empty, so good play keeps it going. In the board game, the length is set by the Landscape stack, and good play earns you more Task tiles inside that limit. If you love the video game, expect the same calm and a sharper puzzle about counting.
