Welcome to Level 3. You know the rules cold, you can count the stack, and you time the ending. This level is about the hardest goals the box offers: high scores, a finished campaign, and a strong solo game. This lesson pulls the earlier ones into one plan.

## Know the ceiling

In a fresh campaign, tasks top out at 130 points: five markers per type (4, 5, 5, 6, 6) across five types. Flags and the two longest lines add more. The High Score List on the Campaign sheet runs all the way up to 400. A base game has at most 70 tiles to score with, so even a perfect one cannot reach the top rows. They are meant for later in the campaign, when new content adds to the bottom of the Score sheet.

So a high score has two parts: play the base game as well as it can be played, and make full use of whatever you have earned. The second part is yours to discover. This lesson covers the first.

## Principle 1: flow beats everything

The Landscape stack is the clock. Task tiles are free turns. Each marker that leaves the map, completed or cancelled, brings another Task tile.

So the number that predicts your score best is how many of the 25 Task tiles you reveal. A group that reveals all 25 has had every chance at task points. A group that reveals 15 has left ten tiles, and their markers, in the box.

To keep tasks flowing:

- **Finish small tasks first.** A 4 that needs one tile should get that tile before a 6 that needs three.
- **Clear dead tasks.** A task that cannot finish still holds a slot. Push it over or close it off to bring a fresh Task tile (Lesson 8).
- **Prefer instant completions.** A Task tile that completes the moment it lands frees its own slot. That is two Task tiles for one turn of attention.

## Principle 2: a home for every marker

Lesson 7 showed that an open area of 3 tiles accepts any marker and completes a 4 at once. Strong groups keep one such waiting area for each Task type that still has Task tiles left: a 3-tile Forest, a 3-tile Grain field, a 3-tile Village, and short pieces of Track and Stream.

Building them costs a little: you place tiles to make a spot ready rather than to finish something now. It pays back because new Task tiles land somewhere useful instead of starting from scratch.

## Principle 3: stack in order

Put same-type tasks in the same area, smallest number first. A territory that completes a 4, a 5, and a 6 as it grows pays 15 points by the time it is 6 tiles big, and each completion brings a Task tile. Two equal markers in one area finish together on one tile.

## Principle 4: one main Track, one main Stream

Your Longest Track and Longest Stream score a point per tile. Pick one main line of each early and keep it free of open markers where you can, so you can extend it without fear.

Put Track and Stream tasks on short side pieces instead. Once those tasks are complete, their markers are banked, and you are free to join a side piece to the main line later. Every tile in it then counts toward the longest. Plan for that: point the side piece toward the main line and leave the gap open.

## Principle 5: Flags late, but closed

Flag territories reward size and punish open edges. Grow them while the stack is deep, ideally while they also hold a task or two, then start closing with about a third of the stack left. A closed Flag territory of 8 tiles that also completed a 4 and a 5 is 17 points from one area.

## Principle 6: use every tile twice

The best placements do two things at once: grow a task and close a Flag edge, extend the Stream and set up a waiting area, finish a task and wall off a neighbor. Before each placement, ask what the tile's second job is. If it has none, look again.

## Review every game

The campaign gives you a record. Add four numbers to each Score sheet:

| Number | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| Task tiles revealed (out of 25) | Your flow |
| Completed markers, divided by Task tiles revealed | Your accuracy |
| Flags closed (out of the Flags that appeared) | Your timing |
| Longest Track plus longest Stream | Your long game |

Improve the weakest number next game.

## Talk less, decide better

At high levels, the conversation gets short and specific. "Forest 3, ready." "Stream task needs 2." "Flag edge left." A shared vocabulary keeps a big group fast and focused.
