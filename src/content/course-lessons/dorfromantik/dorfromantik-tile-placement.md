If you came to the board game from the video game, you may be looking for the satisfying moment when every edge of a tile matches its neighbors. The video game calls that a perfect placement. The board game does not score it, at least not in a fresh campaign. Matching edges still decide almost everything, just in a different way.

## The three placement rules, once more

1. Every tile touches the map on at least 1 edge.
2. Track edges only meet Track edges. Stream edges only meet Stream edges.
3. Forest, Grain, Village, and Meadow edges can meet anything.

You can turn a tile any way you like. That is the whole rule set. Everything else in this lesson is about choosing well among the legal spots.

## Why matching still matters

The rulebook defines a territory as tiles of one type connected with matching edges. A Forest edge that touches a Grain edge does not join anything. So while matching is optional, it is how areas grow:

- **For tasks,** only matched tiles count toward the number.
- **For Flags,** only matched tiles count toward the points, and every unmatched neighbor is a wall that helps close the territory.
- **For the Longest Track and Stream,** the chain only continues through matching edges, which the rules require anyway.

So a tile with all six edges matched is often a good tile, because it usually grows something. But a tile can match everywhere and still be a mistake, if it pushes a territory past its marker's number or seals off a field that needed one more tile.

## Perfect placement in the board game

In the base rules, a tile whose edges all match its neighbors earns nothing extra. Your score comes from tasks, Flags, and the two longest lines. Some groups still enjoy aiming for it, and it does no harm when the counts allow. Just do not chase it over a task.

The campaign adds content as you play, and some of it may change how you value edges. That is the campaign's business; this lesson will not spoil it.

## Habits that keep the map open

**Leave easy edges.** An open edge that faces an empty space will one day need a tile. Meadow, Forest, Grain, and Village edges are easy to fit: anything can touch them. Track and Stream edges are hard: they need a tile with the same feature in the right spot. Leaving a Stream edge facing a gap is a promise you have to keep.

**Avoid tight holes.** A gap surrounded by tiles on four or five sides must take a tile that fits all those edges at once. If two of them are a Track and a Stream, few tiles will ever fit. Holes like that can strand a territory, leaving it neither finished nor closed.

**Point lines outward.** When the Track or Stream reaches the edge of the map, aim it at open table, not back into the middle. Your longest line needs room to run, and every tile it gains is a point.

**Keep areas apart until you want them joined.** Two forests that sit one tile apart are one careless placement away from merging. That can complete a task, or blow past one. Leave a Meadow or other terrain between them unless the count says join.

**Think about the next Task tile.** When there are fewer than 3 open tasks, the next tile is a Task tile of an unknown type. Keep at least one area of each type small and open, so a new Task tile has a sensible home.

## Talk about the edge you leave behind

Most placement talk is about where the new tile goes. The better question is what it leaves: which edges now face empty space, and how hard each will be to fill. Two or three words from the table, "that leaves a Stream gap," catch most of the placements that cost points later.

## When nothing fits well

Some tiles have no good home. A Stream tile when your Stream is boxed in, or a Village with no open tasks nearby. Place it where it does the least harm: at the outside of the map, its awkward edges facing open table, away from territories with open markers. A quiet tile in a quiet corner is fine.
