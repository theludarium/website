Multiplayer Ark Nova has a lot of noise: rivals take your cards, push the break, and trigger the end. The solo game strips that away. You face a fixed clock and a fixed target, and every point you miss is a turn you could have used better. That is why competitive players use it as a drill.

## How solo changes the game

From the rulebook's solo rules:

- **No Break token.** A big solo tile holds 7 player tokens in its left column. At the end of each turn, move the top one to the right column. When the last one moves, you take a break.
- **Each round is shorter.** At each break, the top token goes onto the lowest open donation space, and the rest go back to the left column, so the next round has one fewer turn.
- **Six rounds.** The game ends after the sixth round, with no final break, and you go straight to final scoring.
- **You start at 20 appeal.** Sponsors that say "max. 25 appeal" are treated as "max. 45 appeal," and a small solo tile on the track reminds you.
- **Actions that would move the Break token** pay any money they would pay, but move nothing. You get no X-token for a break.
- **Up to 2 project cards** sit above the association board, and interactive animals use the solo effect in their blue strip.
- **You win with at least 100 points,** which means your two counters met or passed.

## Counting your turns

The round lengths run 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, and 2 turns. That is 27 turns in the whole game, with breaks after the first five rounds. This number is the best thing about solo practice: you know exactly how many actions you have, and you can plan the whole game against it.

The rounds also shrink, so the last two rounds give you only 5 turns between them. Money and cards you carry into those rounds have little time to turn into points. The shape pushes you to build early and cash in fast.

## Difficulty levels

- **20 appeal:** recommended for beginners.
- **10 appeal:** flip the small solo tile to its 35 side and treat "max. 25 appeal" as "max. 35."
- **0 appeal:** no small tile. The rulebook calls it quite a challenge.

Move up a level when you win most games at the current one.

## The three-game challenge

For a real test, the rulebook offers a challenge: play 3 solo games in a row without reshuffling. After each game, remove every card used in it, including the discard pile, the display, your cards, and the projects, plus the two zoo maps you chose from and the 4 Bonus tiles. For the third game, reuse 3 random removed Bonus tiles. Your three scores must total at least 300.

Each game draws from a thinner deck, so you must adapt to cards you would never choose. That is good practice for multiplayer games where the display does not cooperate.

## Drills that carry over

**1. The row log.** Write each turn's action and strength. After the game, look for turns where you used a card at a strength that wasted steps, such as Build at 5 for a 2-space enclosure. The [action cards lesson](/academy/ark-nova/ark-nova-action-cards/) explains why those cost you.

**2. Gap per turn.** Divide your final gap closed by 27. Each time you replay, try to raise that average. It is the same efficiency that wins tight multiplayer games.

**3. Break spending.** At each break, note how much money and how many cards you carried in. Aim for little of both, as the [sponsors and money lesson](/academy/ark-nova/ark-nova-sponsors-and-money/) suggests.

**4. Last-round plan.** At the start of the final 2-turn round, write your two actions before you take them. Then check whether a different plan, started a round earlier, would have scored more.

## What solo does not teach

Solo has no denial, no race for project levels, and no break timing beyond the tile. So pair it with multiplayer games. Solo sharpens your own engine; multiplayer teaches you to read others. The [competitive play lesson](/academy/ark-nova/ark-nova-competitive-play/) picks up there.
