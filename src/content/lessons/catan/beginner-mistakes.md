Most Catan games are not lost to bad dice. They are lost to a handful of habits that show up game after game. The good news is that habits are easier to fix than dice.

Here are the seven we see most, with a fix for each. A few of them are quick recaps of earlier Level 1 lessons. That is on purpose. This lesson is your checklist for the whole level.

## Mistake 1: Why is road spam a trap?

**What it looks like:** you have a brick and a wood, so a road goes down. Next turn, another one. By the middle of the game your roads branch everywhere and lead nowhere in particular.

**Why it hurts:** roads score nothing on their own. Each one costs a brick and a wood, which are exactly the cards a new settlement needs. Two roads to nowhere are half a settlement you did not build.

**The fix:** every road needs a job you can name. Either it reaches a settlement spot within the next road or two, or it blocks a corner someone else wants. Chasing Longest Route on purpose is a job too, and Level 2 covers when it is worth it. "I had the cards" is not a job.

## Mistake 2: Why do beginners wait too long to build cities?

**What it looks like:** you keep chasing one more settlement. Twenty turns in, you have four settlements and no cities, and you are still hunting for open spots.

**Why it hurts:** a city is the easiest point in the game. It needs no road, no new spot, and nothing anyone can take first. It also doubles what that corner produces. A city on a settlement touching a 6, an 8, and a 5 earns twice as many cards from 14 pips, every roll, for the rest of the game.

**The fix:** once ore and wheat are coming in, aim for your first city by the middle of the game, on your highest-pip settlement. Lesson 8 explained why ore and wheat matter more as the game goes on. Level 2 has a full lesson on the city rush.

## Mistake 3: Why is trading with the leader so costly?

**What it looks like:** someone asks the table for ore, and you say yes because you need their wool. It is friendly. It is fair. It is also how the leader gets to 10.

**Why it hurts:** every trade helps both players. The question is who it helps more. The leader can turn cards into points faster than anyone, so even an even trade usually helps them more than you.

**The fix:** before every trade, count the other player's points, including a guess for hidden development cards. Once someone shows 7 or more, trade with them only when the deal is clearly lopsided in your favor, or not at all. Find the card elsewhere: another player, a port, or the bank. Our guide on [how to win at Catan](/articles/how-to-win-at-catan/) has more on trading without feeding the leader. And if you are out of the running, remember that your trades may decide who wins. Our piece on [kingmaking](/articles/kingmaking-board-games/) covers how to handle that fairly.

## Mistake 4: What is wrong with saving up?

**What it looks like:** you hold 10 cards, waiting for the perfect build. Then a 7 lands and 5 of them go back to the supply.

**Why it hurts:** in a four-player game, a hand of 8 or more faces about a coin flip of losing half before your next turn. And a big hand makes you the obvious robber target.

**The fix:** end every turn at 7 or fewer, and if you hold more, make it a choice. Lesson 10 has the full list of ways to spend down.

## Mistake 5: Why is robbing the weakest player a mistake?

**What it looks like:** you roll a 7 and put the robber on the player in last place. They are quiet, they will not complain, and it feels safe. Or the robber goes on whoever annoyed you last.

**Why it hurts:** the robber is the one direct attack in Catan. Every time it hits someone other than the leader, the leader keeps rolling at full speed. Hitting the player in last place also weakens the one person who might have slowed the leader down.

**The fix:** put the robber on the leader's best hex that does not pay you, and steal from the leader. If several players touch that hex, steal from the one with the most cards. Level 3 has a full lesson on robber tactics, but this one rule covers most games.

## Mistake 6: What are the most common card timing slips?

**What it looks like:**

- Trying to play a development card on the turn you built it
- Playing two development cards in one turn
- Sitting on three Knights, then running out of turns to play them
- Showing a Victory Point card early
- Playing Monopoly when nobody holds much of anything
- Forgetting you can play a Knight before you roll

**Why it hurts:** each slip wastes a card that cost you 3 resources, or hands the table information for free.

**The fix:** one card per turn, never the one you just built. Play Knights steadily, and before the roll when the robber sits on your hex. Keep Victory Points hidden until they win you the game. Lesson 11 covers the best moment for every card.

## Mistake 7: How do you get boxed in?

**What it looks like:** your two opening spots are strong, but by turn six the open corners near you are gone. Opponents' roads and settlements surround you, and there is no legal spot you can reach.

**Why it hurts:** you have 5 settlements to place, and each new one needs a free corner that follows the distance rule and connects to your road. A road also cannot run past an opponent's settlement or city. Once the corners near you are taken, your growth stops, no matter how good your numbers are.

**The fix:** check room to grow during setup. Room to expand is on the Lesson 7 checklist for a reason. Point your first roads toward open spots, and race for the contested corner first. The spot nobody else can reach will still be there next turn.

## How do these seven fit together?

Look at the list again. Road spam and hoarding are both about spending well. Late cities and getting boxed in are both about planning your growth. Trading with the leader and robbing the weakest are both about knowing who is winning. Card timing ties the rest together.

That is all of Level 1. You can now read the dots, pick an opening you can explain, spend cards on purpose, and avoid the habits that sink most beginners. Congratulations, Homesteader.

## Try it this week

Your drill: have a friend call out each mistake while you play.

1. **Pick a spotter.** It can be someone at the table or someone watching. Give them the list of seven mistakes.
2. **Agree on a signal.** A light tap on the table and the mistake's number works well. "Three!" means you just traded with the leader.
3. **Calls come after you act,** not before. The point is to notice your habits, not to get coached through every turn.
4. **Keep a tally.** Each call earns one mark next to that mistake. No arguing during the game. Take the call with a smile, the same way you would take a lost game. Our guide on [how to be a good loser](/articles/how-to-be-a-good-loser/) helps with both.
5. **Review after the game.** Which mistake got the most marks? That is your focus for the next game.
6. **Swap roles.** Spot for your friend next time. Watching someone else play is one of the fastest ways to see the game clearly.
