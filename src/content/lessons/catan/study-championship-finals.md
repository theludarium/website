Every Catan player has the same coach available, for free. It is the best players in the world, playing for a title, on camera. Most people watch a final the way they watch a sports highlight. They enjoy it, and they learn very little.

This lesson shows you how to watch a final the way a coach would. Where to find one, how to predict each player's plan before they place, what to write down turn by turn, and how to turn one annotated final into better games of your own.

## Where can you watch championship finals?

On CATAN's official YouTube channel, [youtube.com/@catancommunity](https://www.youtube.com/@catancommunity). As of October 2026, it has streams and videos from several championship events, including:

- [CATAN US National Championship Finals 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Bqoe8y4awR0), streamed from Chicago in September 2026. It runs about six hours, so skip ahead to the final table.
- [The 2023 U.S. National Championship final](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XIb2iapTrcw), about two and a half hours.
- [CATAN World Championship 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rBDWzpjFyjs), the stream from Stuttgart.
- [The CATAN Americas Continental Championship final game](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mFmL_5diuPk), from 2019.

The channel also has Canadian championship finals. Search the channel for "championship" to see everything.

A few cautions. Streams are long, and the camera cannot always show every hand. Older finals use older pieces and older names, like lumber and Longest Road. Tournament rules change from season to season, so a detail in a 2019 final may not match today's [rules](/academy/catan/tournament-rules/). The way strong players think has not changed much, and that is what you are here to study.

## Why study a final at all?

Because a final shows you decisions you would never think of on your own. At home, you mostly play people at your level. Their mistakes look normal to you, and so do yours.

A final is also a clean puzzle. Every table at an official event plays one shared map, and the finalists all earned their seats. When four players that strong read the same board, the differences in their plans are real choices, not luck of the setup.

But watching alone does not teach much. Watching while predicting does.

## How do you watch a final like a coach?

In three passes.

**Pass 1: Predict.** Pause the moment the map is revealed. Run your own [five-minute map study](/academy/catan/map-study/) with a timer. Then, for each seat, write one line: their likely lane, their likely first settlement, and why. Lesson 38 had you try this on a Nationals final. Here it is the first step of a full study.

**Pass 2: Compare.** Play the placements. After each one, pause and mark your prediction right, close, or wrong. Wrong is the good part. For every miss, write what the player saw that you did not.

**Pass 3: Annotate.** Watch the rest of the game with your notes open, round by round. You do not need to log everything. Log the turns where something changed.

Use the pause button freely. A two-hour final might take you four hours to study, and that is fine. You only need to do this a few times a season.

## What should you note, turn by turn?

Five things, the same five that decide most of your own games.

| What | What to write | The question it answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Placements | Each corner, its pips, its resources, and the road direction | What did they value on this map? |
| Trades | Who traded what, with whom, and at what score | Who did they refuse to feed? |
| Robber | The hex, the player robbed, and the scores at that moment | How did they pick a target? |
| Development cards | When each card was built, and when it was played | Did they hold cards, and for how long? |
| Endgame | Each player's path from 7 points to 10 | How did the winner close? |

Number the rounds, just as in your own [game reviews](/academy/catan/reviewing-your-games/). Track scores every round or two. A note like "Red at 8" turns a robber move from random into readable.

You will not see every hand. That is fine. Track what you can see: what each roll paid, what each build cost, and what was traded. That is the same [card counting](/academy/catan/card-counting/) you do at a real table, just with a pause button.

## How do you annotate a turn?

Each line has three parts: what happened, why you think they did it, and what you would have done.

Here is the shape, with a made-up example rather than a real final:

> **Round 4, Blue:** built a development card instead of a road. *Why:* Red was about to block Blue's next corner, and Blue had ore and wheat to spare. *Me:* I would have built the road and lost the race anyway.
>
> **Round 9, Green:** robbed Yellow, not Red. *Why:* Yellow had 7 visible points and two Knights played, so Yellow was one Knight from Largest Army and 9 points. *Me:* I would have robbed Red, who had more cards.
>
> **Round 12, Red:** held a Monopoly for two rounds, then called ore right after an 8 paid ore to three players. *Why:* the ore was sitting in other hands, not yet spent. *Me:* I would have played it the turn I drew it.

That third part, "Me," is where the lesson lives. Every gap between their move and yours is a habit you can test.

## What patterns should you look for?

After one final, you have a page of notes. Read it back and look for patterns, not single brilliant moves. Questions to ask:

- **How early did the winner build a first city?** Compare it with your own games.
- **How many trades did the finalists make?** Strong players often trade less than you expect, and almost never with the leader. See [Lesson 17](/academy/catan/art-of-the-trade/).
- **Where did the robber go, and when?** Did it chase the leader every time, or block a key resource? Compare it with [Lesson 22](/academy/catan/robber-tactics/).
- **When were development cards played?** Players often hold a Knight until it matters for Largest Army or for a robber move that changes the race.
- **How did the winner reach 10?** Write it out the way you would for your own games. Was it a quiet two-point turn? Hidden points? Check it against [Lesson 25](/academy/catan/endgame/).
- **How did the other finalists play once the game was out of reach?** Watch whether they kept building for points.

Then pick one pattern. Just one. Try it in your next three games and see if it helps, the same way you treat line 3 of a review.

## How do you make it stick?

Two ways to get more from each final.

**Replay the opening.** Build the final's map on your own table, or as close as you can. Place for each seat yourself, then compare with what the finalists did. You will remember a placement you made with your own hands far better than one you watched.

**Study with someone.** Watch the same final with a friend, predict separately, and compare notes before you play the placements. Two people catch twice as many patterns. The next lesson is about building a group to train with, and a final is a great first thing to study together.

## Try it this week

Your drill: annotate one final, turn by turn.

1. **Pick a final.** Go to the [CATAN YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/@catancommunity) and choose one championship final. A recent one is closest to today's rules.
2. **Set up a sheet.** One column each for the round, the player, what happened, why, and what you would have done.
3. **Pause at the map reveal.** Run your five-minute map study with a timer. Write each seat's predicted lane and first settlement.
4. **Play the placements.** Mark each prediction right, close, or wrong, and write what you missed.
5. **Annotate the game.** Log placements, trades, robber moves, development cards, and every player's path from 7 to 10. Track scores every round or two.
6. **Read it back.** Circle every line where your "Me" answer was different from theirs. Find the pattern that shows up most.
7. **Try that one pattern** in your next three games, then check your reviews to see whether it helped.
