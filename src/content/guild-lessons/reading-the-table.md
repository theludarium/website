The fourth number this program holds you to is that the newcomer is never out of the game before it ends. That does not only mean eliminated by the rules. A player can still have pieces on the board and be long gone, waiting politely for it to be over.

Your job in the middle of a game is to notice that early and do something about it. The newcomer will almost never tell you. Saying "I'm bored" or "I'm lost" in the middle of someone else's favorite game feels rude, so they say nothing. You have to read it.

## The signs someone has checked out

None of these is proof by itself. Two together usually are.

**The phone comes out between turns,** and then stays out during their own turn.

**Silence where there was talk.** In the first round they asked questions and reacted to other people's moves. Now they only speak when it is their turn.

**"Whatever you think."** When a player hands their own decision back to you, they have stopped playing and started waiting. This is the clearest sign there is.

**Fast, flat turns.** They take the first option they see, without looking at the board. It can look like confidence. Usually it means they want their turn to be over.

**Questions about time.** "How long do these usually go?" is rarely a question about the game.

One more sign belongs to you: if you are the only one still excited, the table has already decided.

## Three moves, smallest first

**Shorten.** Change the length without stopping the game. Drop a round, lower the target score, or agree that the next scoring moment ends it. Say it as an offer to everyone: "It's getting late. Want to make this the last round?" Most people say yes with visible relief.

**Call it at a natural stop.** Finish the round, then end the game, and score it as it stands. A natural stop matters. It gives the ending a shape, and it keeps anyone from thinking the game ended because of them.

**Switch.** If the problem is the game itself, not the hour, end this one and put a short one on the table. Twenty minutes of something light and fast can turn the night around. This is why you plan a backup game before anyone arrives, as [Read the Room on the Night](/academy/guild/read-the-room/) lays out.

Whichever you choose, do it early. A game called at the right moment feels like a choice. A game dragged to its official ending feels like a sentence served.

## A worked example: calling the last round of Ticket to Ride

Picture a four-player game of [Ticket to Ride](/academy/ticket-to-ride/), about an hour in. Your newcomer has stopped looking at the map. Their phone is face up beside their cards. Under the rules, the game ends only when someone gets down to 2 or fewer trains, and nobody is close.

So you make the call at the start of a new trip around the table: "Let's make this the last round. Everyone gets one more turn, then we score."

Then comes the move most hosts miss. In Ticket to Ride, every ticket you kept but did not finish subtracts its value at the end. Your newcomer planned for a longer game, so cutting it short could swing them from a decent score to a negative one. Before anyone takes that last turn, say: "Since we're stopping early, unfinished tickets cost nothing tonight. Finished ones still count." Now the last turn is a chance to score, not a race to avoid a penalty.

Then reveal tickets, give out the 10 points for the longest path, and count it up. The game ended on a reveal, which is the most fun part of Ticket to Ride, instead of on a slow slide.

## Decide before the night

The hardest part of calling a game early is doing it while you are enjoying yourself. So decide in advance. Pick your call-it signal, the one sign that means you will act, and pick the words you will say. When you see it, you do not have to weigh anything. You just say the line.

This is the principle End on a high, in practice. People remember how a night finished far more than how it went in the middle. The last ten minutes are the ones the newcomer carries home.
