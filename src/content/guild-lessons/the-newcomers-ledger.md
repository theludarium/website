Gamers think of a teach as free. You give your time, they give their attention, and then the fun starts. A newcomer sees it differently. Learning a game costs them something, and they keep count, mostly without meaning to.

Call it the newcomer's ledger. One column is cost. The other is fun. If the fun clears the cost by the end of the night, they say yes to the next game. If it does not, they find a polite reason to be busy.

## What goes in the cost column

Most of the costs have nothing to do with how hard the rules are.

- **Feeling lost.** Not knowing what to look at, what matters, or whose turn it is.
- **Feeling stupid.** Everyone else nods, so the question in their head must be a dumb one. They keep it to themselves, and now they are lost and quiet.
- **The rules dump.** Fifteen minutes of facts with no place to hang them. Every rule heard before it matters has to be held in memory, and memory gets tired.
- **Jargon.** Words like "engine," "action economy," or "tableau" tell a newcomer that this is a club, and they are not in it yet.
- **Public correction.** "Actually, you can't do that" in front of four people. Each one is small. They add up fast.
- **The blowout.** Losing by a mile tells them the game is for other people.
- **Your impatience.** A sigh, a glance at the phone, a "so basically" said a little too fast. You do not notice it. They notice nothing else.

That last line is the one hosts forget, and it is often the biggest. A newcomer reads your face more closely than the rulebook, because your face is where the verdict on them shows up first.

## Costs are paid up front

Here is the cruel part of the math. The costs arrive early, during the teach and the first round. The fun arrives late, once the game clicks. So for the first stretch of any evening, the newcomer is paying on credit, trusting you that the payoff is coming.

A gamer extends that credit easily, because they have been paid back many times before. A newcomer has no such history. If the cost column fills up before the fun column has a single entry, they close the account. They will keep playing to be polite, but the decision is already made.

This is why the five principles all lean toward the front of the night. Frame before facts and Just in time, not just in case shrink the costs. First win early puts something in the fun column inside ten minutes. Protect dignity removes the most expensive lines. End on a high makes sure the last entry is a good one.

## A worked example: Wingspan from the in-law's chair

Picture your partner's mother at her first game night. You have chosen [Wingspan](/academy/wingspan/) because she watches birds, which is a good instinct. Now sit in her chair.

You start with the player mat. Three habitats, and each one is also an action. Then the egg costs, which change by column. Then food, the birdfeeder, the face with two foods on it. Then the three colors of bird powers, brown, white, and pink, and what "cache" and "tuck" mean. Then the end-of-round goals, the bonus cards, and the scoring. Twenty minutes in, she has not touched a card.

Here is her ledger. Lost: she still does not know what she is trying to do. Stupid: she asked what a "when activated" power was and got a second explanation that sounded like the first. Jargon: "engine" came up four times. Impatience: someone at the table said "you'll pick it up as we go," which is true and lands as "please stop asking."

She plays. She is kind about it. She says the birds are lovely. And that is all the fun column holds.

Now picture the same evening with the costs cut. You tell her she is attracting birds to her nature preserve, and the more birds in a row, the stronger that row gets. You play one turn with her, real cards in her hand. You use the blue side of the goal board, which the rulebook suggests for new players, and you explain each power the first time one of her birds has it. The birds are still the reward. They just arrive before the bill.

## Read the ledger before you teach

You cannot see the ledger, but you can guess most of it in advance. Every reluctant friend has two or three costs that weigh more for them than the rest. One hates feeling slow. One hates being watched while they think. One hates losing to a spouse in front of the kids.

Know those costs before you pick a game or plan a teach, and you can design around them. The rest of this program is a set of ways to do exactly that.
