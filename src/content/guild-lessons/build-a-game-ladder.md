Most hosts think one game ahead. They pick tonight's game, teach it, and hope. If it lands, they reach for their favorite next, which is often three steps further along than the newcomer is ready for. The second game goes worse than the first, and the streak ends there.

A ladder fixes that. It is a short plan, four games long, for one particular person. You still pick each game for the night, but you pick it knowing what comes before and after.

## The rule of one new idea

Every game you teach is made of ideas: take a card or stop, collect sets, claim a space, build something that grows. A hobby gamer knows dozens of these, so a new game feels like a remix. A newcomer knows almost none, so every one of them is new at once.

The ladder's rule is simple. **Each rung adds one new idea.** Everything else in the game should be something the person has already done. When you teach rung three, you can say "this is like the last one, except," and the "except" is the only thing they have to learn.

Three more rules keep the ladder steady:

- **Rung one is short.** Twenty minutes or less, built on a feeling they already chase. Its only job is to make yes easy next time.
- **Never jump two rungs.** If they loved rung two, it is tempting to skip ahead. Do not. Two new ideas in one evening is how a ladder becomes a rules dump.
- **Repeat a rung freely.** Playing the same game three times is not standing still. It is how the idea moves from new to known.

## A worked example: four rungs to Wingspan

Picture a partner who has never played a modern board game, and a goal: one day, a quiet evening of Wingspan for two. Here is one ladder.

**Rung one: [Flip 7](/academy/flip-7/).** The new idea: one choice, over and over. Each turn you take another card or stop and keep what you have, and a duplicate number busts you. It plays in about twenty minutes. The card game is the whole lesson: a turn is a decision, and decisions have risk. Flip 7 plays best with three or more, so this rung is a good one for a night with friends.

**Rung two: [Ticket to Ride](/academy/ticket-to-ride/).** The new idea: a goal you plan toward across many turns. Each turn is still one choice, now between three things. What is new is the secret tickets, which ask you to think ahead and connect two cities over the whole game. The board is new, but you read it like a map, which everyone already knows how to do.

**Rung three: [Carcassonne](/academy/carcassonne/).** The new idea: a limited supply of pieces you place to claim things. Every turn is the same three steps: place a tile, maybe place a meeple, score what you finished. The map is shared, like Ticket to Ride's, but now you are building it together. The new tension is your meeples. You only have seven, and a meeple on a feature stays there until that feature is finished and scores. Play without farmers, as the rulebook suggests for the first games.

**Rung four: [Wingspan](/academy/wingspan/).** The new idea: an engine, meaning things you play make your later turns stronger. Each turn you still take one action, from four. Placing birds in habitats will feel like placing meeples. Planning toward a bonus card will feel like a ticket. The one truly new feeling is watching a row of birds pay out more each time you use it. By now that is the only thing left to learn, and it is the most delightful part of the game.

Look at what happened. Wingspan is a lot of rules for a first game. As a fourth game, it is one idea on top of three they already own.

## Writing your own ladder

Start from the end. Name the game you would love to play with this person someday. Then list the ideas it is built on, in plain words. Then work backward, finding a game for each idea, ordered so each one adds a single new piece.

Use the [Game Ladder Worksheet](/academy/guild/toolkit/game-ladder/) to lay it out, and check each rung on the [Teachability Scorecard](/academy/guild/toolkit/teachability-scorecard/) from the last lesson. A rung that scores poorly is a sign you need another rung below it.

Keep the ladder to yourself. It is a plan, not a syllabus. Your partner does not need to know they are on rung two. They just need each game to feel like a natural next step, which is what a good ladder does.
