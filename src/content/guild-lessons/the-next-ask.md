Most second games die in the hallway. The night went well, everyone laughed, and on the way to the door someone says, "We should do this again!" Everyone agrees. Nobody picks a date. Three months later you are wondering why your partner's brother never comes to game night.

"We should do this again" feels like a plan. It is a wish. A plan has a game and a day in it, and someone who owns the next step. The next ask is how you make that someone you.

## Ask before anyone stands up

Timing matters more than wording. The best moment is right after the debrief, while everyone is still seated and the last turn is still fresh. The good feeling of a game peaks at the end and fades fast. Once coats come out, the evening is over in everyone's mind, and your ask turns into one more thing to deal with on the way out.

A useful rule: the box stays open until the next date is set. Packing up is a signal that the night is done. Ask first, then pack up together.

## What makes an ask specific

A good next ask has three parts.

**A game.** Name it, and name it in their words. Not the title, which means nothing to a newcomer yet, but a phrase they can picture: "the bird game," "the one with the tile wall," "the spy word game."

**A day.** Offer one, with a fallback. "Saturday the 9th? Or the Sunday after?" Two options feel like a choice. No options feel like homework.

**A reason that is about them.** Tie the next game to something they said in the debrief. "You liked finishing your cities, and this one is all about building something that grows." This is the difference between an invitation and a favor. "I'd love to play this one" asks them to give you something. "I think you'd love this one" offers them something.

The game you name should be the next rung on their ladder, as [Build a Ladder, Not a List](/academy/guild/build-a-game-ladder/) lays out: one new idea on top of what they just learned, never two.

## A worked example: Wingspan, promised after Carcassonne

Picture the end of a first game of [Carcassonne](/academy/carcassonne/) with a friend who does not usually play. In the debrief she said her favorite part was watching her side of the map grow, and next time she would try to keep more of her meeples free.

So before anyone stands up, you say:

"Okay, next time you pick the birds. There's a game called [Wingspan](/academy/wingspan/) where you build your own little nature preserve, and every bird you add makes the next turn better. It has that same feeling of watching your thing grow. Are you free Friday the 15th, or the weekend after?"

Look at what that does. "You pick the birds" hands her a small decision that is hers, which tends to make people want to show up. The description is in plain words with no hobby terms. The reason ties straight back to what she said she liked. And the day is a real day.

It also climbs exactly one rung. Carcassonne taught her to build something over a game. Wingspan keeps that and adds one new idea: what you build makes your later turns stronger.

## When they hesitate

Sometimes the answer is a soft "maybe." Do not push, and do not drop it either. Shrink the ask.

"How about a shorter one? There's a twenty-minute card game I think you'd like." A smaller game is easier to say yes to, and a yes to anything keeps the streak alive. You can climb the ladder later.

And if the answer is a clear no, take it gracefully. "No problem. The offer's always open." The Second Game is a score, but it is never worth making someone feel cornered at your table. A no said kindly today can still become a yes in the spring.
