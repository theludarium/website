There are two ways to ruin a newcomer's first game while playing it. The first is obvious: you crush them. The second is sneakier: you let them win, and they notice. Both teach the same lesson, that this is a game where they do not really belong.

The answer sits between those two. You play a real game, and you shape how real it feels.

## Why throwing the game fails

Losing on purpose looks kind. It almost never works. New players are better at spotting a thrown game than you think, because it shows up in the moves you skip. You take a weak option while a strong one sits in plain view. You hesitate before doing something odd. They see it, and the win they get turns into a gift they did not ask for.

There is a quieter cost too. If you throw the first game, you have to throw the second, or the newcomer finds out how wide the gap was all along. Now every future game carries a small lie.

So do not throw. Play to win, inside limits you set for yourself, and say some of them out loud.

## Three ways to keep it close

**Name an open handicap.** Announce it before the game starts, the way chess players give odds. "I'll play with my secret goal face up." "I'll start with one fewer card." Because everyone agreed to it, any win the newcomer earns is fully theirs. Keep it small and specific. A handicap that sounds like charity will feel like charity.

**Narrate your own moves.** Say what you are doing and why, in plain words. "I'm laying eggs now because the round is almost over." Your turns become free lessons, and the newcomer starts copying good habits by round two. This also slows you down, which is no bad thing when you are the strongest player at the table.

**Turn down the cruelest play.** Most games offer a move that is strong mainly because it ruins someone else's plan: the route they obviously need, the tile they were saving a spot for. In a first game, you can let that move pass and take the second-best one. That is a choice good players make all the time at friendly tables. It is not throwing. You are still trying to win; you are just not trying to win by ninety.

In a cooperative game, the version of this is asking instead of telling. "What do you think we should do here?" keeps the newcomer playing rather than watching. The [cooperative games lesson](/academy/guild/cooperative-games/) covers that in full.

## A worked example: Wingspan, by ninety and by nine

Picture two first games of [Wingspan](/academy/wingspan/) with the same newcomer.

In the first, you play the way you play with your regular group. You build a forest row that hands you food every turn and fill your grassland with birds that lay eggs. By round three your engine fires every time you touch it, and theirs barely turns. Wingspan has no running score track, so nobody sees the gap until the final count. Then it lands all at once: you win by ninety. The newcomer cannot even say where they lost, because the answer is "everywhere, slowly."

In the second, you set up the blue side of the goal board, which the rulebook suggests for new players, so nobody is ranked against anyone. You say why you are playing each bird. When you are about to take the bird they have been eyeing on the tray, you take a different one. In round four you ask, "Any room left for eggs?" because eggs are 1 point each and new players forget them. You still win, by nine. And the newcomer can point to the exact place the game turned: "I should have laid eggs sooner."

That sentence is the whole point. A loss they can explain is a loss they want to fix, and fixing it means a second game.

## Pick games that stay close

Some games make blowouts likely. When an early lead earns you more every turn, the player who learned fastest pulls away and never comes back, and in a first game that is never the newcomer. Other games keep scores tight on their own, through short rounds, shared choices, or scoring that rewards a good finish.

When you are choosing for a newcomer, ask one question: if they fall behind in the first half, can they still see a way back? If the answer is no, save that game for later, or play it with a handicap you name up front.
