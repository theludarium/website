The first thing you say when you teach a game sets the mood for everything after it. Most teachers open with a fact: how many players, what is in the box, or "okay, so, setup." Those are true. They also give your guest nothing to hold on to.

Start with a sentence that tells them who they are and what they want. That is the first step of the five-minute teach from [How to Teach a Board Game in Five Minutes](/articles/how-to-teach-a-board-game/), and it does more work than any rule you will say tonight.

## Why a story comes first

Memory is not a shelf where facts sit side by side. It is closer to a web, and new facts stick when they hook onto something already there. A list of rules with no story has nothing to hook onto. The same rules inside a story each get a place: this is how I build, this is how I steal, this is why I am worried about my neighbor.

That is the first principle of the program, Frame before facts. The theme sentence is the frame. Every rule you say after it answers a question the story raised.

It also does something for the newcomer's mood. A sentence like "you are rival bakers racing to fill orders before the shop closes" is an invitation to play pretend. It tells your guest this will be a bit silly, a bit fun, and about something they can picture. For someone nervous about games, that matters more than any rule.

## The three parts

A good theme sentence has three things in it:

- **A role.** Who are you? "You are a farmer," "you are railroad barons," "we are a team of disease experts." Say "you," not "the players."
- **A goal.** What do you want? Not the scoring yet, just the wish: the biggest farm, the longest railroads, saving the world.
- **A little fun.** One word or phrase with a wink in it. A rival, a deadline, a race, a bit of mischief.

Keep it to one breath. If you need a second sentence, you are explaining rules early.

And leave the category words out. "Tile-laying," "area control" and "engine builder" describe a game to someone who already plays. Your guest wants to know what they get to be.

## A worked example: Carcassonne

Here is how the opening of [Carcassonne](/academy/carcassonne/) often sounds: "Carcassonne is a tile-laying game where you place tiles and meeples to score points on features."

Every word of that is true, and almost none of it is useful. Tile-laying and meeples are hobby words. "Features" is a rulebook word. And there is no role, no goal, nothing to picture.

Here it is with all three parts: "We are building a medieval landscape together, one tile at a time, and competing to get credit for its cities, roads and monasteries."

- **Role:** builders of a medieval countryside.
- **Goal:** get credit for the best bits of it.
- **Fun:** we build it together, and then we squabble over who gets credit.

Now look at how the rules hook on. Placing a tile is building the landscape. Putting one of your people on a city is claiming credit. Finishing a road and scoring it is getting paid for your work. The one big limit, that you cannot claim a city someone else already claimed, now sounds like what it is: a fight over credit. Your guest heard the reason before the rule.

The sentence also sets up the moment the game gets interesting. When two separate cities grow into one, both players have a claim on the same city. A newcomer who heard "competing for credit" understands why that matters without being told.

## Test it on a real person

You will not know if a sentence works until you say it to someone. Watch for one of two reactions.

A good sentence gets a small smile, a question, or a "so I am trying to..." That means your guest has picked up a role and is already thinking about the goal.

A weak one gets a polite nod and a glance at the box. That means they heard a description, not an invitation. Go back and add the role, or the bit of fun, and try it on someone else.

Write the winner into the theme field of your [teach script](/academy/guild/write-your-teach-script/). It is the one line of the script you should say word for word.
