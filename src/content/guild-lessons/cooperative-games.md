Cooperative games look like the perfect first game for a newcomer. Nobody can crush them, the whole table cheers together, and a loss belongs to everyone. All of that is true. But co-ops carry one danger that competitive games do not: someone can play the game for everyone else. Hobby players call it quarterbacking, and a newcomer who gets quarterbacked spends the evening moving pieces for someone else's plan.

This lesson stops it before it starts.

## The likeliest quarterback is you

Here is the uncomfortable part. In a newcomer's first co-op, the person most likely to run the game is the teacher.

Think about who you are at that table. You know the rules best. You see the threats first. You want the newcomer to have a good night, and you know a win would help. So when it is their turn and they hesitate, you help. Then you help a bit more. By the third round you are saying "move here, treat twice, pass me that card," and they are doing it.

From your chair it feels like kindness. From theirs it feels like being a spectator with a pawn. The team might even win, but it is your win. Nobody remembers a game they did not get to play, and the Second Game depends on them feeling that this one was theirs.

## The one-voice rule

The fix is a simple rule, said out loud before the first turn: **the active player speaks first and decides. Everyone else may ask questions.**

Three parts make it work.

- **Speaks first.** Before anyone offers a plan, the player whose turn it is says what they see. A newcomer who speaks first has to look at the board, and looking is how they learn it. Once a confident plan is on the table, a quiet player will just agree with it.
- **Decides.** Their call is final. If they pick a move you would not, you play your next turn around it.
- **Others ask.** "What worries you most?" "Do you want to keep that card for a cure?" A question hands someone a thought without handing them a move.

Say the rule once at setup, as a rule for everyone, gamers included. Said early, it is a house rule. Said in the middle of the game, it sounds like a scolding.

## Hold the rules, not the plan

Your job in a co-op is the rulebook in human form. Answer every rules question fully, including what a move would cost. Then stop.

The line is easy to hear once you listen for it. "Flying there costs you the Cairo card" is a rule. "Fly to Cairo" is a plan. "If that city gets a fourth cube, it spreads to its neighbors" is a rule. "You need to treat that city" is a plan. Give the first kind freely. Hold the second kind back unless someone asks for it, and even then, offer two options rather than one answer (First win early).

One more habit: speak last. When the team is talking a turn over, let everyone else go first. Your read will still be there in a minute, and theirs might be better than you expect.

## Worked example: Pandemic, current player speaks first

Picture a four-player game of [Pandemic](/academy/pandemic/) with one newcomer. You set it up before anyone sits: 4 Epidemics, the easiest level, and every hand face up, as the rulebook suggests for the Introductory game.

Open hands help a newcomer learn, but they also make quarterbacking easy, because the expert can see every card and work out every turn. So the one-voice rule matters more here, not less. During the teach, after the turn steps (4 actions, draw 2 cards, infect cities), you add one sentence: "We plan together, but whoever's turn it is speaks first and makes the call." The rulebook already says the same thing in its own way: players give advice freely, and the active player decides.

Now the newcomer's turn comes. They look at the board for a long moment. You wait. They say, "I think I should go to Lagos, it has three cubes." Someone else asks, "Is it worth a card to fly there, or can you drive?" They count the lines, decide to drive, and treat a cube.

Was it the best move? Maybe not. Was it theirs? Completely. When Lagos does not outbreak on the next Infect step, the table cheers, and the newcomer knows why.
