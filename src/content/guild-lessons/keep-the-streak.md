One great game night is a good evening. A second one is a pattern. A tenth one is a part of someone's life. This lesson is about getting from the first to the tenth without you having to start over every time.

The newcomer who said yes to a second game has not become a player yet. They have agreed to try again. Habits are what turn that yes into something that lasts.

## Same night, same people

Most game nights do not end because someone stops enjoying them. They end in the group chat, when finding a date takes nine messages and three weeks, and everyone quietly gives up.

So take the date off the table. Pick a fixed night: the second Friday of every month, every other Sunday afternoon, the first Thursday. Now nobody has to decide when. They only have to decide whether, and with a fixed night, the default answer is yes.

Keep the people steady too, at least at first. A newcomer relaxes when they know who will be there. A table that changes every time means meeting strangers every time, which is a cost on top of learning games. Add people slowly, one at a time, the same way you add games.

## One new game a month

A new game is exciting, and it is also work. Every new game means a teach, a first round of not knowing what to do, and the small risk of feeling lost. For a hobby gamer that cost is nothing. For someone who just started, it is real.

A simple rule of thumb: about one new game a month, surrounded by games the group already knows. The familiar games are where people get to feel good at something. Someone who has played a game three times starts to make plans, notice other people's moves, and have opinions. That feeling of competence is a big part of what brings people back.

When you do bring a new game, it should be the next rung on the ladder. One new idea on top of what they know, as the [game ladder worksheet](/academy/guild/toolkit/game-ladder/) lays out.

## Hand over the teach

Here is the step most hosts never take. At some point, the newcomer should teach a game to someone else.

It sounds like a big ask. It is the opposite. Teaching a game they know well tells the newcomer that they are now an insider, someone others learn from. It turns a game you showed them into a game they own. And it spreads your hobby into rooms you will never be in.

Start small. When a new person joins your table, ask the former newcomer to teach the turn while you handle setup. "You explain how a turn works. You're better at it than me now, you remember what was confusing." That last part is true: they learned recently, so they still know what a beginner does not know, which is the very thing hobby gamers forget.

## A worked example: Codenames goes to the office

Picture a friend who learned [Codenames](/academy/codenames/) at your table a few months ago. Since then it has been a regular at your game nights, and she has had a turn as spymaster several times.

One night she mentions her team at work has a long lunch on Fridays. You say, "You should bring Codenames. You could teach it in five minutes." She is unsure, so you hand her the [teach sheet](/academy/codenames/teach-sheet/) and run through the order once with her: two teams, the spymaster's one word and one number, guessing until you miss or stop, and the one word that loses you the game on the spot.

Two weeks later she tells you that her team plays every Friday now, and that someone in accounting has turned out to be a fearsome spymaster.

Look at what happened. Your friend did not just keep playing. She became the host of a table of her own. That is the end goal of the whole program: not one newcomer converted, but one newcomer who converts others.

## The streak is the score

The Second Game is the score for one night. The streak is the score over a year. Protect it the way you protect a newcomer's first game: make showing up easy, keep most nights familiar, add one new thing at a time, and look for the moment to hand someone the box.
