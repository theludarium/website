Party and word games are the friendliest games you own, and they are the easiest to over-teach. The rules fit on a card. The fun is in a feeling: the gasp when a clue lands, the groan when it does not. No rules explanation has ever produced that feeling. One round of play produces it every time.

So for this family of games, flip the usual teach. Talk less, play sooner, and let the table learn by watching.

## Sixty seconds, then play

A party game teach needs three things: what we are trying to do, what one turn looks like, and the one way to lose badly. That is it. Everything else is learned by seeing it happen.

Set a hard limit of sixty seconds of talking. It sounds too short. It is not. Party games are built so that the turn is the rule. Once someone has watched one turn, the rules make sense in a way no description could match. Rare rules, edge cases, and scoring details wait until they come up (Just in time, not just in case).

The sixty seconds also tell the room something. A short teach says "this is easy, and you will be fine." A long teach for a light game says "this is harder than it looks," and the guests who were nervous get more nervous.

## The demo round that does not count

After the sixty seconds, play one round where nothing counts. Say so out loud: "This one is practice. Nobody keeps score."

That sentence does more work than it seems. Party games ask people to perform: give a clue in front of the room, guess out loud, be a little silly. For a newcomer, that is the scariest part of the night, much scarier than any rule. A round that does not count lets them see the performance before they have to give one (Protect dignity).

Two habits make the demo work.

- **Go first yourself.** The first performer sets the bar for the room. If you give a brilliant clue, everyone after you feels measured against it. Give a plain, ordinary answer instead. It shows that ordinary is fine.
- **Think aloud.** Say why you picked your answer and what you were worried about. In a party game, the thinking is the strategy, and hearing it once teaches more than any tip.

Then reset and start the real game. The first real round finishes the teach. Answer questions as they come, and let the table's laughter do the rest.

## Worked example: Codenames with one clue

Picture five newcomers and a copy of [Codenames](/academy/codenames/). You set up the 5 by 5 grid of words before anyone sits down.

**The sixty seconds.** "Two teams. Each team has a spymaster who secretly knows which of these words are ours. The spymaster gives one word and one number, like 'ocean: 2.' We guess our words by touching them. Wrong color or a bystander ends our turn. And one word is the assassin: touch it, and our team loses." Hold up the assassin tile as you say it. That is the whole teach.

**The demo.** Now the move that makes the teach. Put a key card in the stand, then turn the stand so everyone can see it, just for this demo. Point at two words of one color, say SHIP and WAVE. Say: "These two go together. I could say 'ocean: 2.' But wait, is any word near the sea the assassin? If BEACH were the assassin, 'ocean' would be dangerous, so I would pick something else." Then give the clue and let the table guess while they can all see the answers.

In under a minute, everyone has seen the clue, the number, the guessing, and the assassin check. Nobody had to perform. Now hide the key, draw a new one, and start the real game. Taking one spymaster seat yourself for game one helps too: your clues keep teaching by example.

Leave the zero and unlimited clues for another night. They are expert rules, and nobody misses them in a first game.

## When the room wants rules

Some guests ask a lot of questions before the first round. That is usually nerves, not curiosity. A kind answer is "Good question, and it will make more sense once you see a round. Let's play the practice one and come back to it." Then come back to it, every time. A parked question that never gets answered teaches people not to ask.
