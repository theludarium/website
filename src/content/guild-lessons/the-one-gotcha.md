Every game has one rule that bites. Not the hardest rule, and often not even an important-sounding one. It is the rule that punishes a new player for doing what any reasonable person would do. Nobody asks about it, because nobody suspects it is there.

Teach that rule up front. Teach no other exceptions. That is the whole lesson, and it is harder than it sounds, because finding the right one takes a little thought.

## What makes a rule a gotcha

A gotcha has two parts. The newcomer makes a natural guess about how something works, and the game says no, at a cost. You can find it with one question: **if nobody told them, would they feel cheated when it happened?**

Run your rules through that test and most of them fall away. Nobody feels cheated by a scoring table; they just look at it. Nobody feels cheated by a turn order. But a rule that takes points away for something that looked safe, or takes cards away for something that looked smart, will feel like a trap if it arrives unannounced. That is your gotcha.

The test has a useful side effect. It shows you that the gotcha is rarely the rule *you* find tricky. You stopped noticing the real gotcha years ago. That is the curse of knowledge from [You Are the Host, Not the Referee](/academy/guild/host-not-referee/), and it is why your solo run notes help: the place you stumbled is often the place they will.

## A worked example: three games, three gotchas

**[Ticket to Ride](/academy/ticket-to-ride/).** The natural guess: a ticket is a bonus, so a missed one just scores nothing. The rule: at the end, each ticket you kept but did not finish subtracts its value. Say it in the teach while they look at their tickets: "Finish it and you score this number. Miss it and you lose this number." The second time is the first time a newcomer draws more tickets mid-game. A light touch is enough: "Remember, the ones you keep and miss count against you."

**[Azul](/academy/azul/).** The natural guess: taking more tiles is always better. The rule: a pick takes every tile of that color, and whatever does not fit in your chosen line falls to the floor line, which costs points at the end of the round, and the spaces further along cost more. Say it with your finger on the floor line. The second time is the first time a newcomer reaches for five tiles headed to a line that holds two. Ask, do not tell: "Where will the extras go?"

**[Catan](/academy/catan/).** The natural guess: saving up cards for something big is wise. The rule: when anyone rolls a 7, every player holding 8 or more resource cards gives back half, rounded down. Say it plainly in the teach: "Holding 8 or more when a 7 comes up costs you half." The second time is the turn a newcomer is about to pass the dice while holding a fat hand. "You are at nine. Anything you want to build first?"

Notice the shape of each one. A natural guess, a cost, and a moment you can see coming.

## Say it twice, at the right moments

The first warning, in the teach, frames the rule. It will not stick on its own. New players are holding a dozen ideas at once, and a warning about something that has not happened yet is the easiest one to drop.

The second warning is the one that works, because it arrives right before the rule matters. It turns the gotcha from an ambush into a choice: they can still take the five tiles, and now it is their decision. That protects their dignity. Losing points to a rule you knew about feels like part of the game. Losing points to a rule nobody mentioned feels like being set up.

Say the second warning once, and only once. After that, the rule is theirs.

## One, not three

The pull is to add a second gotcha, then a third, just in case. Each one you add weakens the first. A teach that warns about everything warns about nothing, and the newcomer leaves the teach thinking the game is full of traps.

If two rules both pass the cheated test, pick the one that costs more, or the one that will come up sooner. Hold the other back and teach it the first time it happens. The [Gotcha Index](/academy/guild/toolkit/gotcha-index/) gives a starting pick for popular games, so you can compare yours against it before you teach.
