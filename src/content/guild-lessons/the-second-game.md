Most people who teach board games keep score on the wrong thing. They ask themselves whether they covered every rule, whether anyone got a rule wrong, whether the game was played properly. Those are fair questions. They are just not the question that decides whether you get to play more games with the people you love.

This program keeps one score, and it is easy to read. After you teach someone a game, do they want to play a second one?

## The newcomer decides during your teach

Here is the uncomfortable truth about teaching a game to someone who does not play. They are not only learning a game. They are deciding whether games are for them.

A hobby gamer who sits through a clumsy teach shrugs and plays anyway, because they already know the payoff is coming. A newcomer does not know that. All they have is the next twenty minutes. If those minutes feel like a lecture, a test, or a slow way to lose, they will draw a fair conclusion: board games are not their thing. They will be polite about it. They will say it was fun. And the next time you suggest a game night, they will have a reason not to come.

So the teach is not the boring part you get through before the real game starts. For a newcomer, the teach is the game. It is the first impression of the whole hobby.

## A worked example: the perfect teach that failed

Picture a Catan night. You know [Catan](/academy/catan/) well, and you explain it thoroughly. Setup, resources, building costs, the robber, trading, development cards, the ports, the longest road. You answer every question correctly. Your guest nods along for twenty minutes.

Then the game starts, and they spend the first half hour trying to remember what a city costs. They trade badly because nobody told them why trading matters. Someone puts the robber on them twice. They finish with four points while you finish with ten.

Score that teach on accuracy, and it was a success. Every rule was right. Score it on the Second Game, and it failed. Your guest learned the rules, and they also learned that this game makes them feel slow.

Now picture the same night with a different teach. Two minutes on what you are building and how you win. One turn played out with real pieces. One warning about the seven. Then you start, and you explain the robber and development cards the first time each one matters. Your guest makes a good trade in the first ten minutes because you pointed out two options. They lose, but they were in it, and they ask about a rematch.

Same game, same rules, same guest. A different score.

## Why accuracy is the wrong scoreboard

Accuracy still matters. A rule taught wrong can wreck a game, and later lessons cover how to prepare so that you rarely get one wrong. But accuracy is a means. It serves the score; it is not the score.

When you keep score on accuracy, you make predictable mistakes. You front-load every rule so that nobody can say you left one out. You correct people mid-game in front of the table. You read from the rulebook to be safe. Each of those choices protects you from being wrong, and each one makes the newcomer's evening worse.

When you keep score on the Second Game, your choices change. You leave rare rules for later, because nobody remembers them up front anyway. You let small mistakes ride. You pick a shorter game. You care more about one good move in the first ten minutes than about one perfect explanation.

The Guild of Game Guides follows a game night from start to finish. After this mindset module come five moves: **Pick** the right game, **Prep** before anyone arrives, **Teach** in five minutes, **Play** the first game as part of the teach, and **Close** with the next game on the calendar.
