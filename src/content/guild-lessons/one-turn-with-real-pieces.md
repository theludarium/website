Here is a sentence every game teacher has said: "On a player's turn, the active player may perform one of three actions." It is accurate. It is also a sentence nobody can picture. The newcomer has to build a little model of the game in their head from words alone, while you keep adding more words.

There is a faster way. Pick up the pieces and play a turn while you talk. A turn they watch is a turn they remember.

## Show, then say

When you describe a turn, the listener does all the work. They hold "a player," "an action," and "the supply" in their head and try to connect them to a board they have not touched. When you show a turn, the table does that work for them. The tile moves. The card gets spent. The rule is now something they saw.

Three habits make the difference:

- **Say "you," never "a player."** "You take all the red tiles" puts the newcomer in the chair. "A player takes all tiles of one color" puts them in a classroom.
- **Point and move as you speak.** Each sentence goes with one motion. If a sentence has no motion, ask whether it belongs in the turn at all.
- **Eyes on the table.** Set the rulebook down beside you, open to the summary, and leave it there. When everyone is looking at the pieces, nobody is looking at you waiting to be quizzed.

## Pick the boring turn

The temptation is to show a clever turn, the one that shows off what the game can do. Resist it. Show the turn the newcomer will take most often tonight, done the most ordinary way. The clever turns are for them to find, and finding them is half the fun.

Then end the turn out loud: "And that is my turn. Now it is yours." This sounds too obvious to say. It is not. A surprising amount of newcomer discomfort comes from not knowing when they are finished, and sitting there while the table waits. Make the ending as visible as the beginning.

## A worked example: an Azul turn in your hands

[Azul](/academy/azul/) is a good test, because its turn is short and its words are strange. "Factory display," "pattern line," and "wall" mean nothing until someone sees them.

So do not say them first. Reach out to a display with, say, three red tiles and one blue one.

"On your turn, you pick a color from one of these plates. You take every tile of that color. All of them, not some." Scoop the three reds into your hand.

"The rest slide into the middle." Push the blue tile to the center.

"Your tiles go into one of these lines on your board. This one holds three." Set the three reds in your third line. "Three tiles, three spaces. Perfect fit."

"If I had taken more than fit, the extras would fall down here and cost me points." Tap the floor line, and do nothing else with it yet.

"You can also take a color from the middle instead, same rule, all of that color." Point at the center.

"That is my turn. Now it is yours."

Count the new words: you named the board parts only after you touched them, so each word arrived with a picture already attached. If you were not really first to play, put the tiles back where they came from. The newcomer will not mind; they just watched the whole turn work.

## Hand them the pieces

A turn you show beats a turn you describe. A turn they do with their own hands beats both. Once your demo is done, the best next step is often to let the newcomer take a turn of their own while you narrate it with them: "Pick a color. Good. Take all of those. Which line holds that many?"

This is not a quiz. You are not checking whether they listened. You are letting their hands learn the shape of the turn while you are right there to steady it. If they put a tile in the wrong place, say what is legal instead of saying what is wrong: "Those go in one line together. Which one?"

The [Five-Minute Teach Script](/academy/guild/toolkit/teach-script/) has a line for the turn. Write it as a list of motions, not a list of rules, and you will find it nearly teaches itself.
