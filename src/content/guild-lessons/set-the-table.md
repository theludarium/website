Picture the start of most game nights. Guests arrive, someone opens the box, and the teacher starts punching tokens, sorting cards and reading setup steps while everyone else watches. Ten minutes pass. Nobody has learned anything yet, and the newcomer has already decided this is going to take a while.

Setup is not part of the teach. It is the part before it, and it belongs before the doorbell. The [Pre-Flight Checklist](/academy/guild/toolkit/pre-flight-checklist/) is one page you run through with the game in front of you. This lesson covers the six items that change the teach most, and why each one is there.

## The checklist, and what each item buys you

- **Setup done.** The board, the decks, the shared supply, all exactly as they will be on turn one. A finished setup is also a free picture of the game: your guest can see the map, the pieces and the score track before you say a word.
- **Pieces sorted by player.** Each seat gets its own pile in its own color. Nobody hunts for their pieces while you talk, and the newcomer can pick up something that is theirs. Holding your own pieces is a small, real comfort.
- **References visible.** If the game has player aids, put one at every seat. If it does not, print the game's teach sheet and set it in the middle. A reference on the table means a rule forgotten is a glance, not a question asked out loud.
- **Rulebook open to the summary.** Not in your lap, not read from. Open at the page you would check mid-game, sitting near you. It tells the table you can settle a question in seconds.
- **Expansions and variants back in the box.** Every extra module is a rule you will be tempted to teach. Take it off the table and the temptation goes with it.
- **The newcomer seated beside you.** This one is easy to skip, and it changes the whole teach.

## Sit beside them, not across

When you demonstrate a turn from across the table, everything you point at is upside down for your guest. Left is right. The text on cards faces you. They have to flip each move in their head while also learning it.

Sit them on your left or right, both facing the same way, and your demo is their demo. You can point at their pieces without reaching across. You can lean over quietly and say "either of these works" without the whole table hearing. And if they are going after you, you get to show your turn right before they take their first one.

Seat the hobby gamers across from you. They can read upside down. They have done it for years.

## A worked example: Ticket to Ride

Here is a pre-flight for [Ticket to Ride](/academy/ticket-to-ride/), the original USA map, for four players.

The board goes in the middle with every scoring marker on 0. Each seat gets 45 trains of one color, counted, not guessed: an extra train or two changes when the game ends. Deal 4 train cards to each seat and turn 5 face up beside the board. The longest path card sits next to the board where everyone can see it. The [teach sheet](/academy/ticket-to-ride/teach-sheet/) goes on the table.

Then deal the destination tickets before anyone sits, face down at each seat. Current editions deal 4 and you keep at least 2; older boxes deal 3, so check yours. The tickets are what usually slows the start, because choosing which to keep takes a minute and new players want to look at the map while they decide.

But there is a catch, and the Ticket to Ride course's own teach holds the tickets back entirely because of it: if people read their tickets during the teach, they stop listening. Dealing face down gets you the same thing. The tickets stay face down until you call for them. Your teach covers the three turn choices first, and then you say "now flip your tickets." That moment becomes the first real decision of the game, and it feels like an event, not a chore.

## The ten minutes that pay for themselves

A full pre-flight takes about ten minutes for most gateway games. That is ten minutes of your time spent alone, in exchange for ten minutes of your guests' patience, which is worth far more on a newcomer's first night.

It also does something less obvious. When everything is ready, you are calm. You are not counting trains with one hand while answering a question with the other. You can look up, greet people, and start the teach when the room is ready, not when the box is.
