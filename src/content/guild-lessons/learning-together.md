Every lesson so far has assumed you know the game. Some nights you do not. A gift gets unwrapped, a friend brings something new, or a newcomer picks a box off your shelf that you have never opened. Now everyone at the table is learning at once, and nobody is the teacher.

This can go very badly or very well. The difference is almost entirely in the first fifteen minutes.

## The trap: one voice reading the book

The usual move is to hand the rulebook to whoever is most patient and have them read it aloud. It feels fair. It is the worst teach available.

A rulebook is written to be complete and to settle arguments. It is not written to be heard in order. It starts with the components list, then setup, then every rule in a turn, then every exception, then scoring. That is the rules dump from [an earlier lesson](/academy/guild/the-newcomers-ledger/), with nobody steering it and no one at the table able to say what matters most. Twenty minutes later, the reader is tired, the listeners are on their phones, and nobody has touched a piece.

The program's principles still hold even when nobody knows the game. You still want the frame before the facts. You still want rules to arrive just in time. You just need a different way to get there.

## The learn-together steps

Four steps, in this order.

**1. One reader, reading ahead.** Pick one person to own the rulebook. They read in silence while everyone else sets up from the setup page. Their job is to find three things: what the game is about, how you win, and what a turn is. Then they say those three things in their own words, in under two minutes. If you are the most practiced teacher at the table, take this job: it is the five-minute teach, built on the fly.

**2. One short video.** If the table would rather watch than listen, find one how-to-play video under fifteen minutes and play it on the biggest screen you have. Watch It Played makes rules videos for many popular games. One video, once. Do not stop it to debate rules; write questions down and come back to them.

**3. Round one from the summary.** Most games have a player aid or a turn summary on the back of the rulebook. Put it in the middle and play round one from it, hands open, everyone thinking aloud. When a question comes up, the reader looks it up while the table keeps going. The reader stays one section ahead of the table, and rules arrive when they matter.

**4. Offer a restart.** After round one, ask: "Want to start over now that we know it?" Most tables say yes, and the real game is better for it.

## Worked example: a new game on a holiday morning

Picture a holiday morning. Someone unwraps [Cascadia](/academy/cascadia/), a calm game about building a landscape of habitats and wildlife. Your in-laws are curious. Nobody has played it, you included.

You take the rulebook. Everyone else punches the tiles and fills the bag with wildlife tokens. You read only the setup and the turn, and you find the advice to use the A scoring cards for a first game. Then you put the book down and say: "Each of us builds our own landscape. On a turn you take one tile and the animal beside it, and add both to your landscape. These five cards say what shapes score for each animal."

Then you read the five scoring cards aloud, one at a time, pointing at each. That is the whole frame. The first few turns go around with everyone thinking aloud and helping each other place tiles. When someone asks a question you cannot answer, you look it up while they keep playing. Rules like nature tokens get explained the first time someone earns one, and the odd case where the display shows three or four of the same animal gets explained the first time it happens.

Then, a few turns in, you offer the restart. Everyone clears back to their starter tile, and the real game begins with the whole table knowing what a good landscape looks like.

## Why this night is a gift

A game nobody knows is one of the best nights you can offer a newcomer. On a normal night, the gamers know more and the newcomer feels it. When the box is new, everyone starts even. Not knowing the rules is normal. The newcomer's questions are everyone's questions, and their good move counts as much as yours.

So do not apologize for not knowing the game. Say it with a grin: "None of us know this one. Let's figure it out." Learning a game together is its own kind of fun, and a newcomer who helps crack a new box feels like a player from the first turn.
