The principle First win early says one good decision in the first ten minutes makes the game feel like theirs. The tricky word is "theirs." If you tell the newcomer what to do, the move works, and they learn that the game is something you play through them. The win belongs to you.

Training wheels are the middle path between playing for them and leaving them alone. You shrink the choice. They still make it.

## Two good options, never the answer

When a newcomer hesitates, look at the board with them and find two moves that are both decent. Then offer them as a pair: "This or that, both work." Stop talking. Let them pick.

Two is the right number. One option is an instruction. Three or more is the full menu they were already stuck on. Two gives them a real choice with almost no way to go wrong.

Both options must be good. This is the part people miss. If one option is a trap, you have built a quiz, and the newcomer can feel it. When both are fine, whatever they pick is a good move, and it is a good move they made.

And when they pick, do not second-guess it. Not a word, not a look. If you would have chosen the other one, keep that to yourself until the game is over, and probably after that too.

## A worked example: the blue row or the red row

Picture the first round of [Azul](/academy/azul/). The newcomer is staring at the factory displays. Every color looks equally possible, and every line on their board is empty. This is exactly the moment a new player freezes.

Lean over and look with them. One display holds two blue tiles. Another holds three red. "The two blues would fill your second line exactly. The three reds would fill your third line exactly. Either one, nothing falls to the floor. Your call."

Both are good moves. Each fills a line with no waste, which is what strong players do in round one, and neither sends a tile to the floor. Whichever they pick, they have made a sound opening move, and they chose it.

Now wait for the payoff. At the end of the round, when finished lines move to the wall, that full line slides a tile across and scores. That is the moment: "Look, your line was full, so it moved over and scored. Good pick." If a second tile later lands next to it and scores more for touching, say that too.

The newcomer learns three things from this: what a filled line does, that waste costs points, and that they are capable of a good move. The last one matters most.

## Name the move when it pays off

Praise right as someone moves is vague, because nothing has happened yet. Praise when the move pays off is specific: it points at a result on the board and connects it to the choice that caused it.

Keep it about the move, not the person. "Good pick" or "That paid off" works. "See, you are a natural" can feel like you are managing them, especially for adults. Say it once, plainly, and move on.

## Take the wheels off on time

Training wheels are for the first ten minutes or so. After the newcomer's first good move pays off, stop offering options unless they ask. Then, if they do ask, ask back: "What are you thinking about?" Most of the time, they already have two options in mind. Your job is now to hear them, not to supply them.

Watch for one risk: offering options to the newcomer while other players get none can feel like special treatment. Make it visible and normal. In an open first round, offer two options to anyone who asks, gamers included. Then the help is part of the table, not a spotlight on one person.
