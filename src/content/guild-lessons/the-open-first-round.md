The teach does not end when you say "Let's start." It ends when everyone can play without you. Between those two moments sits the first round, and that round can either be a test or a rehearsal.

Make it a rehearsal. Call it what it is: "Let's play the first round open, so everyone can see how it works." This one move does more for the Second Game than almost anything else in the program.

## What open means

An open round changes three things.

**Hidden things are shown.** In card games, hands go face up. In games with secret goals, everyone may show theirs. The newcomer gets to see what a good hand looks like, and you get to see where they are stuck before they say so.

**Thinking happens out loud.** Everyone, you included, says what they are weighing before they move. "I could take these, or wait for the blue ones." This is not coaching. It is a window into how a decision gets made.

**"What should I do?" is a good question.** Say so before the round starts. In a normal round, that question sounds like an admission. In an open round, it is the point.

Some games cannot go fully open. A bluffing game shown face up stops being a bluffing game, and in some games secret information is the entire puzzle. Then open whatever you can: talk through your own choices, and tell the newcomer they can show you their hand privately and ask.

## Go first, and think out loud

If you can, take the first turn yourself, or sit so you play just before the newcomer. Your turn becomes the last piece of the teach. Talk through it plainly: two options, the reason you like one, then the move.

Do not narrate a brilliant move. Narrate an ordinary one, the kind the newcomer could make too. The goal is not to show how good you are. It is to show that thinking aloud is normal here, so when it is their turn, they do it too, and you can hear where they are.

## A worked example: Codenames with everyone talking

[Codenames](/academy/codenames/) has a built-in problem: the spymaster cannot talk during the turn, so the newest spymaster freezes, and the operatives guess in silence. An open practice round fixes both.

Before the first real game, lay out the grid and announce that the first clue is for practice. The spymaster gives a clue, say "ocean: 2," and the operatives talk through every word they are considering, out loud. "Wave, for sure. Ship? Or is fish closer?" Everyone hears how a team reasons together.

Then do the one thing a real game never allows: when the guessing ends, the spymaster explains the clue. "I meant wave and ship. I was scared of fish because it could be theirs." Now the whole table understands what a clue is for, and what makes one risky. The newcomer who will be spymaster next has a model in their head before they hold the key.

Then flip the grid to a fresh side, or deal a new one, and start the game for real.

## Offer the fresh start

When the open round ends, say: "That was the practice round. Want to start fresh, now that everyone has the idea?"

Offer it to the whole table, not the newcomer. "Do you want to start over?" aimed at one person tells them they did badly. The same question to everyone tells them this is how learning works.

Timing matters. Offer it right after the open round, before anyone can tell who is winning. A restart that comes after someone has fallen behind is a rescue, and everyone knows it. A restart that comes after round one is just the next part of the plan.

If the table wants to keep going, keep going. Either answer is fine. What matters is that the choice was offered, and that nobody had to ask for it. Learning a game should never cost anyone their dignity, and an open round, ended with an offer to start fresh, is how you make sure it does not.
