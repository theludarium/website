The game ends, the scores are counted, and somebody reaches for the box. This is the most valuable minute of the night, and most hosts spend it packing up.

Before the pieces go away, you have a window of about a minute where the newcomer is still at the table, still a little warm from the last turn, and willing to talk. What you ask in that minute shapes how they remember the night and tells you what to bring next time.

## Ask what, not whether

The natural question is "Did you like it?" It is also the least useful one. A polite guest says yes to a yes or no question, whether they mean it or not. You learn nothing, and you have made them perform enjoyment on the spot.

Ask two other questions instead.

**"What was your favorite moment?"** This assumes there was one, which there almost always was. It asks for a specific memory, not a verdict. And the act of naming a good moment out loud helps make it the thing they remember. A night that ended in a narrow loss becomes the night they finished that big city.

**"What would you try next time?"** This is the quiet magic question. To answer it, the newcomer has to picture a next time. They start planning a second game before you have asked for one. Listen to the answer: it tells you whether they want to get better at this game, or try something else.

Then stop. Two questions is a debrief. Five is an interview.

## Listen for the kind of fun

The answer to the favorite moment question is a clue about the next game, and it is easy to miss if you are only listening for praise.

- **"When I blocked you."** They like interaction. Bring a game where players affect each other.
- **"When it all fit together."** They like puzzles. Bring a game with a satisfying spatial or pattern challenge.
- **"When we were laughing about that clue."** They came for the people. Bring a party or team game.
- **"When I figured out what to do."** They like the click of a plan working. Bring a game with one clear decision each turn.

Notice what none of these is about: the theme, the art, or winning. People often name the feeling, not the game. Write it down after they leave, because it is the most useful thing you learned all night.

## A worked example: the in-law who liked the puzzle part

Picture your partner's father after his first game of [Carcassonne](/academy/carcassonne/). He lost, but not badly. You ask for his favorite moment. He thinks, then says it was when he found the one spot where his tile fit and closed a city. He liked the fitting, not the fighting over meeples.

You ask what he would try next time. "Finish more things, I guess. I had too many of those little guys stuck out there." That tells you he understood the core of the game and saw what to fix. Good sign.

Now you know what to bring next. Not a bigger version of Carcassonne, and not your favorite game. You bring [Azul](/academy/azul/), because Azul is almost all puzzle: every turn is picking tiles and fitting them into rows on your own board, and tiles placed next to each other score more. It gives him more of the exact thing he named, with less of the part he did not mention.

You do not have to say any of that to him. You just say, "Next time, I have a tile game I think you'll like even more."

## The thank-you

End with thanks, and be careful which kind.

"Thanks for being a good sport" sounds kind, but it says there was something to be a good sport about: the loss, the long teach, your strong play. "Thanks for putting up with me" does the same. Both quietly frame the game as a favor they did you.

Thank them the way you would thank any player: "Thanks for playing. That was fun." It treats them as one of the table, which is the whole goal. They were not a guest at your hobby tonight. They were a player.
