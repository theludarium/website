Picture learning a sport by having someone explain the rules for ten minutes before they tell you what counts as a goal. Every rule floats. You hear "you can't pick it up" and wonder why it matters, because you do not know what you are trying to do with it.

That is how most teaches feel to a newcomer. The win comes at the end, after the turn, the exceptions and the edge cases. By then your guest has been holding a pile of rules with no reason attached to any of them.

So say the win second, right after the [theme sentence](/academy/guild/theme-in-one-sentence/), and before any rule about what you do on a turn.

## The win sentence

It has two halves, said in one breath:

> The game ends when X. The winner has the most Y.

That is it. "The game ends when the last tile goes down. Most points wins." "The game ends after four rounds. Most points wins." "The game ends when one team finds all their words, and that team wins."

The first half matters as much as the second. Knowing when it ends tells your guest how long they have and what to watch for. A newcomer who does not know when the game ends cannot tell if they are early, late or doomed, and that uncertainty feels like being lost.

Then, right after, the ways to score, **biggest first**. Not every way. The two or three that decide most games. The biggest first tells your guest where to look on the board, and that is half of playing well.

## A worked example: Ticket to Ride

Here is a win sentence for [Ticket to Ride](/academy/ticket-to-ride/):

"The game ends when someone gets down to two trains or fewer, and then everyone gets one last turn. Most points wins."

Then the ways to score, biggest first:

1. **Routes.** "You score points every time you claim a route, and long routes pay much more than short ones." A six-train route is worth 15; a one-train route is worth 1.
2. **Tickets.** "These secret tickets name two cities. Connect them with your own trains by the end and you score the number on the ticket. Miss, and you lose that number."
3. **The longest railroad.** "Whoever has the longest unbroken line of trains at the end gets 10 more."

Three ways, about thirty seconds. And now listen to how the turn sounds afterward. "On your turn you can draw train cards." Why would I want train cards? To claim routes, which score. "Or you can claim a route." Which route? The ones that connect my tickets. "Or draw more tickets." Why would I risk that? Because they score, and I know they can cost me.

Every rule now answers a question the win sentence raised. Your guest is not memorizing; they are filling in a plan.

The ticket penalty is worth naming here, right in the win, rather than saving it. It is the part that surprises people at the end, and it changes how they play from the first turn. A newcomer who learns at the final count that unfinished tickets cost points feels tricked. One who heard it up front feels warned. That warning is also a preview of [the one gotcha](/academy/guild/toolkit/gotcha-index/), which Module 3 comes back to.

## Why the end comes before the middle

This is the principle frame before facts, applied a second time. The theme told your guest who they are. The win tells them what they want. Now they are ready for the rules, because each rule is a tool for getting what they want.

There is a quieter benefit too. A newcomer who knows the scoring can tell when they did something good. That is how the first win early happens: they claim a long route, see the score jump, and know it was a smart move. Without the win sentence, a good move looks the same as any other.

## Keep it short, even when the game is not

Some games score many ways. [Wingspan](/academy/wingspan/) scores birds, bonus cards, goals, eggs, stored food and tucked cards. You do not need all six in the win sentence. "Most points after four rounds. Most of your points come from the birds you play" is enough to start. The rest arrives as it happens, which is the next principle in action: just in time, not just in case.

If a game has a sudden-death ending, like the assassin in [Codenames](/academy/codenames/), it goes in the win sentence. Anything that can end the game early is part of how the game ends.
