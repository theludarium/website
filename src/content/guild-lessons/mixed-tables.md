A mixed table looks like the easy case. You have a newcomer, but you also have two or three people who already know the game. Surely more help is better.

It often is not. Experienced players, out of kindness and enthusiasm, are the most common way a good teach goes wrong. They add rules you were holding back. They give tips before the first turn. They correct each other mid-sentence. And every addition, however helpful in itself, piles one more thing on the newcomer's plate.

The gamers are not the problem. They just need a role. This lesson gives them one.

## One voice teaches

The core rule: during the teach, exactly one person explains the game. That is you, or whoever you hand it to. Everyone else listens.

Why so strict? Because a newcomer cannot tell which voice to trust. When two people explain the same rule in two ways, the newcomer hears a disagreement, and starts to suspect the game is more confusing than it is. And when a gamer jumps in with a rule you were deliberately saving for later, your whole plan for the teach, everything held back until it matters, falls apart.

Say it kindly, and say it before the teach starts: "I'll do the teach, and if I forget something that matters, I'll ask you." That last part matters. It gives the gamers a job, not a gag.

## Strategy waits until the end

The second rule: no strategy talk until the game is over. No "the trick is," no "you'll want to," no "most people make the mistake of."

Strategy tips feel generous. But a tip before the first turn is one more thing to remember, and it quietly tells the newcomer there is a right way to play that they do not know yet. That is the start of feeling behind.

After the game, though, strategy talk is great. The newcomer has played, has questions of their own, and can hear advice as something to try next time instead of something they got wrong. Tell your gamers: "Save the good stuff for the debrief. They'll want it then."

## Seat for help, not for rivalry

Where people sit decides who helps whom. Put each newcomer beside a gamer who will answer quiet questions and point at the reference card. Two cautions.

First, pick the patient gamers, not the strongest ones. The best helper is the one who enjoys watching someone else get it.

Second, in games where neighbors compete for the same space, do not put the newcomer beside the most cutthroat player. A helper who is also the one blocking every move is a confusing mix.

Brief the helper too: help with the rules and the reference, not the decisions. "You can't take both of those" is help. "Take that one" is playing for them.

## A worked example: the double-route argument

Picture a four-player game of [Ticket to Ride](/academy/ticket-to-ride/): you, two regulars, and a newcomer. Halfway through, one regular reaches for the second of two parallel routes between the same cities. The other regular objects. "You can't take that, the other one's taken." "That's only with two or three players." "No, it's always." A third rule gets dragged in. The newcomer watches, holding their cards, quietly deciding this game has hidden traps.

The rule is clear, as it happens. One player can never claim both routes of a double pair. With 4 or 5 players, both routes can be used, by different players. With 2 or 3, once one is taken, the other closes. The regular is right.

But the right answer is not the point. The argument is. The fix is a quick ruling: "Four players, so both routes are open, just not to the same person. Go ahead." Then move on. If anyone still disagrees, "Let's check the book after the game" ends it without a winner.

Afterward, at a quiet moment, thank the regulars and remind them why it matters: the newcomer was watching. Better still, settle tricky rules like this in your pre-game message, before anyone sits down.
