Rules mistakes happen in every first game. Someone moves a piece where it cannot go. You forget a step. A gamer at the table spots it three turns later and says so, loudly. None of this is a disaster. How you handle it decides whether it becomes one.

This lesson is about one priority: protect the person first and the rules second. The rules can be fixed in a sentence. A newcomer who feels caught out in front of the table may not come back.

## Let it ride

The default for any past mistake is simple: if play has moved on, let it ride. Do not rewind. Do not recalculate. Say what the rule is, apply it from now on, and keep playing.

Rewinding sounds fair, but it rarely is. It takes back moves other players made in good faith. It eats time. And it turns one person's slip into a short trial with the whole table watching. The game result matters much less than the evening, and the evening goes better without the trial.

The phrase that does the work: "Tonight we are playing it this way." It does not say anyone was wrong. It says the table has agreed on a rule, which is all a rule ever is.

## A worked example: the robber, two turns ago

Picture a first game of [Catan](/academy/catan/). Two turns ago, the newcomer rolled a 7, picked up the robber, and set it right back down on the hex where it already sat, on the desert. It did nothing, and they stole nothing. Nobody noticed. Now a gamer at the table says, "Wait, the robber has to move to a different hex."

They are right. When the robber moves, it must go to a different hex than the one it is on. But look at what fixing it now would take: deciding where it should have gone, who would have been robbed, which card would have been drawn, and how the two turns since would have changed. There is no clean version of that.

So you say: "Good catch. From now on the robber always moves to a new hex. Let's keep going." Then turn to the newcomer, not the gamer: "Easy one to miss. The rule is that it always has to go somewhere new." You have fixed the future, kept the past, and put nobody on trial.

## Your own mistakes: fast and small

When the error is yours, say so the moment you notice. One sentence, no long story: "I taught that wrong. Once you have rolled, you can trade and build in any order, so you can trade again after you build. My mistake." Then keep playing.

Two things make this work. First, speed: the sooner you correct it, the less it costs. Second, size: a short correction keeps the mistake small. A long explanation of why the rule was confusing makes the mistake bigger, and it makes the evening about you.

There is a bonus. When the teacher admits an error easily, the newcomer learns that mistakes are ordinary here. That is worth more than any rule.

If the error cost someone something, offer a fair fix that looks forward, like letting them do a trade they would have made. Do not try to rebuild the past.

## Never make a newcomer take back a move

If a newcomer makes an illegal move and play has not moved on, it is tempting to say "you can't do that" and hand back the pieces. Resist the second half. A taken-back move, in front of everyone, is the public correction the newcomer fears most.

Instead, help them finish the move legally, side by side, as if it were the next step: "That one has to go here instead. Want to put it there, or pick something else?" Same fix, no rewind, no spotlight.

## When you must fix it

Let it ride covers the past. It does not cover two cases:

- **A mistake that will keep happening.** If someone is collecting resources wrong every turn, fix the rule now, kindly, before it compounds.
- **A mistake that decides the game.** If a wrong rule hands someone the win, it is fair to sort it out. Talk to the table, not to the person: "We got this one wrong. How should we handle it?"

Decide your policy before the game, not during the argument. A one-line note on your teach script is enough.
